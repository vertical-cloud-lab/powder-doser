## Literature Review: Tapped Density in Small Cylinders and Flow Through Small Orifices

### Part (a): Tapped Density in Small (10–25 mL) Cylinders

#### Standard Methods: USP <616> versus ASTM B527/ISO 3953

The two principal standards differ materially in apparatus geometry and tapping parameters. ASTM B527 (identical to ISO 3953) specifies a 100 mL graduated glass cylinder with 0.2 mL graduations, a tapping amplitude of 3 mm, and a frequency of 100–300 impulses per minute; tapping continues until the powder volume no longer decreases (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 29-31). USP <616> historically uses a 250 mL cylinder and includes both a 14 mm drop apparatus and an alternative 3 mm drop apparatus; the method used must be reported with results (index2011258〈616〉bulka pages 1-2, index2011258〈616〉bulk pages 1-2). USP <616> prescribes reading volumes after sequences of taps (commonly 10, 500, and 1250 cumulative taps), with a criterion that successive measurements must differ by less than 2% before the final tapped density is accepted (index2011258〈616〉bulka pages 1-2). ASTM B527 also permits a smaller cylinder for high-density powders but does not prescribe a fixed number of taps as an endpoint (venkatesh2024characterizationmethodologiesfor pages 9-10).

#### Wall Effects versus Cylinder Diameter-to-Particle-Size Ratio

Wall effects arise because particles near a container wall pack less efficiently than those in the bulk interior, creating a region of reduced packing density extending approximately 4–5 particle diameters inward from the wall. For a 10 mL cylinder (typical internal diameter ~15–17 mm), the ratio of cylinder diameter to particle diameter becomes critically important. Haferkamp et al. demonstrated this directly for monomodal 316L powders spread into an 84.5 µm deep cavity (40 mm diameter): powder layer density increased linearly with decreasing D50/cavity-depth ratio for free-flowing powders (D50 from ~60 µm down to ~30 µm), but fine powders with D50 < 20 µm showed increased standard deviation due to competing wall and cohesion effects (haferkamp2021effectofparticle pages 2-5). For the proposed 10 mL cylinder with ~15 mm bore, wall effects become non-negligible when the particle diameter exceeds roughly 300–500 µm (D/d < 30–50), which is unlikely for most AM powders (D50 typically 15–60 µm) but relevant for coarse salts or food powders.

#### Fill Method Effects

The initial bulk density depends strongly on the filling method. Saker et al. tested multiple filling protocols (gentle, rapid, with or without funnel, different operators) and found that the aerated state obtained after pouring was surprisingly reproducible for a given protocol, contradicting suggestions that HR values are strongly affected by irreproducible initial pouring (saker2019powdersflowabilityassessment pages 7-8). However, the filling method (funnel, sieve, auger) determines the initial packing state and therefore the bulk density component of the Hausner ratio. When using an auger to fill a 10 mL cylinder, the initial density will differ from a free-pour or funnel fill. This systematic offset must be characterized and reported.

#### Drop Height: 3 mm versus 14 mm

The tapping amplitude has a profound and non-trivial effect on the measured Hausner ratio. Saker et al. systematically compared 1 mm and 3 mm tapping amplitudes at 1 Hz frequency over 500 taps for nine pharmaceutical and food powders (saker2019powdersflowabilityassessment pages 6-7, saker2019powdersflowabilityassessment pages 7-8). Changing from 1 mm to 3 mm not only changed the absolute HR values but also changed the rank ordering of powders. For example, Avicel PH105 changed from HR = 1.29 ± 0.02 (1 mm) to 1.42 ± 0.01 (3 mm), while Tablettose 70 changed from HR = 1.25 ± 0.01 (1 mm) to 1.24 ± 0.03 (3 mm). The flow classification shifted between "fair" and "poor" for several materials. The proposed 3 mm cam drop is consistent with ASTM B527/ISO 3953 practice but not with the USP 14 mm apparatus, and results from different drop heights should not be pooled without cross-calibration (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 29-31, saker2019powdersflowabilityassessment pages 2-4).

#### Lateral versus Vertical Tapping

While the most common compendial methods employ vertical tapping (free-fall drop), Saker et al. also investigated sinusoidal (electromagnetic shaker) vibration at 30 Hz with amplitude scanning from 10 to 110 m/s², finding that the stationary packing state was strongly dependent on vibration amplitude but only slightly dependent on frequency (saker2019powdersflowabilityassessment pages 2-4, saker2019powdersflowabilityassessment pages 6-7). The key insight is that equal tap counts do not imply equal compaction energy: free-fall impacts, sinusoidal vibration, and lateral shaking produce fundamentally different acceleration histories. No direct literature comparison of lateral versus vertical tapping in small cylinders was found, but the physics suggests that lateral tapping preferentially drives shear-induced rearrangement while vertical tapping drives gravitational compaction, potentially yielding different final packing fractions.

#### Number of Taps to Plateau

USP <616> uses a 500/1250-tap protocol with a 2% difference criterion (index2011258〈616〉bulka pages 1-2). ASTM B527 simply requires tapping until volume no longer decreases (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 29-31). Saker et al. showed that while compaction curves appear to reach a plateau on a linear tap-count scale at ~200–500 taps, the same data plotted on a logarithmic scale reveal that steady state is not always established at 500 taps for some powders (e.g., Avicel PH102, Retalac, Food D) (saker2019powdersflowabilityassessment pages 7-8). The rate-process theory of Hao predicts that packing fraction approaches its asymptote logarithmically, meaning that a truly stationary state may require thousands of taps for cohesive materials (saker2019powdersflowabilityassessment pages 2-4). For the proposed system, recording the complete height-versus-tap curve via camera and defining a camera-based endpoint (e.g., <0.5% volume change over the last 200 taps) is recommended.

#### Repeatability of the Hausner Ratio

Schlick-Hasper et al. measured Hausner ratios of 12 powdery or granular dangerous-goods substances and found a maximum coefficient of variation (CV) of 2.8%, substantially better than angle-of-repose measurements, which showed CVs exceeding 20% for some cohesive materials (schlick‐hasper2022flowpropertiesof pages 13-14, schlick‐hasper2022flowpropertiesof pages 14-14, schlick‐hasper2022flowpropertiesof pages 15-15). Saker et al. found that three replicate measurements were sufficient for acceptable accuracy, but systematic shifts from changing tapping amplitude exceeded the repeatability error—meaning that apparatus-to-apparatus reproducibility is dominated by tapping protocol rather than random measurement noise (saker2019powdersflowabilityassessment pages 7-8).

#### Kawakita and Other Compaction-Curve Fits

The Kawakita equation applied to tapping takes the form N/C = N/a + 1/(ab), where N is the number of taps and C = (V₀ − V)/V₀ is the fractional volume reduction (nordstrom2009aparticlerearrangement pages 2-4). The parameter *a* represents the limiting fractional volume reduction at infinite taps, while 1/b characterizes the rate of compaction. This linearized form yields excellent fits to pharmaceutical powder tapping data (R² > 0.9975) (nordstrom2009aparticlerearrangement pages 2-4). Nordström et al. showed that *a* can be directly converted to a Kawakita-derived Hausner ratio via HR_K = 1/(1 − a), establishing a direct link between the Kawakita model and the conventional Hausner ratio (nordstrom2009aparticlerearrangement pages 2-4, nordstrom2009aparticlerearrangement pages 4-5). For binary mixtures, Frenning et al. demonstrated that effective Kawakita parameters can be predicted from component properties (frenning2009effectivekawakitaparameters pages 1-2). For the proposed 10 mL test, fitting the full compaction curve to the Kawakita equation provides both the equilibrium packing state and the compaction kinetics, offering more information than the single-point Hausner ratio.

### Part (b): Flow Through Small Orifices

#### Beverloo Equation Constants C and k

The classical Beverloo correlation for mass flow rate through a circular orifice is ṁ = Cρ_b g^0.5 (D − kd)^2.5, where D is the orifice diameter, d is the mean particle size, ρ_b is bulk density, and g is gravitational acceleration (baserinia2018massflowrate pages 1-6, baserinia2018massflowrate pages 6-10). The original Beverloo constant C was found to be close to 0.58, with a general range of 0.55–0.65 (baserinia2018massflowrate pages 1-6, just2023gravitationalmassflow pages 2-3, kachrimanis2005flowrateof pages 3-5). The parameter k accounts for the "empty annulus" at the orifice edge where no particle centers can approach; Beverloo's original work with organic materials found k ranging from 1 to 3 (just2023gravitationalmassflow pages 2-3). For spherical particles, k ≈ 1.5 is commonly used (baserinia2018massflowrate pages 18-22). The zero-flow intercept (the minimum orifice diameter at which flow ceases) was found to range from approximately 1.2 to 2.2 particle diameters, with circular openings generally requiring diameters two to three times larger than this intercept to sustain flow (ring1971studiesinthe pages 86-92, ring1971studiesinthe pages 92-97).

For fine powders with average particle size below approximately 400 µm, the classical Beverloo equation significantly overpredicts the mass flow rate because it neglects interstitial air pressure effects (baserinia2018massflowrate pages 6-10). Verghese proposed a correction: ṁ = ṁ_B(1 − λ/(ρ_b g d²))^0.5, where λ is a function of compressibility and gas properties; for a series of sands, λ/(ρ_b g) ≈ 1.48 × 10⁻⁸ m² (baserinia2018massflowrate pages 6-10). DEM work has shown that C is not truly a constant but depends on the coefficient of interparticle friction (baserinia2018massflowrate pages 6-10). For cohesive materials, Johanson proposed ṁ = ṁ_B√(1 − ff/ff_c), where ff is the flow factor and ff_c is the critical flow factor at which arching occurs (baserinia2018massflowrate pages 6-10).

Kachrimanis et al. studied pharmaceutical diluent flow through miniature die orifices (2–4 mm diameter, 5–15 mm length) and confirmed C in the range 0.55–0.65, with k < 1 as a function of particle shape in the pharmaceutical-orifice convention (kachrimanis2005flowrateof pages 1-3, kachrimanis2005flowrateof pages 3-5). The <90 µm lactose fraction did not flow through any of their orifices (kachrimanis2005flowrateof pages 1-3). Just et al. studied hard-metal (WC-Co) granules through orifices of 1–8 mm and found that the mass flow rate peaked at an intermediate Bond number before declining as interparticle forces became dominant; for these well-flowing granules (HR ≈ 1.08), the fitted Beverloo parameters fell within the standard ranges (just2023gravitationalmassflow pages 2-3, just2023gravitationalmassflow pages 3-4, just2023gravitationalmassflow pages 4-5).

#### Flodex Minimum Orifice Diameter Test (USP <1174>)

The Flodex test measures the minimum orifice diameter (d_min or B_min) through which a powder falls freely under gravity without forming a stable arch. A Flodex value of ≤24 mm is generally considered acceptable for pharmaceutical applications (mahmoud2024measuringpowderflow pages 48-52). The test operates at very low consolidation stresses, typically below 200 Pa (mahmoud2024measuringpowderflow pages 48-52). Tharanon et al. measured Flodex d_min for numerous pharmaceutical powders and found values ranging from <4 mm for highly free-flowing materials (Fast-Flo®, Combilac®) to 34 mm for lactose monohydrate; Avicel PH102 and similar MCC grades showed d_min of approximately 18 mm (tharanon2024asystematiccomparison pages 20-23). They found medium-to-good correlations between d_min and both angle of repose (R² = 0.778) and Hausner ratio (R² = 0.798) (tharanon2024asystematiccomparison pages 20-23).

#### Relationship to Unconfined Yield Strength via Jenike's Arching Criterion

Jenike's arching criterion provides the theoretical link between minimum orifice diameter and powder strength. At the point of arch failure, the abutment stress from the arch's self-weight must exceed the powder's unconfined yield strength (σ_c). The critical outlet diameter is determined by comparing the flow function (σ_c versus major principal stress σ₁) with the hopper's flow factor (cannavacciuolo2009archfreeflowin pages 1-2, stanley‐wood2009bulksolidshandling pages 45-48). Higher unconfined yield strength requires a larger minimum outlet diameter to prevent stable arch formation (cannavacciuolo2009archfreeflowin pages 1-2). For mechanical interlocking (as opposed to cohesive arching), the minimum outlet should be at least 6–8 times the characteristic particle dimension for circular openings (stanley‐wood2009bulksolidshandling pages 83-87). The Flodex test can be interpreted within Jenike's framework by treating the flat-bottomed Flodex apparatus as a hopper and using B_min as the critical outlet opening, allowing conversion to intrinsic flow properties at the relevant low-stress conditions (mahmoud2024measuringpowderflow pages 48-52).

#### Effect of Vibration or Tapping on the Critical Orifice

Vibration promotes powder flow by overcoming static friction, adhesion, and bridging. In the vibrating-capillary method, non-free-flowing powders begin to discharge once a critical vibration acceleration is exceeded (stavrou2020assessingpowderflowability pages 56-59, ogata2019areviewrecent pages 6-8). Yang and Evans reported that for a vibrating capillary valve, the orifice radius should be approximately 2–5 times the particle radius for effective operation (yang2007meteringanddispensing pages 7-9). Vibration can thus allow flow through a smaller orifice than would be possible under static conditions by disrupting dome or arch formation. However, extremely cohesive or fibrous powders may continue to bridge even under vibration (stavrou2020assessingpowderflowability pages 56-59), and in some cases vibration can promote mechanical interlocking and stabilize arch-like structures rather than destroy them (stavrou2020assessingpowderflowability pages 40-42). For the proposed system with solenoid tapping, the transition from no-flow to flow with tapping directly characterizes the cohesive strength at the orifice scale.

#### Air Counter-Flow Effects for Particles Below ~100 µm

As particles discharge through an orifice, air must flow inward to replace the departing solids, creating an adverse (upward) interstitial pressure gradient that opposes particle motion. This effect becomes increasingly severe as particle size decreases because bed permeability decreases with the square of particle diameter (aldin1984theflowof pages 6-7, stanley‐wood2009bulksolidshandling pages 90-94). For fine powders, this counter-flow can reduce discharge to 20–60% of the gravity-only baseline, and at extreme conditions, can completely stop discharge by stabilizing arches (kazhikenova2026gasassistedcontrolof pages 11-13). Al-Din and Gunn showed that the magnitude of the pressure gradient at the orifice vicinity increased with decreasing particle size (aldin1984theflowof pages 6-7). For the proposed 3 mm orifice outlet, air counter-flow effects will be significant for powders with particles below approximately 100 µm, particularly cohesive powders like fumed silica. Baserinia and Sinka developed a modified Beverloo model for fine pharmaceutical powders (MCC PH101, PH102, PH200, PH302) that incorporates differential air pressure and reduces to the classical Beverloo equation when pressure differences are absent (baserinia2018massflowrate pages 1-6, baserinia2018massflowrate pages 6-10).

#### Comparison with Hall and Carney Funnels (ASTM B213, B964)

The Hall flowmeter funnel (ASTM B213) has a 2.5 mm orifice with a 60° cone angle and measures the time for 50 g of powder to discharge gravitationally. The Carney funnel (ASTM B964) has a larger 5 mm orifice and is intended for powders that do not flow freely through the Hall funnel (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 29-31, lorenzo2021metallicpowderflowability pages 2-5). For gas-atomized 316L stainless steel, typical Hall flow times of 13.1–14.3 s/50g have been reported (lewis2022aspectsofthe pages 16-18). For Al-10Si-0.5Mg, one grade showed 34.9 s while two others did not flow at all (venkatesh2024characterizationmethodologiesfor pages 13-13). Ti6Al4V powder had a reported flow time of approximately 37 s (venkatesh2024characterizationmethodologiesfor pages 13-13). Powders that fail to flow through the Hall funnel receive a binary "no flow" classification and cannot be further distinguished from one another, even though their flowability may differ substantially under other conditions (marchetti2021flowabilityofsteel pages 2-2). The Carney funnel partially addresses this by accommodating less flowable powders, but it still provides only a single flow-time value at a fixed orifice size (venkatesh2024characterizationmethodologiesfor pages 5-6, venkatesh2024characterizationmethodologiesfor pages 6-6).

The proposed small-orifice test with interchangeable 1–6 mm orifice discs offers a significant advantage over the fixed-orifice Hall/Carney approach: by measuring mass flow rate across a range of orifice diameters, it generates a continuous Beverloo curve and identifies the critical (no-flow) orifice diameter, providing richer characterization than a single pass/fail threshold. This approach is conceptually similar to the Flodex test but with the addition of quantitative mass flow rate data and the option to apply tapping to distinguish between static and dynamic arching thresholds.

### Summary and Practical Implications

For the proposed 10 mL tapped density test, the key literature findings are: (1) wall effects in a ~15 mm bore cylinder are negligible for AM-relevant particle sizes (D50 < 100 µm) but should be verified against a standard 100 mL cylinder for calibration; (2) the 3 mm drop height aligns with ASTM B527/ISO 3953 but not with USP <616>'s 14 mm apparatus, and the difference materially changes both HR values and powder rankings; (3) 500 taps may not reach steady state for cohesive powders—recording the full compaction curve and fitting the Kawakita equation is recommended; and (4) HR repeatability of CV ≤ 2.8% is achievable with controlled protocols.

For the small-orifice test, the key findings are: (1) the Beverloo equation with C ≈ 0.55–0.65 and k ≈ 1–3 provides a good starting framework, but requires modification for fine and cohesive powders where air counter-flow effects become significant below ~400 µm and especially below ~100 µm; (2) the minimum flowing orifice diameter correlates with both Hausner ratio (R² ≈ 0.80) and unconfined yield strength through Jenike's arching criterion; (3) tapping can initiate flow through orifices that would otherwise arch, providing a direct measure of cohesive versus mechanical arching thresholds; and (4) the multi-orifice approach provides substantially more information than the binary pass/fail Hall funnel test, which cannot discriminate among non-flowing powders.
