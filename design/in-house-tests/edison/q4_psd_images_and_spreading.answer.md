# Part (a): Particle Size and Shape from 2D Backlit Images (Static Image Analysis)

## Systematic Differences Between SIA, Laser Diffraction, and Sieving

Static image analysis (SIA, ISO 13322-1) and laser diffraction (LD, ISO 13320) measure fundamentally different equivalent diameters, leading to systematic differences in d10, d50, and d90 that depend on particle shape, the image-analysis diameter definition, and the weighting basis. SIA typically determines an area-equivalent circular diameter (ECAD) or a Feret diameter from 2D projections, initially on a number basis, whereas LD infers a volume-weighted equivalent-sphere diameter from scattering patterns (tinkeUnknownyearparticleshapeand pages 1-5, hogg2008issuesinparticle pages 4-5).

For non-spherical pharmaceutical particles, Tinke et al. reported that SIA-ECAD gave substantially higher d10 and d50 than LD but similar or lower d90, thereby narrowing the apparent PSD. For example, in Compound B SIA-ECAD yielded d10/d50/d90 of 9.7/17.0/27.7 µm versus 5.7/14.1/36.4 µm for LD—d10 was 70% higher, d50 21% higher, but d90 was 24% lower (tinkeUnknownyearparticleshapeand pages 26-44, tinkeUnknownyearparticleshapeanda pages 26-44). Similar trends held for Compounds C and D, with SIA d10 29–73% higher and d50 34–39% higher than LD (tinkeUnknownyearparticleshapeand pages 26-44). Barreiros et al. found that microscopy d50 was approximately 13% higher than Malvern LD for irregular crushed glass (70.30 vs 62.04 µm) and approximately 24% higher for mica (79.05 vs 63.86 µm), with the ordering d50(SediGraph) < d50(Coulter) < d50(Malvern) < d50(Microscope) (barreiros1996calculatingshapefactors pages 3-4).

For sieving (ASTM B214/B822), the measured diameter corresponds to the minimum cross-section that passes a mesh opening, which differs from both the area-equivalent and scattering-equivalent diameters. Sieving and LD typically agree better for near-spherical powders but diverge for elongated or plate-like particles (hogg2008issuesinparticle pages 4-5).

## Number-to-Volume Conversion

SIA intrinsically produces a number-weighted distribution, since each detected particle contributes one count. Converting to a volume-weighted distribution requires cubing each particle's measured diameter (assuming sphericity), which dramatically increases the influence of a small number of large particles while suppressing the contribution of many small particles (merkus2018particlesandnanoparticles pages 22-24, hogg2008issuesinparticle pages 4-5, merkus2018particlesandnanoparticles pages 19-22). The conversion shifts d50 and d90 upward and can substantially change the distribution shape, especially for broad or skewed PSDs. A quantitative example illustrates the effect: measuring 500 particles microscopically, of which 5% (∼25 particles) exceed 20 µm, yields an RSD of approximately 20% for that fraction in the number basis and approximately 14% when expressed as the corresponding ∼70% volume fraction (merkus2018particlesandnanoparticles pages 22-24). The conversion also amplifies any error in measuring the largest particles, making d90 inherently less precise than d50 in image-derived volume distributions (barreiros1996calculatingshapefactors pages 3-4).

## Number of Particles Needed for a Given Precision

The minimum particle count depends strongly on distribution polydispersity. Wouters et al. (2025), using electron microscopy of nanomaterials with subsampling strategies, reported optimal particle counts (Nopt) for Fmin (analogous to d50): 136 particles for a low-polydispersity material (ERM-FD100), 189 for medium polydispersity (TiO₂RM), and 513 for high polydispersity (BaSO₄RM). For d90, the corresponding Nopt values were 193, 261, and 575 particles, respectively, yielding total expanded uncertainties of approximately 11–14% (wouters2025assessingparticlecount pages 7-9). At lower precision targets, low-polydispersity materials (IQR% ≤ 30) need only 35 particles for 10% uncertainty in the median, while highly polydisperse materials (IQR% 55–80) require approximately 260 particles for the same uncertainty and approximately 1000 particles for 5% uncertainty (wouters2025assessingparticlecount pages 6-7). General literature recommendations for SIA range from 100 to 14,000 particles depending on the application, with 10,000 particles yielding approximately 1% RSD for d50 (schnepf2023apracticalprimer pages 4-5).

## Dispersion of Cohesive Fine Powders on Slides

Dispersing cohesive powders onto slides is critical for unbiased SIA. ISO 13322-1 recommends dispersing in an inert medium to prevent agglomeration. Wet dispersion—suspending powder in a suitable solvent with surfactant, then depositing a thin film on a glass slide—is generally preferred for cohesive fine powders because it separates primary particles more effectively than dry spreading (merkus2018particlesandnanoparticles pages 19-22, linsinger2019preparationandcharacterisation pages 10-13). For dry dispersion, instruments such as the Sympatec RODOS use high-velocity air to deagglomerate and disperse powder onto a measurement surface (linsinger2019preparationandcharacterisation pages 1-6). The interlaboratory characterization study by Linsinger et al. for non-spherical corundum reference materials showed within-laboratory standard deviations of 3.9–7.5% and between-laboratory standard deviations of 9–24% for SIA, with sample preparation (including dispersion) identified as a major contributor to the larger uncertainty (linsinger2019preparationandcharacterisation pages 13-17). Particles below approximately 3 µm are problematic for optical SIA because diffraction halos cause substantial size overestimation and inaccurate shape measurement (schnepf2023apracticalprimer pages 5-7).

## Resolution Limits (Pixels per Particle)

The smallest particle of interest should occupy at least 5–10 pixels across its diameter for reliable size measurement by SIA (schnepf2023apracticalprimer pages 5-7). The theoretical diffraction-limited detection limit of optical microscopy is approximately 0.2 µm, but particles below approximately 0.8 µm are often not imageable under realistic conditions, and the practical lower limit for quantitative measurement is approximately 3 µm (schnepf2023apracticalprimer pages 5-7). For the user's Raspberry Pi HQ camera with a 0.12–1.8× zoom lens, the 12.3 MP sensor (4056 × 3040 pixels, 1.55 µm pixel pitch) at 1.8× magnification would give an image pixel size of approximately 0.86 µm/pixel, theoretically resolving particles down to approximately 4–9 µm (5–10 pixels) if optical quality is sufficient. At the typical AM powder range of 15–45 µm, particles would span 17–52 pixels, well above the minimum.

## Low-Cost Camera/Smartphone Microscopes

Several low-cost microscopy platforms have been validated against reference instruments, though primarily for airborne particulate matter and biological samples rather than bulk powder PSD. Wu et al. demonstrated a smartphone-based lens-free microscopy platform (c-Air) with approximately 93% particle sizing accuracy for PM₂.₅/PM₁₀, validated against EPA-approved beta-attenuation monitors (schnepf2023apracticalprimer pages 5-7). Zhang et al. developed an ultra-low-cost (<$4 USD) smartphone microscope with approximately 5 µm focus-tuning accuracy, using CycleGAN image enhancement to approach benchtop microscope quality (schnepf2023apracticalprimer pages 5-7). However, no published study was identified that directly validates a Raspberry Pi or smartphone camera-based SIA system against laser diffraction or sieving for bulk powder PSD (d10/d50/d90) in the AM-relevant size range. This represents an open gap that the user's planned work could fill.

# Part (b): Laboratory Powder Spreading Rigs for Powder-Bed AM

## Spreadability Metrics

No standardized spreadability metric has yet been established (hulme2023apracticableand pages 1-2, talebi2021characterisationofspreadabilityb pages 46-49). The metrics used across published laboratory spreading rigs include:

- **Coverage**: Build-plate coverage percentage, empty-patch frequency, size, and area (haydari2021thespreadingbehaviourb pages 48-51, haydari2021thespreadingbehavioura pages 48-51, haydari2021thespreadingbehaviourd pages 48-51)
- **Packing fraction / layer density**: Mass of spread layer divided by layer volume, or expressed as a fraction of solid, tapped, or apparent density. Hulme et al. proposed ≥40% of apparent density as a screening threshold (hulme2023apracticableand pages 1-2, hulme2023apracticableand pages 10-11)
- **Areal mass / deposition rate**: Mass per unit area or mass deposited per unit time (haydari2021thespreadingbehaviour pages 51-54, haydari2021thespreadingbehaviourb pages 48-51)
- **Surface roughness**: Ra, RMS roughness from profilometry or laser scanning (brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 1-2, haydari2021thespreadingbehaviour pages 51-54)
- **Uniformity indicators**: Brightness variation, convex hull, bounding box from imaging (whiting2023powderspreadingtestbed pages 9-12)
- **Coordination number**: From DEM simulations (haydari2021thespreadingbehaviour pages 51-54, talebi2021characterisationofspreadabilitya pages 40-43)

Streaks have not been formalized as a named metric but are implicitly captured through roughness, empty-patch, and uniformity measurements.

## Effect of Gap (Layer Thickness) and Speed

**Gap thickness** (expressed in particle diameters) has a strong positive effect on spreading quality. DEM simulations by Fouda and Bayly using idealized Ti-6Al-4V particles showed that packing fraction increases almost linearly with gap thickness across gaps of 2–6 particle diameters (fouda2019ademstudy pages 11-12, fouda2019ademstudy pages 1-2). Increasing gap size also reduces the frequency and size of empty patches and decreases jamming probability (haydari2021thespreadingbehaviour pages 51-54, haydari2021thespreadingbehavioura pages 48-51). At a gap of 4d and 100 mm/s, Fouda and Bayly reported a packing fraction of 35% immediately behind the blade, falling to 25.8% in the final deposited layer due to residual particle inertia (fouda2019ademstudy pages 11-12).

**Spreading speed** generally degrades layer quality. Shaheen et al. reported blade-spread mass fractions (MF) of approximately 50% at 10 mm/s, dropping to 20–30% at 50 mm/s and approximately 20% at 100 mm/s. A counter-clockwise rotating roller achieved a maximum MF of approximately 60% at 10 mm/s, consistently outperforming the blade (shaheen2021theinfluenceof pages 9-12). Higher speeds increase surface roughness, porosity, and particle collision effects, reduce coordination number, and promote looser packing (haydari2021thespreadingbehaviour pages 51-54, haydari2021thespreadingbehavioura pages 51-54, haydari2021thespreadingbehaviourb pages 51-54).

## Repeatability

Hulme et al. reported that their thin-film applicator test was repeatable and reproducible: for Powder G spread at 150 µm nominal thickness and 150 mm/s, two independent operators obtained mean layer densities of 3.89 ± 0.26 and 4.02 ± 0.51 g/cm³, with both measurements within one standard deviation of each other (hulme2023apracticableand pages 8-10, hulme2023apracticableand pages 4-5). Manual spreading rigs showed poorer repeatability due to operator-dependent positioning and velocity errors (talebi2021characterisationofspreadabilityb pages 46-49, talebi2021characterisationofspreadabilitya pages 46-49).

## Flowability Metrics as Predictors of Spreadability

The literature consistently finds that flowability and spreadability are related but distinct properties, and that traditional flowability metrics do not reliably predict spreading quality individually:

- **Angle of repose (AOR)**: High AOR is qualitatively associated with poor spreading (talebi2021characterisationofspreadabilitya pages 46-49, talebi2021characterisationofspreadability pages 46-49), and Carr categories can indicate general flow regime, but no universal quantitative correlation with spreading packing fraction has been established (haydari2021thespreadingbehaviour pages 27-31).
- **Hausner ratio (HR)**: Listed among flowability assessments and qualitatively associated with better spreading at lower values, but no direct HR-to-spreadability quantitative correlation was established (haydari2021thespreadingbehavioura pages 31-34, haydari2021thespreadingbehaviour pages 31-34, haydari2021thespreadingbehaviourb pages 31-34).
- **Rotating drum avalanche angle**: Considered more representative of spreading dynamics than static AOR because it incorporates dynamic powder behavior (haydari2021thespreadingbehavioura pages 31-34, haydari2021thespreadingbehaviourc pages 31-34, brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 1-2). Brocksieper et al. found conditional correlations between drum and rheometer metrics and spreading roughness, but these depended on recoater type and speed (brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 1-2).
- **Shear-cell ffc**: Classifies powders from very cohesive (ffc < 2) to free flowing (ffc > 10) but was not found to reliably predict spreading performance; shear-cell testing may fail to detect some flowability changes relevant to spreading (haydari2021thespreadingbehaviour pages 27-31, haydari2021thespreadingbehaviourc pages 27-31).
- **Hall flow**: Did not correlate with spread layer density in Hulme et al.'s 24-powder study, though the two powders that failed to spread also failed the Hall funnel (hulme2023apracticableand pages 1-2, hulme2023apracticableand pages 10-11).
- **FT4 specific energy**: Higher specific energy (greater interparticle friction) was linked to poorer deposition and lower final-part quality in stainless steel, making it a more promising candidate metric than traditional ones (haydari2021thespreadingbehavioura pages 31-34, haydari2021thespreadingbehaviour pages 31-34, haydari2021thespreadingbehaviourb pages 31-34).

## DEM Validation of Spreading Rigs

DEM validation for powder spreading typically involves calibrating contact parameters (friction, cohesion, restitution) using independent experiments—commonly angle of repose, powder heap profile, or packing density—and then comparing simulated layer properties against experimental measurements (lampitella2021discreteelementmethod pages 1-2, haydari2021thespreadingbehavioura pages 51-54). Avrampos and Vosniakos validated their DEM model by cross-checking simulated angle of repose and angle of avalanche against experiments. Lampitella et al. validated DEM parameters through ad hoc experiments and simulated two spreading velocities representing different flow regimes (lampitella2021discreteelementmethod pages 1-2, lampitella2021discreteelementmethod pages 9-11). The key limitation is that many DEM studies use idealized spherical, monodisperse, non-cohesive particles, which may not represent real polydisperse AM powder behavior (haydari2021thespreadingbehavioura pages 51-54).

## Numbers for AlSi10Mg, 316L, and Ti-6Al-4V

- **Ti-6Al-4V**: Fouda and Bayly's DEM study used idealized spherical Ti-6Al-4V particles and obtained a final layer packing fraction of 25.8% at gap = 4d and blade speed = 100 mm/s (fouda2019ademstudy pages 11-12, fouda2019ademstudy pages 1-2). Brika and Brailovski tested gas-atomized Ti-6Al-4V (d10 = 25.3, d50 = 35.8, d90 = 46.4 µm) in their instrumented apparatus across 30–100 µm layers and 100–500 mm/s speeds. In Hulme et al., Ti-6Al-4V powder J (d50 = 34 µm, apparent density 2.52 g/cm³) was tested and successfully formed thin layers (hulme2023apracticableand pages 2-4).
- **AlSi10Mg**: Hulme et al. tested AlSi10Mg powder K (d50 = 38 µm, 25–45 µm fraction, apparent density 1.34 g/cm³, mean aspect ratio 0.93) (hulme2023apracticableand pages 2-4). AlSi10Mg was found to be especially sensitive to moisture: its rougher, more irregular morphology made flowability and spread-layer relative density more humidity-dependent than Ti-6Al-4V or Inconel 718, though air drying improved its spreading performance (haydari2021thespreadingbehaviourd pages 48-51, haydari2021thespreadingbehaviourc pages 48-51).
- **316L stainless steel**: Multiple 316L powders spanning nominal fractions from 0–20 to 90–150 µm were tested in Hulme et al., with apparent densities ranging from approximately 3.01 to 4.33 g/cm³ (hulme2023apracticableand pages 2-4). In counter-rolling DEM by Chen et al. (reported in Haydari), 316L packing density decreased and roughness increased as spreading speed increased (haydari2021thespreadingbehavioura pages 51-54, haydari2021thespreadingbehaviourc pages 51-54). Hulme et al. proposed that 316L powders of similar size showed similar spreading performance, with one exception (Powder N performing notably better than same-sized peers) (hulme2023apracticableand pages 10-11).

Overall, the literature supports that laboratory spreading rigs using thin-film applicators or modular testbeds can reproducibly rank powders, but no single traditional flowability metric (AOR, HR, avalanche angle, ffc) independently predicts spreading quality, and DEM validation for specific alloys remains largely qualitative rather than quantitatively validated against alloy-specific experimental data.
