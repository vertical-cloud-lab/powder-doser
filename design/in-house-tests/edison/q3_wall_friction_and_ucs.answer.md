## Literature Review: Tilting-Plate Wall Friction and Uniaxial Compression Testing at Low Consolidation Stresses

### Part (a): Inclined-Plane / Tilting-Plate Wall Friction

#### 1. Principle and Comparison with Standard Shear Cells

In an inclined-plane or tilting-plate wall friction test, a powder-filled ring or sled rests on a wall coupon and the inclination is increased quasi-statically until gross sliding initiates. The static wall friction coefficient is obtained as μ_s = tan θ_s for negligible adhesion (ramirezgomez2025testapparatusfor pages 8-10, pierrat1994theflowproperties pages 29-34). This contrasts with standard shear-cell wall friction methods—Jenike translational (ASTM D6128) and Schulze ring shear (ASTM D6773)—which drag pre-consolidated powder across a wall coupon under controlled normal loads and measure the resulting shear stress to construct a wall yield locus (khala2021characterisingpowderflow pages 38-42, han2011comparisonofwall pages 1-3).

The two approaches do not automatically produce identical results. The inclined-plane measures incipient static sliding at a single low normal stress set by gravity, while shear cells produce a locus over a range of controlled normal stresses. Pillai et al. (2007) showed that an on-line wall friction tester, conceptually related to a sled geometry, produced significantly higher wall friction angles than a Jenike tester, in part because longer shear displacements allowed particle embedding into the wall surface—an effect the limited 10 mm Jenike stroke could not replicate (pillai2007comparisonbetweenthe pages 4-5, pillai2007comparisonbetweenthe pages 1-2).

#### 2. Low Normal Stress Behavior (50–500 Pa)

At low normal stress, standard shear cells encounter fundamental limitations. Jenike shear-flow measurements below approximately 1 kPa consolidation stress are generally considered impractical (khala2021characterisingpowderflow pages 38-42). Han (2011) compared Jenike and Schulze ring shear wall friction tests for PE pellets on stainless steel and aluminum wall plates and found that the two testers agreed well at higher pressures (4600 and 10 000 Pa) when wall roughness was comparable, but at 500 Pa the Schulze RST gave substantially larger wall friction angles than the Jenike tester, with the authors unable to determine which method was more accurate (han2011comparisonofwall pages 3-7, han2011comparisonofwall pages 7-7, han2011comparisonofwall pages 1-3). This divergence at low stress is practically important because low wall pressures occur near silo outlets.

A tilting-plate test is naturally well-suited to the 50–500 Pa regime because gravity supplies both normal and tangential forces without requiring subtraction of two small load-cell signals. By varying specimen mass and ring area, normal stresses can be tuned directly to the desired range.

#### 3. Stress Dependence of Wall Friction Angle and Wall Adhesion

The wall yield locus is generally expressed as τ_w = τ_0 + σ_n tan(φ_w), where τ_0 is the wall adhesion (the shear stress intercept at zero normal stress) and φ_w is the wall friction angle (pierrat1994theflowproperties pages 29-34, castellanos2005therelationshipbetween pages 53-55). For an ideal Coulomb material the locus is linear, but curvature is commonly observed in practice (pierrat1994theflowproperties pages 29-34). The wall friction angle is stress-dependent: it increases as wall normal stress decreases, rising sharply below approximately 100 Pa. At very low stresses, even a 1 Pa change in wall shear stress can produce a large angular deviation, so scatter in reported φ_w values becomes considerable (luddecke2026flowabilityofpowder pages 5-7). Lüddecke et al. (2026) showed that for cohesionless stainless steel powder, extrapolating a wall yield locus fitted above 1000 Pa produced an apparent wall adhesion intercept of 120 Pa, which was physically implausible. Direct low-stress measurements down to 25 Pa instead showed a linear wall yield locus with a mean wall friction angle of 19.7° and no true adhesion, demonstrating that apparent wall adhesion can be an artifact of high-stress extrapolation (luddecke2026flowabilityofpowder pages 5-7, luddecke2026flowabilityofpowder pages 4-5).

#### 4. Wall Friction on Polymers (PLA, PETG, ABS, Nylon), Stainless Steel, and Glass

Direct measurements of powder wall friction on 3D-printed polymer surfaces are sparse but beginning to appear. Hirschberg et al. (2018) fabricated a PLA wall plate for a Schulze ring shear tester and measured wall friction of lactose monohydrate and mannitol. At a low normal stress of 200 Pa, the difference between untreated 3D-printed PLA and stainless steel was small and considered to have no practical influence. At higher normal stresses, however, the PLA–stainless steel difference increased, with the rough printed PLA surface producing higher friction (hirschberg2018additivemanufacturingof pages 4-6). The authors noted that solvent-vapor or mechanical post-treatment of PLA could reduce wall friction.

For granular materials on PLA, Arunyanart et al. (2025) measured the coefficient of static friction between rice grains and 3D-printed PLA at 0.324–0.574 (corresponding nominally to 18–30°), identifying this as the dominant DEM calibration parameter with 96.49% contribution to angle-of-repose prediction (arunyanart2025comprehensivedemcalibration pages 8-11, arunyanart2025comprehensivedemcalibration pages 1-2, arunyanart2025comprehensivedemcalibration pages 11-12). These values are specific to rice grains and cannot be transferred directly to fine powders without independent measurement.

#### 5. Start Angle of Thin Powder Layers on Inclined Planes (Pouliquen h_stop/h_start Curves)

For non-cohesive granular media on rough inclined planes, Pouliquen and Forterre (2001) established that both the starting angle θ_start(h) and stopping angle θ_stop(h) depend on layer thickness h, with the two curves separated by approximately one degree of hysteresis. Both critical angles increase strongly as the layer becomes thin—for very thin layers the angles diverge—and approach finite, thickness-independent limits for large h. For glass beads, the empirical fit parameters were δ_1 = 21°, δ_2 = 30.7°, δ_3 = 22.2°, and L = 0.65 mm (pouliquen2001frictionlawfor pages 5-8). The stopping thickness h_stop(θ) is the inverse function of θ_stop(h) and defines the deposit remaining after flow ceases at a given inclination. A non-local friction-based model reproduces this behavior, predicting θ_stop = tan⁻¹[μ_Y(H + z_0)/H] with hysteresis arising from distinct yield and stability thresholds (artoni2010mechanicsofdense pages 65-67).

For **cohesive powders**, the framework is significantly modified. Gans, Pouliquen, and Nicolas (2020) showed that cohesion introduces an additional thickness-dependent yield stress, expressed as h sin θ_start = μh cos θ_start + h_c, where h_c = τ_c/(φρ_g g) is a cohesion length representing the maximum thickness of a vertically self-supporting layer. Thinner layers require a larger angle to initiate flow, and the influence of cohesion diminishes for thick layers (gans2020cohesioncontrolledgranularmaterial pages 8-9). De Ryck and Louisnard (2013) extended this to finite-width channels, finding that cohesion raises the steady-flow onset angle above the angle of repose, creates a surface plug zone, and strongly interacts with channel-width confinement—even weak cohesion can measurably increase the avalanche angle as channel width decreases (ryck2013depthandminimal pages 33-35, ryck2013depthandminimal pages 12-14, ryck2013depthandminimal pages 1-3). Mandal, Nicolas, and Pouliquen (2021) showed that the effective cohesion during flow is not controlled solely by interparticle adhesion but also depends on particle stiffness and inelasticity; cohesion broadens the hysteresis between starting and stopping conditions and promotes shear-band instability (mandal2021flowsofcohesive pages 4-5).

**Williams and Birks split-mould tester.** The original Williams unconfined compression method uses a segmented split mould (H/D = 3.5) with successive compaction increments. The unconfined yield strength is plotted against 1/N (where N is the number of layers) and extrapolated to 1/N = 0, representing infinitely many zero-thickness increments to minimize wall-friction bias. More than ten layers are generally used. The titanium dioxide flow function agreed closely with Jenike shear-cell results (stanley‐wood2009bulksolidshandling pages 55-57, stanley‐wood2009bulksolidshandling pages 57-59).

**Parrella uniaxial compaction tester (UCT).** Parrella et al. (2008) compared a cylindrical UCT with a Schulze ring shear tester for five powders. Without wall-friction correction, the UCT systematically gave lower unconfined yield strengths and could place powders in different flowability classes than the RST. Applying a Janssen-type correction with lateral stress ratio K = 0.4 and independently measured wall friction angle brought the UCT flow function into close agreement with the RST for calcium carbonate, but only at low consolidation stresses for the more cohesive food powders. At high consolidation stresses, additional mechanisms beyond wall friction (particle fragmentation, orientation changes) caused divergence (parrella2008comparisonbetweena pages 9-11, parrella2008comparisonbetweena pages 1-2, parrella2008comparisonbetweena pages 7-9, parrella2008comparisonbetweena pages 4-7, parrella2008comparisonbetweena pages 2-4).

**Freeman Advanced Uniaxial Powder Tester (AUPT).** This instrument uses a 41 mm diameter × 100 mm tall specimen (H/D ≈ 2.44), consolidates at 0.5 mm/s, and crushes at 0.2 mm/s. The analysis assumes uniform stress transmission and does not explicitly correct for wall friction (mahmoud2024measuringpowderflow pages 84-89).

**Greenwich Uniaxial Tester (GUT).** Developed by Salehi et al. (2019), the GUT uses a 95 mm internal-diameter cell with a low H/D ratio, a central 30 mm punch-out hole, and a defined failure plane. It requires approximately 75% less sample than a conventional UCT and reportedly achieves substantially better agreement with Jenike and Brookfield PFT results, though it operates at consolidation stresses of 10–130 kPa (salehi2019anewuniaxial pages 4-6, salehi2019anewuniaxial pages 2-4).

**Johanson Indicizer.** This device uses a cylindrical cell with a two-part upper piston. The sample is consolidated for a preset time, the lower support is removed, and the inner piston is pushed down to failure. The measured empirical strength is multiplied by approximately 2.2 to estimate unconfined yield strength. While faster than Jenike testing, multiple researchers questioned whether the corrected stress represents true unconfined yield strength, and comparisons found that the Indicizer often produced appreciably lower unconfined yield strengths and arching diameters than Jenike and Peschl shear cells (stanley‐wood2009bulksolidshandling pages 59-62).

**Brookfield Powder Flow Tester (PFT).** Though technically an annular shear cell rather than a uniaxial tester, the Brookfield PFT provides a low-stress benchmark. Its standard-volume cell operates over approximately 0.3–4.8 kPa, with the lowest setting at 0.3 kPa (300 Pa), which partially overlaps the user's 0.2–2 kPa target range (perdomo2015developmentofa pages 45-49, berry2015brookfieldpowderflow pages 4-7, stavrou2020assessingpowderflowability pages 62-65).

#### 2. Height-to-Diameter Ratio

The H/D ratio is a critical design parameter. Wall friction produces a Janssen-type exponential decay of vertical stress with depth, so a large H/D creates nonuniform consolidation and variable bulk density along the specimen (chen2017onuniaxialcompression pages 4-8, chen2017onuniaxialcompression pages 1-4). Minimizing H/D reduces this gradient but, below a critical value, the failure plane intersects the end plates and invalidates the measurement. While a 45° failure-plane assumption suggests H/D ≥ 1, experimental evidence indicates that end-plate slippage effects become negligible only when H/D exceeds approximately 2 (stanley‐wood2009bulksolidshandling pages 55-57). Published values range from H/D = 2.0 (Chen et al. 2017, using 80 mm diameter × 160 mm height) to H/D = 3.5 (Williams) to H/D ≈ 2.44 (Freeman AUPT) (chen2017onuniaxialcompression pages 8-14, stanley‐wood2009bulksolidshandling pages 55-57, mahmoud2024measuringpowderflow pages 84-89). For the user's proposed 20 mm diameter × 40 mm column, H/D = 2.0, which is at the minimum recommended value.

#### 3. Mould Wall Friction During Consolidation

Wall friction during uniaxial consolidation is the primary source of discrepancy between uniaxial and shear-cell methods. The applied vertical stress decreases exponentially with depth according to a Janssen force balance, shifting the specimen to a lower effective consolidation stress, increasing the voids ratio, and producing a lower yield locus than achieved in shear cells (chen2017onuniaxialcompression pages 4-8, chen2017onuniaxialcompression pages 1-4). This results in uniaxial testing generally indicating apparently higher flowability (lower unconfined yield strength) than shear testing (parrella2008comparisonbetweena pages 1-2, parrella2008comparisonbetweena pages 2-4).

Correction approaches include: (i) the Janssen-based Parrella correction using K = 0.4 and independently measured wall friction angle (parrella2008comparisonbetweena pages 9-11, parrella2008comparisonbetweena pages 2-4); (ii) the Williams infinite-layer extrapolation, which mathematically removes the layer-based wall-friction contribution (stanley‐wood2009bulksolidshandling pages 57-59); (iii) Chen et al.'s (2017) undercompaction technique, targeting final bulk density rather than specified normal stress, using ten layers with 5% undercompaction at the bottom and linear variation to the top (chen2017onuniaxialcompression pages 4-8); and (iv) the POSTEC tester's flexible membrane, which reduces specimen–die friction by wrapping the sample (stanley‐wood2009bulksolidshandling pages 59-62).

For the user's 20 mm × 40 mm split-mould design, wall friction will be particularly important because the small diameter increases the wall-contact-area-to-cross-section ratio. Using a PLA split mould where the wall is removed before failure testing is advantageous; measuring wall friction on the same PLA surface with the tilting-plate test can provide the correction inputs.

#### 4. Uniaxial Strength vs. Shear-Cell Unconfined Yield Strength and ffc

The flow function coefficient is defined as ff_c = σ_1/σ_c (some authors use the inverse notation), with Jenike classifications: ff_c < 1 not flowing; 1–2 very cohesive; 2–4 cohesive; 4–10 easy flowing; >10 free flowing (khala2021characterisingpowderflow pages 38-42). Uniaxial testers generally produce lower unconfined yield strengths than shear cells at the same nominal consolidation stress. Parrella et al. found that after wall-friction correction, agreement was obtained for calcium carbonate but only at low consolidation levels for other powders; at high consolidation, the corrected UCT sometimes predicted higher strengths than the RST, possibly due to particle structural changes during shearing (parrella2008comparisonbetweena pages 9-11, parrella2008comparisonbetweena pages 7-9). Salehi et al. (2019) confirmed that conventional UCT flow functions were substantially lower than Jenike and PFT results; applying a wall-friction and weight correction brought agreement only for the free-flowing powder and reduced, but did not eliminate, the discrepancy for cohesive materials (salehi2019anewuniaxial pages 6-6, salehi2019anewuniaxial pages 4-6).

Critically, directly extrapolating UCT flow functions to low consolidation conditions can underestimate powder cohesion (parrella2008comparisonbetweena pages 1-2), which is particularly relevant for the user's 0.2–2 kPa target range.

#### 5. Smallest Consolidation Stresses at Which Strength Has Been Measured Reliably

The uniaxial compression test requires sufficient consolidation to form a self-supporting column, fundamentally limiting its applicability at very low stresses (mahmoud2024measuringpowderflow pages 52-57). The practical lower limit depends on powder cohesion, column dimensions, and the ability to demould without collapse, but is generally several kPa for typical powder columns.

For shear cells, the Schulze ring shear tester can measure below 1 kPa (khala2021characterisingpowderflow pages 42-46), and the Brookfield PFT has its lowest standard setting at 0.3 kPa (300 Pa) (perdomo2015developmentofa pages 45-49, berry2015brookfieldpowderflow pages 4-7). The FT4 shear cell was found unreliable for most materials at pre-shear normal stresses of 1 kPa and below, with Brookfield and FT4 results both producing lower unconfined yield strengths than the Schulze at low stresses (stavrou2020assessingpowderflowability pages 62-65). Shear-cell measurements at 0.06 and 0.25 kPa showed large variability in repeat results, with target and actual stresses differing substantially (stavrou2020investigationofpowder pages 26-34).

**Ball indentation** extends to significantly lower stresses: Stavrou et al. (2020) demonstrated measurements at major principal stresses as low as 0.1 kPa (100 Pa) and 0.2 kPa (200 Pa), with measurements also at 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 kPa (stavrou2020investigationofpowder pages 26-34, stavrou2020investigationofpowder pages 20-26). The constraint factor relating hardness to unconfined yield strength was validated down to 0.1 kPa through DEM simulations (stavrou2020investigationofpowder pages 20-26). However, at these very low stresses, hardness rises steeply with stress due to rapid packing-fraction changes, error bars increase, and careful control of bed preparation and contact detection becomes critical (stavrou2020investigationofpowder pages 20-26).

The SSSpinTester has been reported at stresses as low as 50–500 Pa using sample quantities of 0.02–1.00 g (fernandezUnknownyeardevelopmentandevaluationa pages 34-37). The Flodex method operates at major principal stresses below approximately 200 Pa (mahmoud2024measuringpowderflow pages 48-52). The Sevilla powder tester measures tensile failure stress under very small applied loads (khala2021characterisingpowderflow pages 42-46).

For the user's proposed 20 mm × 40 mm split-mould uniaxial test at 0.2–2 kPa, the lower end (200 Pa) is at the frontier of reliable uniaxial measurement. Very cohesive powders may form stable columns at 200 Pa, but free-flowing materials will not. Complementing with ball indentation or the Brookfield PFT at 0.3 kPa would provide cross-validation.

---

### Summary and Practical Implications

For the tilting-plate wall friction test (Test 3), the literature supports the measurement approach at 70–450 Pa as filling a genuine gap where standard Jenike and Schulze methods struggle. Key recommendations include: using multiple ring loads to separate wall friction coefficient and wall adhesion; recognizing that apparent wall adhesion from high-stress extrapolation can be artifactual; verifying that failure occurs at the powder–wall interface rather than within the powder; and cross-calibrating against an annular shear cell at overlapping stresses (300–500 Pa). PLA–stainless steel differences are expected to be small at 200 Pa but grow at higher stress.

For the uniaxial test (Test 8), the proposed 20 mm × 40 mm column (H/D = 2.0) is at the minimum acceptable ratio. At 0.2–2 kPa consolidation, only cohesive powders will form stable columns. The split-mould design appropriately eliminates wall contact during failure, but Janssen-type stress nonuniformity during consolidation will affect the effective stress. Using incremental layered filling (following Williams' method) with 1/N extrapolation would reduce wall-friction bias. The measured strength should be treated as an application-specific index rather than a direct equivalent of shear-cell ff_c, unless validated by cross-comparison.
