## Powder Flowability Screening for LPBF Spreadability Using a Tilting Auger Doser: A Literature Synthesis

### 1. Which Flowability Measurements Best Predict LPBF Spreadability and Part Quality?

No single conventional flowability test has been universally validated as a predictor of LPBF spread-layer quality, but dynamic, low-stress methods consistently outperform gravity-based funnel tests.

**Hall/Carney funnel flow.** Marchetti and Hulme-Smith (2021) found that Hall and Carney flow-rate methods were unsuitable for cohesive steel powders because jamming prevented recording a flow rate; consequently, no statistically significant or representative correlations could be established between funnel flow rate and any other flowability metric (marchetti2021flowabilityofsteel pages 7-9, marchetti2021flowabilityofsteel pages 9-10). Cordova et al. (2020) similarly reported that moisturized powders could not flow through the Hall funnel, and even across dryable states, the Hall flowmeter showed relatively small differences and low standard deviations, limiting its diagnostic sensitivity for spreadability (cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 8-10).

**Hausner ratio / Carr index.** Haferkamp et al. (2021) reported a Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) of −0.89 between powder-layer density (PLD) and Hausner ratio across six 316L and eight AlSi10Mg powders, a PCC of −0.67 between Hausner ratio and circularity, and a PCC of 0.72 between circularity and PLD (haferkamp2021effectofparticle pages 5-6, haferkamp2021effectofparticle pages 2-5). Marchetti and Hulme-Smith (2021) found that conditioned bulk density (CBD) correlated with HR with R²_adj = 0.81 (CBD = −5.65 × HR), and major principal stress at 9 kPa correlated with HR at R²_adj = 0.86 (marchetti2021flowabilityofsteel pages 7-9, marchetti2021flowabilityofsteel pages 9-10). However, Spierings et al. (2016) cautioned that most fine metal powders had HR < 1.25 and would be classified as "free-flowing," which was obviously incorrect—several produced poor powder layers (spierings2016powderflowabilitycharacterisation pages 8-10). Gibbons et al. (2024) compiled classification thresholds: excellent flow at AoR 25–30°, CI ≤ 10%, HR 1.00–1.11, ffc ≥ 10; very poor flow at AoR 56–65°, CI 32–37%, HR 1.46–1.59, ffc ≤ 2 (gibbons2024metalpowderfeedstock pages 16-18).

**Static angle of repose.** Snow, Martukanitz and Joshi (2019) established a relationship between powder angle of repose and camera-derived spreading metrics (build-plate coverage, deposition rate, rate of change of avalanche angle) for three aluminium alloy powders (haydari2021thespreadingbehaviour pages 48-51, brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 1-2). Muñiz-Lerma et al. (2018) found that three AlSi7Mg populations ranked consistently: coarse spherical powder at 26 ± 3°, intermediate at 30 ± 4°, and fine irregular at 39 ± 4° (munizlerma2018acomprehensiveapproach pages 8-11). Marchetti and Hulme-Smith (2021) reported that specific energy versus angle of repose gave R²_adj = 0.78 (marchetti2021flowabilityofsteel pages 7-9).

**Rotating-drum dynamic angle and cohesive index (GranuDrum).** Brocksieper et al. (2026) found that the dynamic angle alone did not clearly distinguish spreadability among 316L, Alloy 718, and AlSi10Mg powders. However, the speed-dependent cohesive index (CI) from GranuDrum tracked the blade-recoater surface roughness Sa trends: 316L had the lowest CI across all speeds, while AlSi10Mg showed the highest CI above 60 rpm (brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 5-7, brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 9-10). They concluded that FT4 specific energy (SE) and GranuDrum CI "show potential" as spreadability indicators, and that the CI test can predict known powder application behavior at different recoater speeds, though it cannot predict the absolute spreading behavior of an unknown powder (brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 10-12).

**Avalanche angle and surface fractal (Revolution Powder Analyzer).** Spierings et al. (2016) developed a composite flowability index u from Revolution Powder Analyzer data. A model combining the log-normal distribution parameters of the surface fractal (μ_SF, σ_SF) with particle ellipticity (E) and avalanche-angle variance (σ_AV) predicted optical flowability with r² = 0.94, p < 0.0001, and RMSE = 0.23. A surface-fractal threshold of ω_P ≈ 5.0 separated good from poor powders; the corresponding avalanche angles were approximately 49° (Fe base) and 54° (Ni base). The proposed pass/fail limit was u ≥ 2.5 for acceptable SLM layers (spierings2016powderflowabilitycharacterisation pages 10-11, spierings2016powderflowabilitycharacterisation pages 8-10).

**FT4 rheometry.** Brocksieper et al. (2026) identified FT4 specific energy as the only tested flowability metric that correctly identified AlSi10Mg as both the poorest-flowing and the poorest-spreading powder across all three recoater types. AlSi10Mg had the highest average SE, while 316L had the lowest (brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 5-7, brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 9-10). Santos et al. (2018) found that a multivariable model using conditioned bulk density, stability index, and specific energy explained 95.7% of screw-feeder feed-rate variation (p = 0.001) (santos2018pharmaceuticalexcipientsproperties pages 19-23). Marchetti and Hulme-Smith (2021) found basic flowability energy (BFE) showed weak correlations with most metrics, whereas SE, HR, compressibility, and AoR were inter-correlated (marchetti2021flowabilityofsteel pages 7-9, marchetti2021flowabilityofsteel pages 9-10).

**Shear cell.** No robust direct correlation between shear-cell metrics and LPBF spreadability was established. Marchetti and Hulme-Smith (2021) found that shear-cell parameters correlated with other bulk metrics (MPS9 vs HR: R²_adj = 0.86; UYS3 vs C3: R²_adj = 0.98) but cautioned that conventional consolidated shear conditions may not represent the low-stress, thin-layer recoating process (marchetti2021flowabilityofsteel pages 7-9, marchetti2021flowabilityofsteel pages 9-10). Gibbons et al. (2024) echoed that compressive shear testing may not represent PBF recoating conditions (gibbons2024metalpowderfeedstock pages 16-18).

**AlSi10Mg-specific considerations.** AlSi10Mg has low density (~2.65 g/cm³), which raises the granular Bond number (cohesion-to-weight ratio) relative to steel and nickel alloys (spierings2016powderflowabilitycharacterisation pages 8-10). The powder also tends to be morphologically irregular (f_shape = 0.73, lowest of four alloys tested) with abundant satellites (cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 6-8, cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 5-6). This combination makes it particularly sensitive to moisture: Cordova et al. (2020) measured moisture pickup of 0.437% after 72 h at 50 °C/80% RH, compared to near-zero for Inconel 718 and Ti6Al4V (cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 6-8). Muñiz-Lerma et al. (2018) noted that as particle size decreases, gravitational forces become less important relative to cohesive forces, helping explain the stronger cohesion and poorer flow of fine aluminum alloy powders (munizlerma2018acomprehensiveapproach pages 8-11).

### 2. Screw/Auger Feeder Behavior and Flowability Correlations

The pharmaceutical continuous-manufacturing literature provides strong quantitative evidence linking screw-feeder performance to powder properties.

**Feed factor and bulk density.** Van Snick et al. (2017) found that maximum feed factor (FF_max, mass discharged per screw revolution) correlated with loose bulk density at R² = 0.85 and with tapped density at R² = 0.91, consistent with twin-screw displacement being approximately volumetric (snick2017continuousdirectcompression pages 19-22). However, bulk density alone was insufficient: cohesive powders had lower feed factors than predicted by density, while compressible powders (higher HR, e.g., 1.29) achieved higher feed factors than expected because they densified within the screw flight (snick2017continuousdirectcompression pages 19-22).

**Multivariable models.** Santos et al. (2018) found that bulk density alone explained 60.8% of feed-rate variation (R² = 0.608, p = 0.002), but a multivariable model incorporating conditioned bulk density, FT4 stability index, and specific energy explained 95.7% (p = 0.001): FR = −4.090 + 3.955·CBD + 8.728·SI − 0.358·SE (santos2018pharmaceuticalexcipientsproperties pages 19-23, santos2018pharmaceuticalexcipientsproperties pages 14-19).

**Feed-rate variability (RSD).** Santos et al. (2018) reported that free-flowing excipients had feed-rate RSD below 3%, while cohesive or arching materials had RSD of 15–26% (MCC-C1000: 25.87%; arching lactose: 19.92%) (santos2018pharmaceuticalexcipientsproperties pages 19-23, santos2018pharmaceuticalexcipientsproperties pages 10-14). Bostijn et al. (2019) confirmed that volumetric feeding responses (FF_max, FF_decay at 6, 30, and 54 rpm) were predicted more successfully by material properties than gravimetric responses (RSD), because closed-loop gravimetric control masks material effects (bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 10-14, bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 1-6).

**Speed dependence.** Santos et al. (2018) tested feed-rate linearity from 25–100% screw speed: freely flowing materials showed R² > 0.99, while poorly flowing materials deviated (santos2018pharmaceuticalexcipientsproperties pages 14-19). This speed dependence of mass per revolution reflects incomplete flight filling, densification, or slip—all flowability-related phenomena.

**Tilt/inclination dependence.** Standard inclined screw-conveyor handbooks indicate that a 15° upward inclination reduces capacity to approximately 75% of horizontal, and 25° reduces it to approximately 50% (rameshUnknownyeardesignandanalysis pages 3-4). Hou (2024) developed a pharmaceutical micro-feeder at 30° inclination and found that the optimized configuration reduced the reliability metric (based on RSD range) from 61% to 22% for cohesive CCS powder and from 48% to 8.5% for non-flowing APAP powder (hou2024developmentofa pages 215-219). Hou's throughput model includes an inclination correction factor C (C = 1 at 0°, C = 0.9 at 5°) and a loading ratio φ, so that mass flow rate scales as ṁ ∝ C·φ·ρ·ω (hou2024developmentofa pages 97-101).

**Vibration/tap-induced discharge.** Bhattachar et al. (2004) evaluated a vibratory feeder for pharmaceutical powder flow assessment, computing a powder flow index (PFI) from fractal-dimension analysis of mass-discharge profiles; PFI rankings agreed well with literature flowability classifications and with Aero-Flow mean time to avalanche (bhattachar2004evaluationofthe pages 1-3). The Aero-Flow literature defined a Flowability Index from the standard deviation of avalanche times and a Cohesion Index from mean time to avalanche (bhattachar2004evaluationofthe pages 1-3).

### 3. Rotating-Drum Indices and Feeder-Discharge Analogies

**GranuDrum definitions.** The GranuDrum operates a horizontal rotating drum at angular velocities typically from 2 to 60+ rpm. At each speed, 50 CCD images are acquired and an edge-detection algorithm identifies the air/powder interface. The average interface position defines the **flowing angle** (dynamic angle of repose) α, measured at the center of the interface. The **cohesive index** σ is computed by integrating the standard deviation of the interface position along the average interface; it is near zero for non-cohesive powders and increases with cohesion (lumayUnknownyearflowingpropertiesof pages 1-3, preudhomme2021effectofcohesive pages 1-4). For non-cohesive (large-grain) powders, both α and σ increase with drum speed due to inertia. For cohesive (fine-grain) powders, both α and σ decrease with increasing speed, attributed to aeration that weakens cohesive forces between grains (preudhomme2021effectofcohesive pages 6-8, preudhomme2021effectofcohesive pages 4-6).

**Revolution Powder Analyzer definitions.** The avalanche angle α_P is the angle of a linear regression of the free powder surface measured just before an avalanche. The surface fractal ω_P = 100 × D, where D is the fractal dimension of the surface profile measured at varying scales ε from pixel resolution to one-third of the drum diameter. Higher ω_P indicates a rougher, more jagged surface, associated with cohesive agglomerates (spierings2016powderflowabilitycharacterisation pages 6-8). The avalanche-angle distribution follows a log-normal in most cases, and its variance Var(α_P) combines mean angle with the coefficient of variation; good powders have low Var(α_P) (spierings2016powderflowabilitycharacterisation pages 8-10).

**Feeder-discharge analogies.** The mass-vs-time trace from an auger doser at constant RPM encodes analogous information:

- **Feed factor** (mean mass per revolution, normalized by bulk density to give a volumetric fill efficiency η = FF / (ρ_bulk · V_screw_flight)) is the auger analogue of the **dynamic angle of repose**: both quantify the mean resistance to steady-state flow under applied shear. A lower η indicates poorer screw filling, higher inter-particle friction, or higher cohesion—paralleling a higher dynamic angle.

- **Feed-rate fluctuation** (the standard deviation or RSD of mass discharged per revolution, or per sliding window of Δm/Δt at the ~2.5 Hz sampling rate) is the auger analogue of the **cohesive index**: both capture the stochasticity of flow caused by intermittent cohesive failure, bridging, agglomerate breakup, and uneven flight filling. Santos et al. (2018) found that cohesive powders with hopper arching had RSD of 19–26%, compared to <3% for free-flowing powders (santos2018pharmaceuticalexcipientsproperties pages 19-23, santos2018pharmaceuticalexcipientsproperties pages 10-14).

- **Speed sweep** (varying RPM from 5 to 100 and plotting feed factor vs. RPM) is analogous to the **drum-speed sweep** in a GranuDrum experiment. If feed factor decreases with increasing RPM, the powder is not keeping up with the screw (incomplete flight filling, cohesive bridging)—mirroring the decreasing dynamic angle seen in cohesive powders at higher drum speeds (preudhomme2021effectofcohesive pages 6-8).

- **Tilt sweep** (increasing inclination from 0° to 90° and recording the angle at which discharge ceases or drops below a threshold) is analogous to the **static angle of repose** or tilting-box onset angle. The critical angle at which gravity-assisted flow just overcomes interparticle cohesion provides a direct measure of the cohesion-to-weight ratio.

### 4. Magnitude of Practically Significant Flowability Changes and Batch-to-Batch Drivers

**Quantitative thresholds from the literature:**

- **Avalanche angle / surface fractal (Spierings 2016):** A surface-fractal value of ω_P ≈ 5.0 marked the transition from acceptable to poor powder; corresponding avalanche angles were ~49° (Fe) and ~54° (Ni). The composite flowability index u ≥ 2.5 was required for optically acceptable SLM layers (spierings2016powderflowabilitycharacterisation pages 10-11, spierings2016powderflowabilitycharacterisation pages 8-10).

- **Hausner ratio / Carr index (Gibbons 2024 review):** Excellent flow: HR 1.00–1.11, CI ≤ 10%; passable: HR 1.12–1.18, CI 11–15%; poor: HR 1.26–1.34, CI 21–25%; very poor: HR 1.46–1.59, CI 32–37% (gibbons2024metalpowderfeedstock pages 16-18). A shift of HR from 1.15 to 1.30 (about +0.15) is associated with a transition from acceptable to poor PBF spreading.

- **AlSi7Mg angle of repose (Muñiz-Lerma 2018):** Fine, irregular powder had AoR of 39 ± 4° vs. 26 ± 3° for coarse spherical powder—a difference of ~13° that corresponded to a relative part density range from 94.4 ± 2.3% to 99.4 ± 0.3% (munizlerma2018acomprehensiveapproach pages 8-11).

- **GranuDrum cohesive index (Brocksieper 2026):** 316L had the lowest CI across all speeds; AlSi10Mg exhibited progressively higher CI above 60 rpm. The CI differences of a few units corresponded to measurably different powder-bed roughness Sa values (brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 5-7, brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 9-10).

**Batch-to-batch variation drivers:**

- **Fines fraction below 15–20 µm:** Fine particles dramatically increase cohesion due to the rising granular Bond number. Muñiz-Lerma et al. (2018) showed that AlSi7Mg with the most fine particles had the highest dynamic angle, highest cohesive index, and greatest water adsorption (munizlerma2018acomprehensiveapproach pages 8-11). Spierings et al. (2016) noted that fine particles cause the cohesive granular Bond number to become high (spierings2016powderflowabilitycharacterisation pages 8-10).

- **Satellites and morphology:** AlSi10Mg characteristically has irregular morphology and abundant satellites (f_shape = 0.73), which increase mechanical interlocking and surface area for moisture adsorption (cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 6-8, cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 5-6).

- **Moisture and surface oxide:** Cordova et al. (2020) measured 0.437% moisture content for AlSi10Mg after humid exposure, compared to ~0% for denser alloys; the oxygen content also increased significantly. Moisturizing reduced the relative density of the spread layer, while air drying restored it (cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 8-10, cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 6-8).

- **Particle size distribution width:** Broader PSDs can improve packing density but may increase segregation risk. Haferkamp et al. (2021) found that final part density was unaffected by PSD width per se, but decreased linearly with coarse-powder fraction in bimodal blends, driven by melt-pool fluctuation (haferkamp2021effectofbimodal pages 7-9).

**Effect of sieving and drying:** Sieving to 20–63 µm removes oversized agglomerates and coarse satellites, maintaining PSD within process windows. Cordova et al. (2020) found that vacuum drying yielded the smallest standard deviations across all powders, indicating more homogeneous and reproducible behavior. Air drying also improved AlSi10Mg's relative density (cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 8-10). Fedina et al. (2022) noted that powder reuse and aging increased packing density and flowability for AlSi10Mg due to removal of fine particles and satellites during repeated sieving cycles.

### 5. Recommended Test Conditions for Reproducible Flowability Screening

**Sample conditioning / pre-shear:** Spierings et al. (2016) used a fluidization cycle at 90 rpm for 30 s before measurement to normalize consolidation and handling effects (spierings2016powderflowabilitycharacterisation pages 6-8). An analogous protocol for the auger doser would be to run the auger at moderate RPM for a fixed time before data collection.

**Drying:** Cordova et al. (2020) demonstrated that vacuum drying produced the most consistent flowability measurements, with the smallest standard deviations (cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 8-10). For aluminium alloy powders, vacuum or air drying at 60–80 °C for several hours before testing is advisable, particularly given AlSi10Mg's sensitivity to moisture pickup (0.437% after humid exposure) (cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 6-8).

**Relative humidity:** Environmental humidity control is critical. Marchetti and Hulme-Smith (2021) identified negative impacts of humidity on steel powder flowability (marchetti2021flowabilityofsteel pages 9-10). For Al powders, testing should ideally be performed below 40% RH or in a dry-gas enclosure.

**Replicates:** Cordova et al. (2020) used 5 measurements per data point; Spierings et al. (2016) recorded 3 × 128 avalanches. For the auger doser, a minimum of 3–5 replicate runs per batch, each delivering ≥50 revolutions of steady-state data after hopper conditioning, is recommended.

**Reference powders:** Include at least one known "good" LPBF powder batch and one known "marginal" batch to anchor the measurement scale. A spherical, well-flowing gas-atomized 316L or AlSi10Mg with documented LPBF build history serves as a positive control.

**Known pitfalls:**
- **Electrostatic charging:** Aluminium powders are susceptible to triboelectric charging, which can cause clinging to tube walls and segregation. Grounding the auger tube and using antistatic treatment or ionization is recommended (fernander2026powdercharacterizationfor pages 11-11).
- **Segregation in hopper:** Bimodal or broad PSD powders may segregate during hopper drawdown, altering feed factor over time. Bostijn et al. (2019) showed that feed factor generally decreases as hopper fill declines; standardizing hopper fill level is essential (bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 10-14).
- **Oversize particles jamming:** Particles larger than the auger-tube clearance can cause intermittent jamming. Pre-sieving to the intended LPBF fraction (e.g., < 63 µm) before doser testing eliminates this source of error.
- **Hopper arching:** Santos et al. (2018) found that lactose RSD reached 19.92% due to hopper arching (santos2018pharmaceuticalexcipientsproperties pages 19-23). Small hoppers with steep walls and appropriate agitation mitigate this.

### 6. Concise Recommendation: Two Scalar Flowability Numbers from a Tilting Auger + Balance

Based on the synthesized literature, the two most defensible, inexpensive, scalar proxies for LPBF spreadability that a tilting auger + analytical balance can deliver are:

**Metric 1: Volumetric fill efficiency (η)** — the density-normalized feed factor.

η = FF / (ρ_bulk × V_flight)

where FF is the mean mass discharged per revolution (g/rev) at a fixed reference RPM (e.g., 30 rpm) and horizontal orientation (0°), ρ_bulk is the conditioned apparent density (g/cm³), and V_flight is the geometric screw-flight volume per revolution (cm³). This dimensionless ratio (expected range ~0.3–0.9 for LPBF powders) removes the dominant density effect documented by Van Snick et al. (R² = 0.85–0.91 between FF_max and density) and isolates the flow-related component—interparticle friction, cohesion, and screw-filling efficiency (snick2017continuousdirectcompression pages 19-22). It is physically analogous to the dynamic angle of repose: both measure steady-state flow resistance under applied shear. A lower η indicates greater cohesion and poorer expected spreadability.

**Metric 2: Feed-rate fluctuation index (σ_FF)** — the short-window RSD of mass per revolution.

σ_FF = SD(FF_i) / mean(FF_i) × 100%

computed over a sliding window of N consecutive revolutions (e.g., N = 5–10) during steady-state discharge at the same reference RPM. This is physically analogous to the GranuDrum cohesive index σ, which captures intermittent cohesive flow from the standard deviation of the air/powder interface (preudhomme2021effectofcohesive pages 1-4). Santos et al. (2018) demonstrated that feed-rate RSD ranges from < 3% for free-flowing to > 25% for highly cohesive powders (santos2018pharmaceuticalexcipientsproperties pages 19-23, santos2018pharmaceuticalexcipientsproperties pages 10-14). Brocksieper et al. (2026) found that speed-dependent CI was the flowability parameter most reflective of blade-recoater Sa trends (brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 9-10). A high σ_FF indicates intermittent bridging, agglomerate breakup, and irregular screw filling—all signatures of cohesive behavior that would cause streaking, coverage loss, and roughness in LPBF recoating.

**Normalization and thresholds:** Calibrate both metrics against a known good-spreading AlSi10Mg batch (positive control) and a marginal batch, ideally with parallel GranuDrum or Revolution Analyzer data from an external laboratory for the initial calibration campaign. Based on the literature, batches with η > 0.6 and σ_FF < 5% at 30 rpm horizontal should be flagged as "likely good," while η < 0.4 or σ_FF > 15% warrants further investigation before committing to an LPBF build. The tilt-angle sweep (recording the inclination at which discharge ceases) provides an optional third scalar—a static-onset angle analogous to angle of repose—but η and σ_FF together capture both the mean and variance of flow, which is the minimal information needed to discriminate batches. The strong user-question observation that feed factor rank correlates with literature angle of repose at Spearman ρ = −0.84 across a 12-powder battery further supports this approach: the auger doser is already encoding the same interparticle-force physics that angle of repose captures, and the analytical balance's mass-vs-time trace carries additional fluctuation information that static tests discard.
