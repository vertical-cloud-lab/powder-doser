## Physics-Based Simulation Methods for Screw/Auger Feeders and Powder Micro-Dosing: A Comprehensive Review

### 1. Introduction

The simulation of screw and auger feeders for powder dosing has been approached through a range of physics-based methods spanning particle-resolved discrete element methods, coupled fluid-particle approaches, continuum formulations, and analytical reduced-order models. This review synthesizes the available literature to identify which methods, software codes, contact and cohesion models, speed-up strategies, and validation approaches have been employed, and ranks them by suitability for a millimetre-scale auger dosing cohesive fine powders (10–100 µm) and salts.

### 2. Discrete Element Method (DEM) — The Dominant Approach

DEM is by far the most widely used physics-based simulation method for screw feeders and powder dosing. It resolves individual particle contacts, force chains, wall friction, bridging, and intermittent flow that are critical for understanding dose-to-dose variability.

**2.1 DEM with Linear Spring-Dashpot Contact (CSIRO Code)**

Owen and Cleary (2009) used the CSIRO in-house DEM code to predict screw conveyor performance including particle speed, mass flow rate, energy dissipation, and power consumption (owen2009predictionofscrew pages 1-2, owen2009predictionofscrew pages 4-8, owen2009predictionofscrew pages 10-11). They employed periodic one-pitch domains with a 38 mm diameter screw and studied the effects of screw speed (600–1400 rpm), inclination (0–90°), and fill level (30–70%). Mass flow rate decreased strongly and linearly with increasing inclination until about 60° and was reduced by 23–47% when operating vertically compared to horizontal (owen2009predictionofscrew pages 10-11). McBride and Cleary (2009) applied the same code to the OLDS elevator, using deliberately reduced contact stiffness (1000 N/m for sorghum, 250 N/m for wheat) to achieve computationally tractable time steps of 10⁻⁴–10⁻⁶ s with at least 15 steps per collision (mcbride2009aninvestigationand pages 5-6). Simulated mass-flow rates agreed very well with experiments, and flow rate was approximately linear with rotational speed (mcbride2009aninvestigationand pages 18-19). Super-quadric particle shapes were available but cost 2–5× more than equivalent sphere simulations (mcbride2009aninvestigationand pages 5-6).

**2.2 DEM with Hertz-Mindlin + SJKR Cohesion (LIGGGHTS)**

El-Kassem et al. (2021) conducted a systematic parametric study using LIGGGHTS with the Hertz-Mindlin contact model, the EPSD2 rolling resistance model, and the modified simplified Johnson-Kendall-Roberts (SJKR) cohesion model (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 5-6). The SJKR model adds a cohesive normal force F_coh = C·a to the Hertzian contact, where C is the cohesion energy density (J/m³) and a is the contact area (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 5-6). They investigated six DEM input parameters—Young's modulus, particle size, restitution coefficient, static friction, rolling friction, and cohesion energy density—and validated against angle of repose, porosity, and mass flow rate using free-flowing lactose (SpheroLac 100) and cohesive lactose (InhaLac 251) powders (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 7-9, elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 9-10). For cohesive powders, the rolling friction coefficient had the most substantial impact on angle of repose, while static friction played a minor role (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 23-24). Simulations were run on an eight-node cluster using 32 Intel Xeon CPUs; coarse-graining with a scaling factor S_f was used, preserving energy density while reducing particle count by S_f³ (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 7-9).

**2.3 DEM with Hertz-Mindlin + JKR (EDEM and Other Commercial Codes)**

EDEM, the most widely used commercial DEM platform, employs the JKR surface-energy adhesion model where setting surface energy to zero recovers non-cohesive Hertz contact (karkala2021analysisofpowder pages 19-24). Arthur and Rahmanian (2024) reviewed DEM applications to twin-screw granulators and feeders using EDEM with JKR cohesion, finding that simulations predicted mixing diffusion coefficients that agreed with experiments at different screw speeds (arthur2024processsimulationof pages 17-18). For feeders specifically, DEM was used to examine residence-time distribution, discharge, refill mixing, and material tracking, with perfect-mixing models adequate only at fill levels ≤30% (arthur2024processsimulationof pages 17-18). Rocky and PFC are also established commercial codes that support Hertz-Mindlin with JKR or similar adhesion models, with Rocky offering a GPU solver and realistic particle-shape libraries (fourie2019discreteelementsimulation pages 18-21).

**2.4 DEM for Metal Powder Cohesion (AM-Focused)**

Meier et al. (2018) developed a DEM framework specifically for fine metal powders used in additive manufacturing, incorporating frictional contact, rolling resistance, and a regularized van der Waals adhesive pull-off force (meier2019modelingandcharacterization pages 1-2, meier2019modelingandcharacterization pages 2-4). For plasma-atomized Ti-6Al-4V powder (mean diameter 34 µm), they calibrated an effective surface energy of γ₀ = 0.1 mJ/m² by matching simulated and experimental angle-of-repose tests (meier2019modelingandcharacterization pages 14-16, meier2019modelingandcharacterization pages 9-12). This effective value is orders of magnitude lower than theoretical metal surface energies (~1000 mJ/m²) and experimental flat-contact values (30–50 mJ/m²), attributed to surface roughness, contamination, and oxidation (meier2019modelingandcharacterization pages 14-16, meier2019modelingandcharacterization pages 6-7). They estimated that 0.1–1 mJ/m² is a plausible effective range for metal AM powders and found that angle of repose is more sensitive to surface energy than to particle stiffness, friction coefficient, or coefficient of restitution (meier2019modelingandcharacterization pages 14-16).

### 3. Coupled CFD-DEM

Coupled CFD-DEM adds gas-phase pressure, permeability, and drag forces to the particle-resolved framework. For screw conveyors, this approach has been applied primarily to pneumatic conveying and aeration-assisted systems rather than slow, dry micro-dosing. Coarse-graining in CFD-DEM must preserve both contact and drag similarity; simply enlarging particles without consistent coarse-graining produces erroneous, overly rapid filling because air drag effects change (goh2026bookofabstracts pages 31-33). No persuasive published precedent was identified for a dry, slowly rotating 3 mm micro-auger using CFD-DEM. The approach would be justified if aeration, gas leakage, or fine-powder entrainment materially affects flow at the operating speeds of 1–30 rpm.

### 4. Continuum Approaches

**4.1 Eulerian Granular CFD**

Continuum frameworks treating powder as a compressible granular fluid have been applied to compression-feed screws, where inlet velocity is prescribed from experimental mass flow rate, density, and fill fraction (fakhrabadi2021flowbehaviorand pages 93-98). The pressure-dependent density model predicts densification through the feeder, with dense plug formation contributing to jamming. However, a 3 mm outlet with 10–100 µm particles provides only tens of particle diameters across the opening, making continuum averaging inadequate for capturing discrete arches, individual avalanches, tap release, afterflow grains, and zero-dose increments.

**4.2 SPH and Material Point Method (MPM)**

No published precedent was identified for SPH or MPM applied specifically to screw feeders or auger powder dosing. While these methods handle large deformation, free surfaces, and elasto-plastic constitutive laws without mesh distortion, the resolution needed near a small outlet erodes their speed advantage, and stochastic particle-scale stoppages remain unresolved.

### 5. Analytical and Reduced-Order Feed-Factor Models

Several reduced-order approaches predict screw-feeder mass flow without particle-level resolution. The basic analytical model treats mass flow as the product of screw conveying volume, powder density, and rotational speed: ṁ = φ·A·P·ρ·ω·C, where φ is the loading coefficient, A is cross-sectional area, P is pitch, ρ is bulk density, ω is speed, and C is an inclination correction (hou2024developmentofa pages 97-101). Bascone et al. (2020) developed a hybrid mechanistic-empirical model combining hopper stress, pressure-dependent powder densification, vortex-related volumetric efficiency, and first-order-plus-dead-time (FOPDT) dynamics: ṁ_level = nPAρ_e(t)η (bascone2020hybridmechanisticempiricalapproach pages 4-8, bascone2020hybridmechanisticempiricalapproach pages 8-11). Fathollahi et al. (2020) introduced a "displacement-feed factor" concept where feed rate equals effective displacement density multiplied by displaced volume per unit time, capturing how powder density evolves during feeding through densification, dilation, and elastic recovery (fathollahi2020performanceevaluationof pages 1-2, fathollahi2020performanceevaluationof pages 4-5). These models evaluate in milliseconds and are ideal for closed-loop control and Bayesian sampling, though they cannot uniquely identify microscopic cohesion or friction parameters.

### 6. Speed-Up Techniques

Three principal speed-up strategies have been documented for DEM of screw feeders:

1. **Coarse-graining**: Original particles of radius R are replaced with larger particles of radius R′ = S_f·R, with interaction laws adjusted to preserve gravitational potential-energy density, kinetic-energy density, and dissipation. Particle count decreases by S_f³ while total mass is conserved. The method produced acceptable agreement for angle of repose but cannot reproduce exact heap shape (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 7-9).

2. **Reduced stiffness/Young's modulus**: McBride and Cleary used stiffness values of 250–1000 N/m (far below physical values) to enable larger time steps, achieving average overlaps of only 0.15–0.17% (mcbride2009aninvestigationand pages 5-6). This is the most common approach for making DEM tractable.

3. **GPU acceleration**: Available in modern commercial codes (Rocky, EDEM) and some research implementations. Quantitative speed-ups for micro-auger applications were not reported in the retrieved literature, but GPU DEM can reduce wall time by 10–100× depending on particle count and hardware.

### 7. Calibration and Inverse Inference

**7.1 Current Practice**

The dominant calibration approach uses design of experiments (DoE) combined with multivariate regression or response surface methods. El-Kassem et al. (2021) used a D-optimal quadratic design varying six DEM parameters across three levels, requiring 40–53 simulation runs per study (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 7-9, elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 9-10). Meier et al. (2018) calibrated surface energy by deterministically matching simulated and experimental angle-of-repose values (meier2019modelingandcharacterization pages 9-12). General DEM literature references Bayesian optimization and Bayesian filtering for parameter calibration, but no feeder-specific Bayesian inference from dosing data was identified in the retrieved record.

**7.2 Opportunity for Simulation-Based Bayesian Calibration**

The "dosing fingerprint" concept—mass per revolution versus tilt and speed, tap-released mass, afterflow time constant, and no-flow fraction—provides a rich, multi-output dataset well suited for simulation-based inference. A practical workflow would train a Gaussian-process or neural-network emulator on a Latin-hypercube DEM design, then use the emulator in approximate Bayesian computation (ABC) or neural posterior estimation to obtain posterior distributions over cohesion, friction, and rolling resistance. The reduced-order feed-factor model could serve as the discrepancy or mean-function layer.

### 8. Connection to Powder-Bed Additive Manufacturing Spreadability

DEM has been extensively applied to simulate powder spreading in powder-bed fusion using LIGGGHTS and other codes (ganesan2025experimentalandnumericala pages 14-19, ganesan2025experimentalandnumerical pages 14-19). Cohesive energy density has been identified as a particularly influential DEM parameter for spreading; if cohesion is omitted, simulations overestimate particle mobility and fail to capture actual powder-bed characteristics (astarita2026powderspreadingand pages 9-12). Spreadability is closely related to flowability, integrating particle-size distribution, morphology, density, surface texture, and cohesion. However, standard flowability tests (Hall/Carney flow, angle of repose, shear cell) do not fully reproduce the constrained geometry of layer deposition and should be treated as indicators rather than direct predictors of spreading performance (astarita2026powderspreadingand pages 9-12, astarita2026powderspreadingand pages 7-9). The same DEM parameters calibrated from auger dosing data—particularly cohesion, friction, and rolling resistance—can be transferred directly to powder-spreading simulations to anticipate spreadability.

### 12. Key Findings and Recommendations

1. **DEM with cohesion is the gold standard** for this application. The existing LIGGGHTS model with Hertz-Mindlin, SJKR cohesion, and EPSD2 rolling friction already reproduces the 105 mg/rev for salt with no fitted parameters, providing an unusually strong validation anchor.

2. **No published study has used simulation-based Bayesian calibration to infer powder properties from feeder/dosing data.** This represents a clear research gap. Current calibration relies on DoE + regression or deterministic angle-of-repose matching (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 7-9, meier2019modelingandcharacterization pages 9-12).

3. **Coarse-graining is the primary speed-up strategy** for reducing the 26–30 CPU-min/rev cost, but care is needed: coarse particles must remain small relative to the 3 mm outlet to preserve arching and intermittency statistics (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 7-9).

4. **Analytical feed-factor models** provide an essential fast companion for real-time control and Bayesian sampling. The hybrid mechanistic-empirical FOPDT model of Bascone et al. (2020) combines screw geometry, pressure-dependent densification, vortex efficiency, and dynamics (bascone2020hybridmechanisticempiricalapproach pages 4-8, bascone2020hybridmechanisticempiricalapproach pages 8-11).

5. **For AM spreadability prediction**, the same DEM cohesion and friction parameters calibrated from dosing data can be transferred to powder-spreading simulations. Effective surface energy of ~0.1 mJ/m² for Ti-6Al-4V (meier2019modelingandcharacterization pages 14-16) and cohesion energy density of 30–70 kJ/m³ for cohesive lactose (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 9-10) provide transferable prior ranges.

6. **Continuum methods (Eulerian CFD, SPH, MPM) are not recommended** as primary models for this application due to insufficient resolution at the 3 mm outlet scale and inability to resolve stochastic dose-to-dose events.

7. **Coupled CFD-DEM** should be reserved as an escalation model only when dry DEM leaves systematic residuals correlated with speed, powder permeability, or air displacement effects.
