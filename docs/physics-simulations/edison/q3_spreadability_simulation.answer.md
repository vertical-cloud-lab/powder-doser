# Physics-based prediction of powder-bed spreadability

**Bottom line.** Particle-resolved discrete-element modelling (DEM) is the best-supported way to predict *how a specified powder, recoater and operating condition* produce a bed. It can resolve coverage gaps, local packing, roughness and size segregation that a bulk-flow number cannot. Its predictions still require independent spreading measurements: fitting a powder to a doser, shear cell or consolidation test does not establish that its contact parameters transfer to a thin, rapidly sheared layer. A published Bayesian-optimization study reproduced consolidation yet produced an excessively cohesive bed when its parameters were transferred to spreading. (fouda2019ademstudy pages 1-2, katagiri2025bayesianoptimizationbasedparameter pages 1-2, katagiri2025bayesianoptimizationbasedparameter pages 14-15)

## Models, outputs and validation

In **blade or rake DEM**, particles move through a prescribed clearance under gravity, particle–particle and particle–wall forces. In **roller DEM**, traverse speed, rotation, roller diameter and gap additionally control compaction, jamming and shear. Common choices are Hertz–Mindlin normal/tangential contact, Coulomb sliding, a rolling-resistance torque and an adhesive law; published spreading models use either JKR adhesion or simplified JKR (SJKR). These are *different parameterizations*: an SJKR cohesion-energy-density value should not be interpreted as a JKR surface energy or an intrinsic material constant. Spheres efficiently represent near-spherical atomized alloys; bonded multisphere clumps better represent irregular or satellite-bearing particles at greater cost. Fouda and Bayly’s idealized blade study isolates shear dilation before and within the gap and inertial dilation after deposition: increasing blade speed decreased packing, whereas increasing the gap raised it. Roller studies likewise found layer thickness and traverse speed consequential, but their conclusions depend on the powder and recoater geometry. (fouda2019ademstudy pages 1-2, zhang2020discreteelementsimulation pages 3-5, angelidakis2025demsimulationof pages 1-4, brika2023anovelapparatus pages 3-5)

For comparison across simulations, define **local packing fraction** as solid particle volume divided by a *specified sampling volume*, **void fraction** as one minus that fraction, and **areal loading** as deposited mass divided by sampled area. Also report deposited height and its distribution, substrate **coverage** or uncovered-area fraction from registered images, surface-height roughness such as *Sa*, and species-resolved particle-size/composition maps along and across spreading. These observables are not interchangeable: a smooth surface does not establish a dense interior. Include the sampling depth, treatment of particles crossing its boundary, edge exclusions and number of layers with every reported packing value. Lee et al. assessed height, packing, roughness and size redistribution; binder-jet experiments demonstrate why directional segregation and local density must also be measured. (lee2020effectofparticle pages 1-2, lee2020effectofparticle pages 9-11, brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 9-10, shahed2025powderspreadingbehavior pages 10-11)

The following comparison separates direct experimental agreement from simulation-to-simulation benchmarking and unvalidated model possibilities. (lee2020effectofparticle pages 9-11, haapa2023validationofpowder pages 7-9, qabani2026discreteelementanalysis pages 7-9)

**The strongest numerical validation found illustrates a measurement hazard.** In an Alloy 718 LPBF open-cup experiment, five samples gave **52.4% mean packing fraction**, with **0.20 percentage-point standard deviation**. A four-layer DEM result was **52%** using a corresponding central sampling region. Merely changing the simulated sampling-volume definition yielded **45%, 52% or 58%**; sampling the entire bed rather than its stable central region also changed the answer. Thus, agreement to within a percentage point is meaningful only when experimental and simulated volumes are constructed alike. The cropped density-window comparison makes this dependence visible. Lee et al. independently reported recoated-bed height within **1%** of experiment, alongside qualitative agreement in pile motion, rather than a comparably precise experimental confirmation of every simulated packing or segregation output. (haapa2023validationofpowder pages 5-7, lee2020effectofparticle pages 9-11, haapa2023validationofpowder pages 7-9, haapa2023validationofpowder media 7454531e)

**Across processes.** Binder-jetting DEM uses the same dry recoating mechanics but particularly needs spatial packing and composition because these determine binder access and green-part heterogeneity. In a 5/20 µm alumina mixture, simulated and observed segregation both placed more fines early in the spreading direction and coarser particles later; the measured bed-packing decline along that direction was **7.65%**, but a point-by-point numerical DEM error was not established. For electron-beam powder-bed fusion, a study of reused feedstock simulated rake coverage and packing for spherical and irregular multisphere particles; it found poorly covered thin layers and shape-dependent jamming. Its recoating agreement was not reported as a numerical error. EBM also warrants process-specific temperature and powder-state validation rather than assuming ambient LPBF parameters transfer. (shahed2025powderspreadingbehavior pages 10-11, kelley2024investigationsofparticleprocesspart pages 15-17)

**Coupling and acceleration have narrower evidence.** A metal binder-jet study first made a 17-4PH bed with LIGGGHTS DEM, then passed fixed particle geometry to a finite-volume, volume-of-fluid simulation of liquid-binder wetting and penetration, checking the workflow against single-layer printing experiments. This is *sequential DEM → CFD*, not a demonstrated two-way gas–particle CFD–DEM predictor of recoating. Two-way CFD–DEM is appropriate when carrier-gas drag or gas-driven entrainment matters; liquid infiltration requires wetting and capillarity, not merely a gas-drag correction. Neither should be added to an ambient dry-recoating model without an experimentally motivated fluid effect. (sang2026numericalinvestigationof pages 3-5)

Coarse-grained DEM reduces particle count but changes the ratios among gravity, cohesion, contact forces and fluid drag unless its scaling rules preserve them. An industrial **shoe-filling**, *not AM recoating*, DEM–CFD example retained macroscopic behavior with a calibrated coarse-grained model, whereas simply tripling particle diameter made filling faster because air drag changed. Geometry reduction without enlarging particles is a different compromise: Sani et al. retained the real alloy size distribution in a rotating-drum calibration, reduced drum radius fivefold and adjusted speed to preserve Froude number. They explicitly did **not** establish that a drum-calibrated response predicts recoating. Continuum granular-flow, finite-volume or material-point formulations can represent averaged stresses, solids fraction and a moving free surface at larger scale; in the retrieved studies, however, there was **no independently quantified validation of a continuum recoater model** for fine-layer coverage gaps, roughness and segregation. These approaches are candidates for larger, well-resolved regions or hybrids, not a demonstrated replacement for particle-resolved thin-layer DEM. (goh2026bookofabstracts pages 31-33, sani2026efficientdemcalibration pages 2-4, fouda2019ademstudy pages 1-2, qureshi2023developmentofa pages 238-243)

## Do conventional flow tests predict spreadability?

**Not reliably as universal rankings.** Hall/Carney discharge interrogates an orifice and can return *no flow* for powder that a recoater can spread; angle of repose depends on its formation protocol; Hausner ratio reflects tapping rather than the weak confinement and shearing of recoating. Shear-cell flow function, *ffc*, depends on consolidation stress and usefully constrains yield behavior, but a value measured under substantial consolidation is not itself a prediction of a thin layer. FT4 blade energy and rotating-drum avalanching probe more dynamic states, yet their ability to rank powders remains conditional on test protocol, material and spreader. Use B213/B964, B212/B527, D7891/D6773, repose, sieving/laser diffraction and loss on drying as **independent characterization**, not interchangeable measures of spreading quality. For D6773 or FT4 shear, retain the full stress-dependent yield locus and wall-friction information where possible rather than reducing everything to one *ffc*. (spierings2016powderflowabilitycharacterisation pages 3-4, talebi2021characterisationofspreadability pages 28-33, brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 9-10, zhang2020discreteelementsimulation pages 3-5)

Two experiments show the distinction particularly clearly. Spierings et al. found that the Hausner ratio failed to track their **optical powder-flowability classification**, whereas a fitted combination of drum avalanche/surface descriptors achieved **R² = 0.94** against *that optical rating*. This is **not** an R² for measured recoated-layer packing or roughness. In a later **27-condition, 405-layer** comparison of 316L, Alloy 718 and AlSi10Mg across blade, roller and hopper recoaters at 50, 150 and 250 mm/s, Brocksieper et al. found significant powder × recoater × speed interactions in measured layer *Sa*. Alloy 718 did **not flow in the Hall funnel** yet could make blade/roller layers as smooth as, or smoother than, the more freely flowing 316L; AlSi10Mg gave the roughest layers, a ranking most conventional flow results did not anticipate. FT4 **specific energy** indicated its poorer behavior. Drum avalanche and dynamic angles did **not** correlate with observed application behavior in that study, although speed-dependent drum cohesive-index trends resembled **blade** roughness trends for powders already characterized. The authors explicitly did **not** establish prediction for an unknown powder or for roller/hopper systems. They measured surface roughness, **not bed density**. (spierings2016powderflowabilitycharacterisation pages 8-10, brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 7-9, brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 9-10, brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 4-5)

Accordingly, evaluate each conventional test against an actual, held-out spreading endpoint—coverage, *Sa*, areal mass or packing—**separately for each recoater and speed regime**. A significant within-alloy or within-machine association is useful screening evidence, not a transferable material law. (brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 9-10, spierings2016powderflowabilitycharacterisation pages 3-4)

## Open implementations and reproducibility

- **LIGGGHTS-PUBLIC 3.8.0** is directly relevant to the existing doser: Katagiri et al. publish a model specification using Hertz–Mindlin, `sjkr2` cohesion and `cdt` rolling resistance; Sang et al. use open-source LIGGGHTS for binder-jet bed generation. The published parameter table and equations are useful *starting descriptions*, **not a verified downloadable runnable recoater input deck** or transferable parameters. In particular, changing from constant-directional-torque rolling resistance to another rolling law requires recalibration. (katagiri2025bayesianoptimizationbasedparameter pages 2-4, sang2026numericalinvestigationof pages 3-5, qureshi2023developmentofa pages 238-243)
- **LAMMPS** was used for Haeri’s blade-profile and multisphere recoating simulations; **MercuryDPM** and **YADE** are documented open granular-simulation packages that can serve as alternative modelling bases. The evidence retrieved does not establish a specific maintained, ready-to-run AM recoating example for each. **EDEM, ANSYS Fluent and FLOW-3D** appear in relevant studies but should not be represented as open-source substitutes. Check the version, license and implemented adhesion/rolling-law definitions before porting any deck. (brika2023anovelapparatus pages 3-5, qureshi2023developmentofa pages 238-243, sang2026numericalinvestigationof pages 3-5, haapa2023simulationofmetalb pages 89-94)

## Recommended doser-to-spreader calibration experiment

1. **Separate measured particle properties from inferred effective contacts.** For each powder, measure particle skeletal density, number/volume size distributions, morphology, water content and controlled relative humidity; record PLA/PETG surface condition and powder history. Set DEM size and density from these measurements. Infer *effective model-specific* particle–particle and particle–wall adhesion, sliding and rolling parameters, rather than claiming that a uniquely inferred `sjkr2` coefficient is intrinsic cohesion. The need to account for shape, adhesion and particle-size distribution in packing has experimental DEM support. (angelidakis2025demsimulationof pages 8-11, angelidakis2025demsimulationof pages 1-4, zhang2020discreteelementsimulation pages 3-5)
2. **Calibrate against several distinct features, not salt throughput alone.** Use replicated mass/revolution versus tilt and rpm, per-tap released mass, stop-afterflow curves and zero-flow increments with their uncertainty; include fill level, initialization and wall/material state as inputs. A practical *proposed* likelihood uses a continuous error model for mass and afterflow, a Bernoulli/binomial model for no-flow events, and a separate tap-response component. Treat cohesion–rolling-friction and wall-friction–packing trade-offs as identifiability questions. The observed **105 mg/rev** salt match without fitted parameters is an excellent forward check, but cannot identify the contact parameters of the other 12 powders. A published consolidation-only calibration provides a concrete warning about transfer to shear-dominated spreading. (katagiri2025bayesianoptimizationbasedparameter pages 1-2, katagiri2025bayesianoptimizationbasedparameter pages 11-14, katagiri2025bayesianoptimizationbasedparameter pages 14-15)
3. **Make posterior inference computationally feasible, and distinguish it from Bayesian optimization.** At **26–30 CPU-min per revolution**, 100 full-revolution evaluations cost approximately **43–50 CPU-hours**, before replication or multiple operating points. First screen parameters with a reduced, demonstrably representative outlet or experimental design; fit a multi-output emulator with predictive uncertainty, adaptively acquire additional DEM runs, and then sample a posterior that includes measurement noise and simulator discrepancy. Check the posterior against reserved tilt/speed/tap conditions and direct shear/density measurements. Bayesian *optimization* of a squared-error objective, as published for consolidation, finds candidate point estimates; it does not by itself deliver posterior uncertainty. Geometry-downscaled drum calibration may save cost, but particle enlargement or altered tool-gap/particle-size ratio must be independently tested before using it for the approximately 3 mm outlet or micron-scale recoater gap. (katagiri2025bayesianoptimizationbasedparameter pages 4-6, katagiri2025bayesianoptimizationbasedparameter pages 2-4, katagiri2025bayesianoptimizationbasedparameter pages 14-15, sani2026efficientdemcalibration pages 8-10, sani2026efficientdemcalibration pages 2-4)
4. **Test transfer in a separate spreading rig.** For gas-atomized AlSi10Mg and Al 4047 first, measure blade and roller layers at several clearances and traverse speeds under controlled humidity. Register optical coverage and profilometric *Sa* with weighed areal loading and a trapped-volume/open-cup packing measurement; map PSD or composition along the travel direction for mixtures. Compare *posterior-predicted distributions* with held-out layers, not just a calibrated mean. Extend to irregular silicon grades and deliberately different sizes only after checking shape and cohesion-model adequacy. A doser fingerprint may be a useful prior or screening feature, but **its predictive value for recoating remains to be demonstrated**, especially because the doser’s tapping and wall contacts differ from blade/roller shear. (haapa2023validationofpowder pages 7-9, shahed2025powderspreadingbehavior pages 10-11, brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 9-10, katagiri2025bayesianoptimizationbasedparameter pages 1-2)
