# End-to-End Workflow: Physics-Based Simulation for Powder Property Inference and Doser Calibration

## 1. Overview

## 2. Step 1 — DEM Parameter–Property Mapping

The existing LIGGGHTS model uses Hertz–Mindlin contact with SJKR cohesion and constant-directional-torque rolling friction. Four primary DEM parameters map onto measurable powder behaviour and dosing-fingerprint features:

**Sliding friction (μ_s).** This represents tangential resistance between particles and is the primary determinant of the internal friction angle measured in shear-cell tests and the static angle of repose. El-Kassem et al. studied six DEM inputs (including static friction, rolling friction, restitution, particle size, Young's modulus, and cohesion energy density) and their effects on angle of repose, porosity, mass flow rate, and kinetic energy using a D-optimal design and multivariate regression (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 1-2, elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 7-9). In the dosing fingerprint, μ_s primarily affects how mass per revolution varies with tilt angle and the afterflow time constant.

**Rolling friction (μ_r).** This serves as a surrogate for particle shape, surface roughness, and interlocking resistance. El-Kassem et al. found that rolling friction has a "substantial" effect on angle of repose for cohesive powders, while static friction and particle size play minor roles (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 1-2). Porosity (and therefore Hausner ratio) is strongly affected by both static and rolling friction. In the auger, μ_r influences the fraction of increments with no flow and the tap-released mass, since particles with high rolling resistance resist gravitational rearrangement.

**Effective surface energy (γ) or cohesion energy density (C₀).** For the JKR model, Meier et al. calibrated Ti-6Al-4V powder by matching numerical and experimental funnel-test angle of repose (41°), obtaining γ = 0.1 mJ/m² — substantially below flat-metal contact values of 30–50 mJ/m² due to surface roughness and oxide contamination (meier2019modelingandcharacterization pages 1-2, meier2019modelingandcharacterization pages 9-12). The corresponding granular Bond number, defined as Bo = 36γ/(ρD₅₀²g), was Bo = 54.5 for this calibration (roy2024structuralfluctuationsin pages 3-6). For the SJKR model used in the existing LIGGGHTS setup, the cohesion energy density C₀ plays an analogous role; Rößler and Katterfeld calibrated C₀ alongside friction for wet sand using cylinder-lift angle-of-repose tests (roessler2019demparametercalibration pages 1-2). Artoni et al. varied surface energy over Bond numbers from 0 to 200 and observed distinct flow regime transitions in rotating drums (artoni2026discretenumericalanalysis pages 5-9). In the dosing fingerprint, cohesion most strongly affects the no-flow fraction (arching probability), the tap-released mass, afterflow persistence, and the low-speed mass per revolution.

**Moisture/capillary term.** A humidity-dependent multiplier on cohesion can capture the before/after-humidity shift in dosing behaviour. Coetzee and Scheffler reviewed calibration of capillary-bridge and moisture-dependent contact models, noting that cohesion energy density for wet materials must be separately determined (coetzee2022reviewthecalibration pages 32-34, coetzee2022reviewthecalibration pages 1-2).

## 3. Step 2 — Simulation Design and Emulator Construction

**Design of experiments.** With four free DEM parameters and a simulation cost of approximately 26–30 CPU-minutes per revolution (approximately 2 CPU-hours per full fingerprint evaluation at multiple tilt/speed conditions), a 48-point Latin hypercube design costs approximately 96 CPU-hours — within the stated budget. Published particle-simulation calibration campaigns typically use 60–184 simulations for Gaussian process surrogate construction. Gel et al. used 60 simulations for an initial deterministic emulator and 120–184 for a robust Bayesian calibration campaign with six parameters (gel2023comparisonofdeterministic pages 12-14, gel2023comparisonofdeterministic pages 14-16). The ACCES framework calibrated four DEM parameters with 144 simulations using CMA-ES evolutionary optimization (nicusan2025accesnoninvasivesimulation pages 5-6, nicusan2025accesnoninvasivesimulation pages 27-28). El-Kassem et al. used a quadratic D-optimal design for six DEM inputs and five output responses (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 1-2). A budget of approximately 12 simulations per parameter is a reasonable starting point, with adaptive augmentation if needed.

**Surrogate model choice.** A multi-output Gaussian process (GP) is recommended because it provides posterior uncertainty estimates required for downstream Bayesian calibration, naturally handles the moderate dimensionality, and has been widely applied in DEM contexts (gel2023comparisonofdeterministic pages 12-14). Hadi et al. demonstrated that Gaussian process regression efficiently builds accurate surrogates on very small DEM datasets and that transfer learning with as few as 1–5 samples from an unseen scenario improves surrogate R² by 17–47% (nicusan2025accesnoninvasivesimulation pages 4-5). The emulator should jointly predict mass per revolution, tap-released mass, afterflow time constant, and no-flow fraction across the tested tilt/speed grid. Leave-one-out cross-validation with a threshold of R² > 0.85 and calibrated predictive intervals should be required before proceeding to calibration.

## 4. Step 3 — Bayesian Calibration with Model Discrepancy

**Kennedy–O'Hagan framework.** The foundational formulation decomposes the observed dosing fingerprint as y(x) = η(x, θ) + δ(x) + ε, where η is the GP-emulated DEM prediction at operating condition x and DEM parameters θ, δ(x) is a GP representing systematic model discrepancy (e.g., from auger geometry simplifications, wall compliance, or electrostatic effects not in the DEM), and ε is measurement noise (gel2023comparisonofdeterministic pages 6-8, gel2023comparisonofdeterministic pages 8-10). Prior distributions for θ come from literature ranges and direct measurements where available. Bayesian posterior inference is performed via MCMC, using the cheap emulator rather than the expensive simulator at each likelihood evaluation (gel2023comparisonofdeterministic pages 18-19).

**Identifiability.** The central challenge is confounding: both calibration parameters θ and discrepancy δ can explain mismatches between simulation and experiment, making them difficult to separate without informative priors or sufficient data variety (gel2023comparisonofdeterministic pages 6-8, gel2023comparisonofdeterministic pages 4-6). Gel et al. found that posterior distributions peaking at parameter-space boundaries can signal surrogate error or identifiability failure rather than genuine physical information (gel2023comparisonofdeterministic pages 12-14).

**Synthetic recovery.** Before applying to real data, generate synthetic dosing fingerprints from known DEM parameter values (e.g., the existing validated NaCl case at 105 mg/rev), add realistic noise, and run the full calibration pipeline. The test passes if posterior medians recover the true parameters within 20% for at least 3 of 4 parameters and posterior 90% intervals contain the true values. This is analogous to the analytical-solution-based synthetic calibration performed by Gel et al. (gel2023comparisonofdeterministic pages 23-24). If recovery fails, consider reducing the parameter set, adding operating conditions to the fingerprint, or restricting the discrepancy GP's flexibility via tighter lengthscale priors.

**Practical numbers.** No new DEM simulations are needed after emulator construction. Approximately 15 dosing experiments per powder (spanning 3 tilt angles × 3 speeds, plus 3 tap conditions × 2 replicates) provide the experimental fingerprint for calibration.

## 5. Step 4 — Validation Against Standard Tests and Spreading Predictions

**Predicting standard test outcomes.** Once θ posteriors are obtained, push posterior samples through virtual angle-of-repose simulations and simulated bulk/tapped density packing to predict the independent measurements. Meier et al. demonstrated that surface energy calibrated via angle of repose (γ = 0.1 mJ/m²) successfully predicted powder spreading layer quality: increasing cohesion reduced packing fraction and increased surface roughness, with cohesive forces exceeding gravity by nearly two orders of magnitude for 17 μm Ti-6Al-4V powder (meier2019criticalinfluencesof pages 23-25, meier2019criticalinfluencesof pages 1-2). Lee et al. calibrated Co–Cr powder using a 33° static angle of repose and validated the model against multi-layer spreading packing density, roughness, segregation, and dynamic angle of repose at multiple recoater speeds (lee2020effectofparticle pages 2-5, lee2020effectofparticle pages 1-2).

**Known limitations.** Lupo et al. found that calibrating JKR surface energy and rolling friction against angle of repose versus unconfined yield strength from shear testing produced inconsistent parameter pairs, demonstrating that a single calibration experiment may not constrain all parameters (lupo2021calibrationofdem pages 9-11, lupo2021calibrationofdem pages 2-3). Coetzee and Scheffler's review documented that rolling friction values and cohesion energy densities differed between slump, hopper, and draw-down tests, underscoring the difficulty of obtaining one universal parameter set (coetzee2022reviewthecalibration pages 32-34, coetzee2022reviewthecalibration pages 21-23).

**Spreading prediction.** Roy et al. linked Bond number to spreading-layer metrics: at the calibrated Bo = 54.5, Ti-6Al-4V produced acceptable layers, while increasing Bo toward 200 progressively degraded packing fraction and increased structural heterogeneity (roy2024structuralfluctuationsin pages 3-6). Weissbach et al. used a calibrated γ₀ = 0.08 mJ/m² for 15–45 μm Ti-6Al-4V and evaluated spreading-layer quality via the spatially resolved packing fraction field, targeting mean and standard deviation (weissbach2025spreadingofhighly pages 2-4). Sehhat et al. noted that DEM-predicted flowability and spreadability measures — including powder-bed density, surface roughness, dynamic AoR, coverage, and empty-area fraction — should be verified experimentally (sehhat2021powderspreadingin pages 2-4).

**Validation criteria.** For the feasibility test, require predicted angle of repose within ±5° and predicted Hausner ratio within ±0.10 for at least 2 of 3 test powders. Treat shear-cell ffc and spreading packing fraction as genuine held-out predictions rather than additional calibration targets.

## 6. Step 5 — Simulator-Assisted Bayesian Optimization of Dosing Settings

The simulator can accelerate the Bayesian optimization of the eight dosing controller settings for a new powder through four mechanisms:

**(a) Prior mean function.** Use the calibrated emulator's prediction of mass/rev and afterflow as a function of operating conditions as the GP prior mean in the dosing BO. This is the simplest approach and requires no modification of the Ax/SAASBO pipeline beyond supplying a non-zero mean function.

**(b) Multi-fidelity BO.** Treat the DEM emulator as a low-fidelity (LF) source and physical dosing as high-fidelity (HF). Menon et al. demonstrated that multi-fidelity GP-BO for laser-directed energy deposition improved optimization solution quality compared with single-fidelity BO while using fewer expensive HF evaluations (menon2022multifidelitysurrogatebasedprocess pages 7-9). Foumani et al. showed that multi-fidelity BO can handle heterogeneous errors and locally correlated LF sources (foumani2023ontheeffects pages 7-9). The DEM emulator queries are effectively free after construction, making cost-aware acquisition functions strongly favor LF exploration.

**(c) Physics-based task descriptors in multi-task BO.** The posterior DEM parameters (μ_s, μ_r, γ, and packing) can serve as continuous task descriptors in the multi-task GP already planned with a task-index kernel plus a descriptor kernel. This enriches the fingerprint-based descriptors with physically interpretable coordinates that may capture inter-powder correlations more robustly. Bai et al. surveyed transfer-learning mechanisms for BO and identified surrogate-level transfer using multi-task GPs as a key approach (bai2023transferlearningfor pages 22-24, bai2023transferlearningfor pages 11-13). Wagner et al. demonstrated sample-efficient transfer BO for laser cutting, finding improved configurations within 4–5 iterations when warm-started from source-task optima (wagner2025sampleefficientbayesiantransfer pages 5-6). Tighineanu et al. showed that jointly-trained GP transfer methods (HGP, WSGP) significantly outperform single-task BO when source data are scarce but informative (tighineanu2022transferlearningwith pages 7-9).

**(d) Search-space narrowing.** Simulations can identify tilt/speed regions where arching is certain or afterflow is excessive, allowing the optimizer to exclude these regions and focus physical doses on the productive subspace.

**Expected savings.** The current NaCl calibration uses 42 doses (20 screening + 14 optimizer-selected + 8 validation). Transfer-learning GPR for DEM segregation improved R² by 17% with 1 sample and 47% with 5 samples from an unseen scenario. If the multi-task or multi-fidelity approach provides a similarly warm-started initial model, screening could be reduced from 20 to approximately 5 doses, targeting a total of 20–25 doses for a new powder — a roughly 40–50% reduction. This target should be evaluated against single-task BO and nearest-powder carryover baselines under identical initial conditions and dose budgets.

## 7. Feasibility Test Design

The feasibility test is structured as a gated four-phase pilot that can be stopped at any phase if a criterion fails, preventing wasted effort:

*Table: A gated, scope-matched pilot that tests parameter recoverability, emulator accuracy, external powder-property prediction, and practical savings over simple baselines before committing the manuscript to a simulator-centered claim.*

### Baselines the simulator must beat

1. **Direct statistical mapping.** Regress measured properties (AoR, Hausner ratio) directly on the dosing-fingerprint vector (mass/rev curve, tap mass, afterflow, no-flow fraction) without any simulator. This is a GP or ridge regression trained on the 13 powders already dosed. The simulator adds value only if its property-prediction MAE is ≥20% lower.

2. **Closed-form Beverloo/volumetric model.** For auger dosing, mass per revolution can be approximated as the auger cross-section area times pitch times fill fraction times bulk density. The classical Beverloo equation predicts hopper discharge rate proportional to outlet diameter raised to the power 2.5 and does not include a cohesion term (limhengha2026explainablesurrogatebasedknowledge pages 15-17). This model will likely fail for cohesive or irregular powders, providing a floor that the simulator should substantially exceed.

3. **Single-task BO.** The existing SAASBO/qNEHVI pipeline applied independently to each new powder, without any simulator information. The simulator-assisted approach must reduce the number of physical doses by ≥25% (from 42 to ≤31) without degrading final mass error or median dose time.

4. **Nearest-powder carryover.** Use the optimized settings from the most similar previously dosed powder (by fingerprint Euclidean distance). This is the simplest possible transfer baseline.

### When to drop the simulator from the paper

Drop the DEM simulator from the manuscript if any of the following hold:

- **Phase 0 fails:** Synthetic recovery cannot recover ≥3 of 4 parameters within 20%, even after reducing to 3 parameters. This indicates the dosing fingerprint does not contain enough information to identify the DEM parameters, and the calibration is fundamentally ill-posed.

- **Phase 1 fails:** The GP emulator cannot achieve R² > 0.85 on leave-one-out with 48 + 16 = 64 simulations. This suggests the DEM-to-fingerprint mapping is too noisy, non-smooth, or high-dimensional for the available compute budget.

- **Phase 2 fails:** Predicted AoR and Hausner ratio are outside ±5° and ±0.10 for 2 of 3 powders. This means the calibrated DEM parameters do not generalize to independent tests, and the simulator provides no predictive advantage.

- **Phase 3 fails:** The simulator does not beat all three simple baselines on at least one preregistered primary metric. In this case, the dosing fingerprint alone or the existing multi-task GP approach is sufficient, and the simulator adds complexity without value.

In any failure case, the paper can still report the dosing fingerprint methodology, the Bayesian optimization of controller settings, and the multi-task GP across powders — all of which are publishable contributions independent of the simulator. The simulator attempt becomes a reported negative result that strengthens the paper's credibility.

### Total resource commitment

The feasibility test requires 96 DEM simulations (approximately 192 CPU-hours), 3 powders, approximately 45 dosing experiments, and 6 standard-test sets (AoR and bulk/tap density). With one graduate student and AI agents, this can be completed in approximately 2–3 weeks, leaving ample time before the March 2027 conference deadline.

## 8. Summary of Key Numbers

- **DEM cost:** 26–30 CPU-min/rev, approximately 2 CPU-h per full fingerprint evaluation.
- **Emulator training:** 48 simulations (96 CPU-h), with up to 16 adaptive additions.
- **Published calibration budgets:** 60–184 sims for GP (gel2023comparisonofdeterministic pages 12-14, gel2023comparisonofdeterministic pages 14-16); 144 sims for 4 parameters with ACCES/CMA-ES (nicusan2025accesnoninvasivesimulation pages 5-6, nicusan2025accesnoninvasivesimulation pages 27-28); 34 design points per system with LHS (limhengha2026explainablesurrogatebasedknowledge pages 15-17).
- **Calibration surface energy reference:** γ = 0.1 mJ/m² for Ti-6Al-4V with Bo = 54.5 (roy2024structuralfluctuationsin pages 3-6, meier2019modelingandcharacterization pages 1-2).
- **Transfer learning savings:** 17–47% R² improvement with 1–5 samples from unseen DEM scenarios; 4–5 iterations to find improved settings with warm-started BO (wagner2025sampleefficientbayesiantransfer pages 5-6).
- **Existing doser baseline:** 42 doses for NaCl (20 screening + 14 optimizer + 8 validation), 0.5 g in 78 s median, 1.8 mg error.
- **Target with simulator:** 20–25 doses per new powder (40–50% reduction).
