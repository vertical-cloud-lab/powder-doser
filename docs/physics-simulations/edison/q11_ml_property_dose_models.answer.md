**Forward models (properties → feeder performance).** The most systematic forward-model program is from the Ghent/FDA collaborative group. Bostijn et al. (2019) characterized 15 pharmaceutical powders with 25 descriptors spanning particle-size distribution, flow function (ffc), Hausner ratio, basic flow energy, compressibility, aeration response, and charge density, then built four separate PLS regressions predicting maximum feed factor (FFmax), feed-factor decay, feed-rate relative standard deviation (RSD), and relative error (RE) on a K-Tron MT12 twin-screw feeder; volumetric responses (FFmax, FFdecay) were predicted more successfully than gravimetric variability, while RE was only weakly related to material properties because values clustered near zero (bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 18-22, bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 1-6). Li (2020), extending the Rutgers/FDA framework of Wang et al. (2017), used 30 flow properties from 7–14 materials as inputs to PLSR models predicting refill disturbances (maximum deviation, deviation time, excess mass) on loss-in-weight feeders, validating with leave-one-material-out cross-validation and finding qualitatively successful prediction (li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 78-82, li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 36-40). Conditioned bulk density (cBD) emerged as the single strongest correlate of feed factor in that library, enabling approximate feeder and screw selection once cBD is known (li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 36-40). Wang et al. (2019) built a material library of approximately 20 powders with 30+ flow descriptors reduced via PCA; a reduced set of four tests—compressibility, permeability, and shear-cell measurements at two stresses—produced clustering equivalent to the full set, using about 50 g and three instrument-hours per material (wang2019developmentandapplications pages 19-23, wang2019developmentandapplications pages 10-15). Benedetti built a multivariate weighted PLS (MwPLS) model for a single material across 34 batches, predicting feed-factor profiles from hopper weight and flow-rate setpoint with calibration R² = 0.91 and external-validation R² averaging 0.87 (benedettiUnknownyeardataanalyticsfor pages 80-86). Rezaeizadeh (2026) compared PLS, random forest, Gaussian-process regression, and SVR for feed-factor-profile prediction, finding all models achieved MAPE < 5%, with PLS including material properties yielding RMSECV of 0.118 g versus 0.122 g for RF without material properties—a marginal improvement (rezaeizadeh2026applicationofmachine pages 48-50).

**Inverse models (process signals → powder properties).** Diaz et al. (2023) used 112 pharmaceutical systems (30 APIs, 43 excipients, 40 blends) characterized by dynamic image analysis (D10, D50, D90, aspect ratio, sphericity) and bulk density to classify powders into flow-function-coefficient (FFc) classes (cohesive, easy-flowing, free-flowing). Among six classifiers (MLP, RF, SVM, kNN, naïve Bayes, AdaBoost), MLP achieved the highest single-step ROC-AUC of 0.823 under 10-fold cross-validation, though the middle "easy-flowing" class was classified below 45% correct (diaz2023machinelearningmodels pages 97-102, diaz2023machinelearningapproaches pages 4-5, diaz2023machinelearningapproaches pages 3-4). In the companion thesis, a random-forest model on 103 formulations predicted continuous FFc with MAPE = 0.173 and wall-friction angle with MAE = 1.83°, using SHAP to reveal that API D10, D50, and aspect ratio D10 were the most predictive features (diaz2023machinelearningmodels pages 167-170). Owasit et al. (2025) assembled 410 blends from 9 APIs and 18 excipients, classifying flow regimes (2–5 classes defined by FFc thresholds) with RF, XGBoost, SVM, logistic regression, kNN, and MLP. RF performed best, reaching approximately 85% accuracy for two-class individual components, 94.67% for uncoated blends, and 81.36% on an entirely unseen API (IBU25) for two classes, though accuracy declined to 45.76% for five classes (owasit2025predictingpowderblend pages 17-18, owasit2025predictingpowderblend pages 1-2). SHAP analysis identified dry-coating parameters as the most influential features, followed by particle size and morphology (owasit2025predictingpowderblend pages 1-2).

**PLS as the recommended baseline.** For pharmaceutical material-library modeling, Cao et al. (2024) systematically investigated PLS sample-size requirements using a 45-material library with 27 physical-property descriptors predicting tablet tensile strength. Performance (minimum R² and RMSE) stabilized and remained nearly unchanged as sample size increased from 20 to 45 materials, indicating that approximately 20 diverse materials suffice for dependable PLS prediction (cao2024samplesizerequirements pages 1-2). Below 15 materials, hierarchical sampling based on a tabletability classification index d was more reliable than random sampling, reducing the incidence of low-quality few-shot models (cao2024samplesizerequirements pages 13-14). For the user's 13-material set, PLS is therefore feasible but near the lower limit; expanding to 20–25 materials with deliberately diverse flow behavior would improve stability.

**Handling repeated measurements, censored data, and noisy labels.** Multiple measurements per material (e.g., six single-revolution mass values at each tilt angle) should be treated as repeated measures within a material-level statistical unit, not as independent observations. Material should be the blocking factor in cross-validation (leave-one-material-out or grouped k-fold), as demonstrated by Wang/Li's leave-one-material-out scheme (li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 78-82) and Limhengha et al.'s replicate-aware grouped five-fold cross-validation, which assigns all replicates of a design point to the same fold to prevent leakage (limhengha2026explainablesurrogatebasedknowledge pages 12-13). Censored responses (five powders that barely convey, yielding near-zero mass per revolution) can be handled by Tobit-style censored regression within PLS or by treating the threshold between "no flow" and "flow" as a binary classification layer preceding a continuous regression for flowing materials (a two-step scheme analogous to Diaz et al.'s two-step FFc classifier) (diaz2023machinelearningmodels pages 97-102). Literature-derived property values with wide reported ranges (e.g., angle of repose 6–16°) introduce label noise that can be modeled by Bayesian measurement-error or errors-in-variables extensions, or by treating literature values as informative priors and updating them as measured values become available (fransen2026towardsscientificmachine pages 14-16).

**Validation.** Leave-one-material-out cross-validation is the gold standard for material-level generalization with 10–20 materials (li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 78-82). With more materials, nested cross-validation (inner loop for hyperparameter tuning, outer for performance estimation) is preferred. Prospective tests on genuinely new materials provide the strongest evidence: Bostijn et al. validated on two held-out powders (bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 18-22), and Owasit et al. tested on an entirely unseen API (owasit2025predictingpowderblend pages 17-18).

### 3. Combining Physics-Based and Data-Driven Models

Several strategies for integrating DEM, Beverloo, Jenike, or screw-conveyor models with data-driven approaches have been demonstrated.

**DEM-generated training data.** Desai and Higgs (2019) calibrated a polydispersed DEM model of Ti-6Al-4V powder spreading against rheometer data, then ran approximately 50 virtual spreading simulations varying spreader translation and rotation speeds. The DEM outputs (retained mass, throughput, roughness, porosity) served as noise-free training data for a back-propagation neural network with one hidden layer of 200 sigmoid neurons. The trained surrogate achieved ≥96% accuracy and was used to generate spreading process maps, enabling rapid exploration that would be prohibitively expensive with DEM alone (desai2019spreadingprocessmaps pages 3-5, desai2019spreadingprocessmaps pages 12-14, desai2019spreadingprocessmaps pages 5-8). This approach maps directly onto the user's situation: each auger revolution costs 26–30 CPU-minutes in DEM, so a design of 20–50 DEM simulations spanning friction coefficients and speed could train a surrogate that runs in milliseconds.

**DEM-based calibration with DoE and regression.** El Kassem et al. (2021) used a vertical auger doser as the calibration device, extracting angle of repose, bulk density, and mass flow rate as three experimentally measurable bulk responses. A D-optimal design of experiments varying four DEM input factors (auger speed, particle–particle static and rolling friction, particle–wall static friction) was simulated in LIGGGHTS with Hertz–Mindlin contact and EPSD2 rolling resistance. Multivariate regression analysis built prediction models from only 20 DEM simulations. Superimposing contour plots of the three predicted responses dramatically reduced admissible parameter combinations, yielding an optimized set with maximum deviation of 2.86% for dosing time and mean deviation of 0.87% across 12 bulk responses (kassem2021asemiautomateddem pages 1-2, kassem2021asemiautomateddem pages 5-6, kassem2021asemiautomateddem pages 3-5, kassem2021asemiautomateddem pages 12-13). This workflow directly parallels the user's DEM twin and shows that multiple bulk responses (their dosing fingerprint) can be used jointly to constrain DEM parameters.

**Multi-fidelity and transfer learning.** Kim et al. (2026) introduced Bridged Simulation-Based Inference (Bridged SBI) for cost-efficient Bayesian calibration of particle-based simulators. The method uses inexpensive low-fidelity DEM simulations (coarser particles, fewer particles) to identify a coarse posterior region, then trains a residual correction or transport bridge using a small number of high-fidelity simulations. In real-to-sim experiments calibrating friction and restitution of granular soil from depth images, Bridged SBI achieved KL divergence of 2.79 ± 0.77 at a total budget of N = 7 high-fidelity evaluations, compared with 11.19 ± 6.80 for naïve multi-fidelity SBI and 6.53 ± 3.62 for high-fidelity-only SBI—a 75% and 67% reduction respectively (kim2026bridgedsbicorrecting pages 1-2, kim2026bridgedsbicorrecting pages 6-7, kim2026bridgedsbicorrecting pages 5-6). This approach is directly applicable to the user's scenario: a coarse-grained DEM at 6× particle diameter (as used by Kassem et al.) could serve as the low-fidelity simulator, while the current full-resolution model provides the high-fidelity simulator, with a small number of full-resolution runs used to debias the coarse posterior.

**Surrogate model selection for DEM outputs.** Limhengha and Sudsawat (2026) compared five surrogate families—RSM, ANN, RF, GBM, and GPR—on 34 DEM design points for silo discharge and belt-conveyor responses. They found that the best algorithm is response-specific: ANN achieved R² = 0.964 for silo discharge mass flow rate, while GPR attained R² = 0.976 for belt mass-flow but only 0.384 for silo discharge, and RSM reached R² = 0.999 for belt discharge angle (limhengha2026explainablesurrogatebasedknowledge pages 28-30, limhengha2026explainablesurrogatebasedknowledge pages 13-15). SHAP analysis confirmed physical expectations (outlet width dominated silo flow with 78.6% importance) and the proposed SHAP Asymmetry Ratio offered a diagnostic for algorithm pre-selection (limhengha2026explainablesurrogatebasedknowledge pages 2-4, limhengha2026explainablesurrogatebasedknowledge pages 23-25).

**Physics-informed features.** While granular Bond number (ratio of cohesive to gravitational force) and orifice-to-particle size ratio are well-established dimensionless groups in the powder-flow literature (wang2019developmentandapplications pages 15-19, li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 21-25), and Girnth et al. (2024) specifically advocated formula-based dimensionless DEM parameterization for additive manufacturing, the retrieved studies did not demonstrate a completed workflow using these features as explicit ML inputs for feeder prediction. Incorporating such physics-informed features into the user's PLS or GP models—for example, using the ratio of auger outlet diameter to D50 rather than each separately—would reduce dimensionality and improve out-of-domain extrapolation.

### 4. Dataset Size Requirements and Most Predictive Descriptors

**How many materials are needed.** For PLS models, Cao et al. (2024) found that 20 materials produced stable performance equivalent to 45 materials, and that hierarchical sampling gave more reliable few-shot models when libraries contained fewer than 15 materials (cao2024samplesizerequirements pages 13-14, cao2024samplesizerequirements pages 1-2). For ML classifiers, Owasit et al. (2025) used 89 single-component measurements from 27 distinct materials to train RF classifiers achieving 85% accuracy (owasit2025predictingpowderblend pages 1-2). Bostijn et al. (2019) built useful PLS models from 13 calibration materials plus 2 external (bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 18-22). Collectively, these suggest that 15–20 diverse materials provide a practical lower bound for regression models, while 10–13 may suffice for coarser classification or ranking tasks. The user's 13 powders, spanning more than three decades of mass per revolution, provide substantial diversity but are near the minimum; adding 7–12 materials (metal AM powders, sieved fractions, humidity-conditioned salt) as planned would substantially improve reliability.

**Most predictive descriptors for small feeders.** Across the literature, conditioned bulk density (cBD) emerges as the single strongest correlate of feed factor and mass flow rate in screw feeders (li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 36-40). Beyond this, the most informative descriptor sets include: (i) compressibility at a defined stress, which reflects interparticle cohesion and predicts feed-factor decay at changing hopper levels (wang2019developmentandapplications pages 15-19, li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 21-25); (ii) flow-function coefficient (ffc) from shear-cell testing, the standard cohesion/flowability classifier (wang2019developmentandapplications pages 15-19, diaz2023machinelearningmodels pages 167-170); (iii) particle-size distribution measures D10, D50, and D90, with D10 and D50 identified as the most important SHAP features for FFc prediction (diaz2023machinelearningapproaches pages 4-5, diaz2023machinelearningapproaches pages 1-2); (iv) particle shape (aspect ratio and sphericity), which affects mechanical interlocking and flow through small orifices (diaz2023machinelearningapproaches pages 4-5); (v) permeability, which governs air-entrainment and de-aeration behavior during feeding (wang2019developmentandapplications pages 15-19); (vi) dynamic flow energy (BFE, specific energy, stability index), which reflects flow behavior under active agitation such as screw rotation (bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 18-22, wang2016usingmultivariateanalysis pages 68-73); and (vii) charge density, which was included in the Bostijn et al. descriptor set (bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 18-22). Wall friction angle is important for hopper and die-filling contexts (diaz2023machinelearningmodels pages 167-170) but was not identified as a dominant feed-factor predictor. Moisture was recognized as influential through capillary forces and electrostatic effects (li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 160-162, li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 21-25), though its role was not isolated in the retrieved feeder-specific models.

Wang et al. (2019) demonstrated that for routine flow characterization, a reduced set of four tests—compressibility, permeability, and shear-cell measurements at 3 and 9 kPa—captured flow-classification structure equivalent to the full test battery, consuming approximately 50 g and three instrument-hours per material (wang2019developmentandapplications pages 19-23). This represents a practical minimum measurement set for the user's planned characterization.

**Relevance to additive manufacturing spreadability.** Hall flow time does not correlate with spread-layer density in AM (desai2019spreadingprocessmaps pages 1-3), consistent with the user's observation that Hausner ratio and d50 do not correlate with auger mass per revolution. The angle of repose, which did correlate with the user's dosing data (Spearman −0.84 at 45° tilt), is used in DEM calibration as a standard bulk-flow target (kassem2021asemiautomateddem pages 1-2, kassem2021asemiautomateddem pages 3-5) and is one of the most accessible single descriptors of interparticle friction, though it conflates friction and cohesion effects (kassem2021asemiautomateddem pages 3-5).

### 5. Forward–Inverse Model Consistency and Bayesian Optimization for New Materials

**Three architectures for consistency.** The user asks how a model predicting powder properties from dosing data (inverse) and a model predicting dosing from properties (forward) can be kept consistent. The literature suggests three approaches in order of increasing rigor:

(a) **Two separate regressions** is the simplest and most common approach in practice—one PLS or RF model maps properties to feed factor, another maps dosing behavior to a flowability class. This is how most pharmaceutical studies operate (bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 18-22, diaz2023machinelearningapproaches pages 4-5). The drawback is that the two models can be mutually inconsistent: a material classified as "cohesive" by the inverse model may be predicted to have acceptable feed factor by the forward model.

(b) **Bayesian inversion of a forward model** is the natural architecture for the user's DEM twin. The forward model (DEM or a trained surrogate) maps particle-level properties (friction, cohesion, rolling friction) to observable dosing fingerprints (mass per revolution, tap-freed mass, afterflow). Bayesian inference—using MCMC, sequential Monte Carlo, or simulation-based inference—then inverts the forward model to obtain a posterior distribution over particle properties given observed data. El Kassem et al. (2021) accomplished this via contour-plot intersection from a regression surrogate (kassem2021asemiautomateddem pages 12-13). Kim et al. (2026) demonstrated Bridged SBI for the same task, using neural posterior estimation with a residual correction bridge between low- and high-fidelity DEM (kim2026bridgedsbicorrecting pages 1-2, kim2026bridgedsbicorrecting pages 2-3). Fransen et al. (2025) reviewed Bayesian inference for granular-material simulations, formulating the state–parameter model with process and measurement uncertainties and noting that surrogate models (Gaussian processes, neural networks) are needed to make posterior sampling tractable (fransen2026towardsscientificmachine pages 14-16). This architecture ensures automatic consistency: every property value that the inverse model returns is also the input that the forward model would use to predict the observed behavior.

(c) **A joint latent-variable model** would posit that each powder has a low-dimensional latent representation (e.g., a "flow phenotype") from which both the dosing fingerprint and the standard-test properties are generated. PCA or PLS already implicitly provide such a latent space when applied jointly to dosing and property data. No published study was found that implements a fully Bayesian joint latent-variable model specifically for powder dosing, though the pharmaceutical material-library framework of Wang et al. (2019) uses PCA to place materials in a latent space that simultaneously represents flow behavior, feeder performance, and material similarity (wang2019developmentandapplications pages 19-23, wang2019developmentandapplications pages 10-15).

**Bayesian optimization for feeding/dosing of new materials.** The most advanced published implementation is the Digital Formulator and self-driving Tableting DataFactory of Abbas et al. (2025, 2026). The Digital Formulator uses a forward model—ensembles of 20 deep neural networks predicting tablet porosity and tensile strength from raw-material attributes, formulation composition, and compression pressure—validated by leave-API-out testing. It optimizes formulation to maximize flowability (ffc) while constraining tablet quality, using NSGA-II for in-silico search. The Tableting DataFactory then applies physics-informed Bayesian optimization to refine process parameters, typically converging in fewer than six iterations (abbas2025acceleratedmedicinesdevelopment pages 23-26, abbas2025acceleratedmedicinesdevelopment pages 4-8, abbas2025acceleratedmedicinesdevelopment pages 20-23, abbas2025acceleratedmedicinesdevelopment pages 1-4). The powder-dosing system uses a volumetric dispenser whose chamber volume, vacuum duration, and air pressure are adjusted based on density and particle properties, with real-time weighing to reject doses outside ±5% of target (abbas2026accelerateddrugdevelopment pages 4-4, abbas2026accelerateddrugdevelopment pages 11-12). This platform demonstrates the principle of using a forward model to initialize process settings for a new material and then refining them via closed-loop Bayesian optimization—directly applicable to the user's auger doser.

For the user's system specifically, the recommended workflow would be: (1) measure a compact property set (angle of repose, bulk and tapped density, PSD by sieving or laser diffraction, and moisture) on each of the 13+ powders; (2) build a forward PLS or Gaussian-process model mapping these properties to the dosing fingerprint; (3) for Bayesian inversion, train a surrogate on the existing DEM twin (20–50 parameter combinations, each requiring 26–30 CPU-minutes), then use simulation-based inference or MCMC to obtain posterior distributions over cohesion, friction, and rolling-friction parameters given the observed fingerprint; (4) validate inferred values against measured properties where available; and (5) for a new powder, use the forward model's prediction as the initial setting and prior for Bayesian optimization of auger speed and tilt, closing the loop with the balance feedback already implemented. Multi-fidelity inference via Bridged SBI (kim2026bridgedsbicorrecting pages 1-2) could reduce the DEM budget by using coarse-grained simulations for initial exploration and full-resolution runs only for refinement.
