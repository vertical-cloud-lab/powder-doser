## Query

Context: we run an open-source, 3D-printed (PLA/PETG) auger powder doser for self-driving labs in alloy discovery for additive manufacturing. A small helical auger (1 mL and 10 mL reservoirs, about 3 mm outlet) is turned by a stepper motor at 1-30 rpm, the doser can be tilted 0-30 degrees from horizontal, and a solenoid taps the housing; doses of 0.05-8 g fall onto an analytical balance under closed-loop control. We have dosed 13 powders (NaCl table salt, gas-atomized AlSi10Mg and Al 4047, two silicon grades, rice flour, xanthan gum, carboxymethyl cellulose, BaCl2 and others) and record a 'dosing fingerprint': mass per auger revolution versus tilt and speed, the mass freed by one tap, the afterflow time constant after the auger stops, and the fraction of increments with no flow. A LIGGGHTS DEM model of the auger outlet (Hertz-Mindlin, SJKR cohesion, constant-directional-torque rolling friction) reproduces the measured 105 mg/rev for salt with no fitted parameters but costs 26-30 CPU-minutes per simulated revolution. We want to (i) infer powder properties (cohesion, friction, moisture sensitivity, packing) from dosing data by simulation-based Bayesian calibration, (ii) check the inferred values against standard tests (ASTM D7891 FT4 shear cell, D6773 Schulze ring shear, B213 Hall and B964 Carney flow, B212/B527 apparent and tap density, angle of repose, B214 sieving, B822 laser diffraction, loss on drying), and (iii) anticipate spreadability in powder-bed additive manufacturing. Has anyone used the dosing or discharge response of a small screw or auger feeder or powder doser (mass per revolution as a function of tilt angle and screw speed, flow intermittency, afterflow) together with discrete element simulations to inversely infer powder properties such as cohesion or interparticle friction, that is, used the feeder itself as a powder characterization instrument, and validated the inferred values against shear-cell or other standard flowability measurements?

No. # Can an auger doser serve as a powder-characterization instrument?

**Short answer:** I found close precedents for each *part* of the proposed approach, but **no verified publication that combines all three steps**: infer cohesive and frictional DEM parameters *from the measured response of a small screw doser itself*; independently test those inferences against shear-cell flow properties; and use them to predict powder-bed spreadability. This is a qualified literature finding, not a claim that no such study exists. Two especially pertinent El-Kassem auger-dosing publications were identifiable but their full texts were unavailable for verification. (jonessalkey2023reviewingtheimpact pages 4-6, elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 1-2, bhalode2022multiscalemodelingand pages 71-77, hou2024developmentofa pages 200-207)

## Closest precedents—and the missing step

- **Screw feeding modeled by DEM:** López and colleagues simulated how changing particle adhesion and screw speed affects paracetamol feeding. Greater adhesion reduced mass discharged per unit power; sufficiently strong adhesion caused bridging, incomplete screw-pitch filling, and irregular flow. This demonstrates that feeder output contains information about cohesion, **not** that cohesion was recovered by inverting an experimental dosing fingerprint or checked against an independent shear test. The accessible account describes adhesive *stiffness*, which should not be relabeled as the SJKR cohesion-energy-density parameter. (jonessalkey2023reviewingtheimpact pages 4-6)
- **Inversion followed by feeder simulation:** Bhalode inferred particle sliding friction, rolling friction, restitution, and wall friction from a **separate drawdown experiment** measuring repose angle, shear angle, collected mass, and maximum discharge rate. The resulting DEM analysis related those parameters to a screw feeder’s feed-factor-versus-hopper-weight curve. Crucially, the feeder model used **cohesionless** spheres at fixed screw and impeller speeds; its calibration data were not the feeder response, and independent ring-shear validation was not demonstrated. (bhalode2022multiscalemodelingand pages 45-49, bhalode2022multiscalemodelingand pages 71-77, bhalode2022multiscalemodelingand pages 67-71)
- **Inclined microfeeder plus conventional characterization:** Hou tested a screw microfeeder at an inclination of **30°**, varying screw/agitator settings and measuring flow-rate variation, repeatability, and stability. The powders were also characterized with a shear-cell-based flow-function measurement. This is particularly close experimentally, but the accessible thesis describes equipment optimization rather than DEM inversion of its feeding signals or DEM-to-shear-cell validation. (hou2024developmentofa pages 200-207, hou2024developmentofa pages 1-5, hou2024developmentofa pages 106-108)
- **Other complementary examples:** El-Kassem and colleagues used designed DEM simulations, including SJKR cohesion, to study how contact parameters affect *funnel* discharge, repose angle, and packing and compared calibrated bulk responses with experiments—not a screw-derived inverse characterization. Klinger and colleagues measured shear properties alongside pilot-scale screw-feeder performance in milled biomass, without demonstrating feeder-based DEM inversion. Faqih and colleagues related cohesion-dependent, intermittent *rotating-vessel* flow signals to DEM and hopper behavior, rather than screw dosing or independent shear-cell validation. (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 1-2, elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 5-6, klinger2022multiscaleshearproperties pages 1-2, faqih2006anexperimentalcomputationalapproach pages 8-9, faqih2006anexperimentalcomputationalapproach pages 1-2)

**Positioning:** The defensible novelty is *feeder-as-sensor inverse calibration, using a multistate dosing fingerprint, followed by genuinely held-out bulk-flow and recoating tests*. It is **not** the first DEM screw-feeder study, the first inverse DEM calibration, or the first comparison between feeder performance and conventional powder properties. (jonessalkey2023reviewingtheimpact pages 4-6, bhalode2022multiscalemodelingand pages 71-77, hou2024developmentofa pages 200-207, sani2026efficientdemcalibration pages 8-10)

## A discriminating validation study

1. **Fit only dosing observations.** At controlled reservoir fill, preparation history, print material/surface state, and humidity, obtain replicate mass-per-revolution curves over tilt and speed, no-flow probabilities, tap-released masses, and stop–afterflow traces. Model the balance’s response delay separately from physical afterflow. Fit joint distributions or a likelihood for both continuous mass and intermittent events; mean throughput alone can conceal bridging. Varying fill matters: feeder DEM work finds different contact parameters important at high and low hopper weights. (jonessalkey2023reviewingtheimpact pages 4-6, faqih2006anexperimentalcomputationalapproach pages 8-9, bhalode2022multiscalemodelingand pages 67-71)
2. **Infer an *effective contact-model parameter set*, not intrinsic material constants by fiat.** Treat particle-size distribution and density as measured inputs; estimate particle–particle sliding/rolling friction, particle–PLA/PETG friction or adhesion, and SJKR cohesion with uncertainty. Test parameter identifiability by examining correlations and alternative sets that reproduce the same fingerprint. Drawdown DEM work explicitly illustrates that multiple contact-parameter combinations can give the same bulk response. In SJKR, cohesion energy density enters a **contact-force law**; a shear-cell’s macroscopic cohesion is a **yield-locus quantity**, so numerical equality is neither required nor expected. (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 5-6, bhalode2022multiscalemodelingand pages 41-45, bhalode2022multiscalemodelingand pages 67-71)
3. **Make validation independent.** Hold FT4 shear-cell measurements under ASTM D7891 and Schulze ring-shear measurements under ASTM D6773 out of the fit. Predict their yield loci, bulk cohesion, internal-friction behavior, and flow-function coefficients at *specified consolidation stresses* using the feeder-inferred DEM ensemble. Compare intervals and trends, not just a single powder ranking. FT4 and ring-shear values can differ because conditioning and preshear protocols differ; an eight-powder inter-device study found lower FT4 yield/steady stresses under its protocol. Measure wall friction against the actual printed polymer separately where possible. If shear measurements are used to tune a parameter, report them as **calibration**, not independent validation. (wang2016usingmultivariateanalysis pages 43-47, hou2024developmentofa pages 106-108, shi2018effectofparticle pages 17-18, shi2018effectofparticle pages 5-7)
4. **Use the other standard tests as complementary checks, with their proper scope.** B213/B964 measure Hall/Carney funnel flow; B212/B527 measure apparent/tap density; B214/B822 address sieve/light-scattering particle sizes. Record non-flow through a funnel as an outcome rather than forcing a flow-time number: in a study of cohesive steel powders, jamming made both Hall and Carney flow rates unsuitable for discriminating samples. Density, size distribution, repose angle, and controlled loss-on-drying/humidity experiments help diagnose packing, fines, and moisture effects, but none independently measures a unique DEM cohesion coefficient. (cooke2012propertiesofmetal pages 23-27, cooke2012propertiesofmetal pages 27-28, marchetti2021flowabilityofsteel pages 9-10, ghadiri2020cohesivepowderflow pages 1-2)
5. **Test spreadability directly.** On the alloy powders, use a controlled blade or roller recoating experiment, with specified layer gap, speed, substrate, and atmosphere; hold measured deposited-layer density, spatial coverage/empty patches, and surface roughness out of the feeder fit. Experimental recoating and DEM studies show why this is necessary: cohesion can reduce layer packing and increase roughness, while gap and spreading conditions introduce jamming and defects that feeder throughput does not represent. A recent surrogate-based DEM calibration to *rotating-drum* responses for Inconel 718 and Ti-6Al-4V likewise explicitly leaves transfer to recoating unestablished. (habiba2023powderspreadingmechanism pages 2-4, roy2024structuralfluctuationsin pages 16-18, ghadiri2020cohesivepowderflow pages 5-6, sani2026efficientdemcalibration pages 12-13, whiting2023powderspreadingtestbed pages 9-12)

**Feasibility caveat:** At the stated **26–30 CPU-minutes per simulated revolution**, a full-grid Bayesian inversion would be costly. An initial sensitivity/design-of-experiments screen, a surrogate with simulation-error checks, and sequential simulations focused on parameter combinations that the observed fingerprint can distinguish are better justified than attempting to fit every contact parameter simultaneously. Published DEM calibrations already use response surfaces or surrogates, but success within the calibration device does **not** establish transfer to a shear cell or recoater. (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 1-2, bhalode2022multiscalemodelingand pages 41-45, sani2026efficientdemcalibration pages 1-2, sani2026efficientdemcalibration pages 8-10)

**Limit of this assessment:** The bibliographic search identified El-Kassem and colleagues’ *Experimental study and discrete element simulation of auger dosing of different pharmaceutical powders* (2018, DOI **10.1002/pamm.201800261**) and *A multivariate statistical approach to analyze the impact of material attributes and process parameters on the quality performance of an auger dosing process* (2020, DOI **10.1016/j.jddst.2020.101950**), but their full texts could not be inspected here. Their existence precludes an absolute first-in-literature claim; the available evidence does not establish that either performed the complete feeder-response inversion **and independent shear-cell validation** posed in the question. (elkassem2021amultivariateregression pages 1-2, jonessalkey2023reviewingtheimpact pages 4-6, bascone2020hybridmechanisticempiricalapproach pages 4-8)
