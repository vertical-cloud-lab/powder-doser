# Granular-discharge models for a small cohesive-powder auger

**Recommendation.** Use a calibrated screw-capacity model for mean mass per revolution, coupled to a separate probabilistic outlet-arching, afterflow, and tapping model. Use the existing DEM selectively to constrain that model and investigate mechanisms. Shear-cell-based hopper design is valuable for screening blockage, and continuum simulations can test geometry changes, but none of the cited validations establishes an accurate, transferable prediction of *increment-by-increment* delivery from a tapped, approximately 3-mm outlet at 1–30 rpm. In particular, matching salt at 105 mg/revolution at one operating point does not identify the contact parameters of the other powders. Published percentage errors below refer to their stated test geometries, not this doser. (anand2009predictingdischargedynamics pages 5-7, aschenbrenner2024numericalvalidationof pages 5-9, pardikar2019predictingthecritical pages 8-9)

## Model selection and quantitative evidence

| Model | Inputs and useful prediction | Validation and limitation for this doser |
|---|---|---|
| **Beverloo; cohesion-modified Beverloo** | For a freely draining circular aperture, \(\dot m=C\rho_b\sqrt g\,(D-kd)^{5/2}\): bulk density, aperture diameter, representative grain diameter, and fitted \(C,k\). A wedge/slot has a different geometric exponent. For wet spherical grains, Anand *et al.* fitted the effective-size coefficient \(k=1.9\exp(0.39Bo)\), where \(Bo\) is maximum cohesive force divided by grain weight; increasing cohesion reduces the effective opening. | Anand’s correlation differed by approximately **8% and 10% from the DEM results used to develop it** at two tested outlet-to-grain ratios; these are **not** independent experimental errors. Neither this fit nor ordinary Beverloo resolves motor-driven replenishment, stationary arches, tapping, or afterflow. Apply it only to the gravity-draining part of an *open* outlet, after checking \(D/d\) and flow regime. (eman2021scaledphysicalmodelinga pages 26-30, anand2009predictingdischargedynamics pages 5-7, anand2009predictingdischargedynamics pages 4-5, anand2009predictingdischargedynamics pages 2-3) |
| **Jenike–Johanson arching and hopper design** | Measure the flow function—unconfined yield strength \(f_c\) versus major consolidation stress \(\sigma_1\)—together with wall friction, bulk density, and hopper geometry; compare material strength with the stress available to break an arch to estimate a critical opening. A Johanson cohesion correction to an otherwise free-flowing discharge rate has been reported, but it still needs the stress at the opening. | Best used to classify blockage risk and guide outlet redesign, **not** as a guaranteed mass-per-revolution law. Low-stress flow functions can be curved: extrapolating a high-consolidation shear test toward the weakly consolidated outlet may give a substantially wrong critical width. (ennis2007solidsolidoperationsand pages 19-19, pardikar2019predictingthecritical pages 8-9, ennis2007solidsolidoperationsand pages 16-17) |
| **Janssen stress saturation** | From reservoir width, fill height, bulk density, lateral-stress ratio, and powder–wall friction, estimate how wall support makes vertical stress saturate with depth. Feed the resulting stress estimate into an arch or consolidation calculation. | A *stress closure*, not a discharge-rate prediction. Reservoir depletion, wall material, and tapping can change its assumptions; do not substitute hydrostatic head for measured low-stress strength. (mehos2018designinghoppersbins pages 5-7, pardikar2019predictingthecritical pages 8-9, parker2006acomponentbasedarchitecture pages 1-3) |
| **Screw-conveyor volumetric efficiency; Roberts-type design approaches** | Start with displaced volume per turn, approximately \(V_p=\pi p(D_s^2-D_h^2)/4\) for pitch \(p\), screw diameter \(D_s\), and hub diameter \(D_h\). Then \(m_{\rm rev}\simeq\rho_{b,\mathrm{channel}}V_p\eta\), with *effective* fill/transport efficiency \(\eta\) depending on tilt, speed, clearance, wall friction, pitch, feed availability, and backflow. This capacity must still be coupled to outlet release. | In a much larger screw-conveyor study, transport efficiencies varied substantially with pitch and speed; published experimental trends were compared with DEM, but the cited study does **not** supply a transferable percentage error for a 3-mm outlet. Its simulations were horizontal, largely used spherical particles, and did not establish performance for intermittent microdosing. Owen–Cleary-type DEM studies inform the dependence on screw geometry rather than providing an independently validated universal efficiency for these powders. (aschenbrenner2024numericalvalidationof pages 5-9, aschenbrenner2024numericalvalidationof pages 23-28, aschenbrenner2024numericalvalidationof pages 9-13, aschenbrenner2024numericalvalidationof pages 13-18) |
| **Local \(\mu(I)\); nonlocal granular fluidity** | Local friction depends on inertial number, commonly \(I=d\dot\gamma/\sqrt{P/\rho_s}\); it needs particle size, solid density, pressure, and calibrated friction parameters. Nonlocal models additionally evolve a fluidity field and require a grain-scale cooperativity parameter, boundary conditions, and an arrest/free-separation rule. | Local rheology has difficulty predicting a grain-size-controlled finite-opening arrest. A **two-dimensional, cohesionless** nonlocal MPM study predicted the flow/no-flow transition between \(W/d=1\) and 1.5 for one parameter choice and compared it with previously measured thresholds around 1–1.16; it did **not** validate cohesive auger throughput. At a 3-mm outlet, a continuum approximation also becomes questionable if only a few grains or agglomerates span the opening. (dunatunga2022modellingsiloclogging pages 1-3, dunatunga2022modellingsiloclogging pages 3-5, dunatunga2022modellingsiloclogging pages 12-14, dunatunga2022modellingsiloclogging pages 14-16) |
| **Mohr–Coulomb or Drucker–Prager FEM; MPM** | Represent yield, friction, cohesion, sometimes a compaction cap or stress-dependent flow function; supply density, elastic properties, internal and wall friction, strength versus consolidation, initial packing/stress, contact, and moving-boundary conditions. FEM is useful for a critical opening; MPM accommodates large deformation and separation more naturally. | In a wedge-hopper **critical-width**, not throughput, comparison, Mohr–Coulomb FEM bracketed **18.0–18.5 cm** against **20 cm** measured for polymer, and **8.8–9.4 cm** against **5 cm** for SiC. An assumed low-stress bilinear SiC flow function improved its predicted bracket to **5.5–6.1 cm**. These results show both the value of continuum mechanics and its sensitivity to low-stress characterization; they do not establish mg/revolution accuracy. (pardikar2019predictingthecritical pages 4-5, pardikar2019predictingthecritical pages 7-8, pardikar2019predictingthecritical pages 8-9) |
| **SPH; stochastic cellular/avalanche models** | SPH discretizes a *continuum* into numerical parcels, requiring a chosen granular or yield-stress constitutive law, density/pressure and viscosity or plasticity closures, and wall/contact treatment; its numerical particles are not automatically physical powder grains. Stochastic void-migration/avalanche schemes instead need grain-size and packing descriptions, repose/stability criteria, and calibrated transition or migration rates. | Useful for free surfaces, intermittent-state surrogates, or rapid screening, but a fluid-style SPH calculation without a granular constitutive law cannot predict arching. The open HGD void-migration model reproduces selected silo **velocity-field** features against X-ray measurements of rice; its paper does not establish an error for mass flow through a cohesive auger and identifies missing momentum/energy coupling and compaction physics. (marks2025heterarchicalgranulardynamics pages 7-8, smith2015progressforthe pages 20-23, marks2025heterarchicalgranulardynamics pages 6-7) |

**Code availability is distinct from validated capability.** The HGD authors provide figure-generating source code at `github.com/benjym/HGD`; PySPH is documented as open-source SPH software. Published work also establishes Uintah as an MPM framework and Taichi-based differentiable MPM implementations, including granular Drucker–Prager examples. OpenFOAM granular/continuum solvers and Basilisk are plausible development platforms, but the evidence assembled here does **not** verify a maintained, turnkey cohesive-screw implementation or a measured 3-mm auger mass-flow error for either. The cited critical-width FEM study used **Abaqus**, not an open-source solver; implementing its constitutive and opening procedure in an open FEM code would require independent verification. The nonlocal silo example used a research MPM implementation, not a demonstrated drop-in auger package. (pardikar2019predictingthecritical pages 4-5, dunatunga2022modellingsiloclogging pages 12-14, marks2025heterarchicalgranulardynamics pages 7-8, smith2015progressforthe pages 20-23, hu2019chainqueenarealtime pages 1-2, parker2006acomponentbasedarchitecture pages 1-3, yang2026onthenumerical pages 1-2)

## Calibration and independent tests

1. **Separate transport from release.** Fit a measured channel-capacity term \(\rho_bV_p\eta(\theta,n,h,\mathrm{RH})\) and an outlet-state model. Give each commanded increment an open/blocked state; model flowing increment masses conditionally on that state, a separate tap-triggered release distribution, and a post-stop tail (the observed afterflow time constant). Include fill level, direction of tilt relative to gravity, print material and roughness, humidity, and tap protocol as recorded covariates. A mean-flow-only fit will otherwise mistake blocked increments and their subsequent release for changes in bulk friction. This is a proposed *fit-for-purpose model*, not a published prediction of the doser’s accuracy. (anand2009predictingdischargedynamics pages 5-7, aschenbrenner2024numericalvalidationof pages 5-9, pardikar2019predictingthecritical pages 8-9)

2. **Constrain rather than presume DEM parameters.** Supply measured size distribution and morphology, particle and loose/tapped bulk densities, and wall-contact measurements as inputs or informative priors. Infer a small number of identifiable *effective* quantities—such as cohesion relative to weight, particle/printed-wall friction, rolling resistance, and packing response—using the joint likelihood of mass/revolution, zero-flow frequency, tap mass, and afterflow. Treat humidity-dependent adhesion, batch variability, and simulator discrepancy explicitly. Agreement on one salt rate is a useful forward-model check, not proof that SJKR adhesion, contact friction, or rolling friction has been uniquely recovered. Test posterior predictions on powder, humidity, tilt, speed, and reservoir levels withheld from calibration. Low-stress shear data are especially valuable given the SiC hopper sensitivity to extrapolated strength. (anand2009predictingdischargedynamics pages 2-3, pardikar2019predictingthecritical pages 8-9, ennis2007solidsolidoperationsand pages 16-17, mcglinchey2009bulkpropertycharacterization pages 239-241)

3. **Budget simulation strategically.** At the stated **26–30 CPU-minutes per simulated revolution**, 100 one-revolution runs cost approximately **43–50 CPU-hours**, before equilibration, replication, or longer afterflow runs. Use replicated physical fingerprints and a space-filling subset of DEM cases to train an uncertainty-aware surrogate; add simulations where candidate parameter sets predict distinguishable fingerprints or where model uncertainty is high. Use posterior predictive checks rather than quoting a point-fit error as forecast accuracy. Differentiable MPM is not automatically an easy substitute: a Taichi-based granular parameter-identification benchmark reported that long, contact-rich GPU rollouts could even change a gradient’s sign with accumulation order. (yang2026onthenumerical pages 1-2, yang2026onthenumerical pages 2-3)

4. **Use the tests for different questions.** ASTM **D7891** FT4 shear-cell and **D6773** Schulze ring-shear measurements constrain yield, internal friction, and flow function at *reported consolidation stresses*; measure the printed PLA/PETG wall-friction condition separately. **B213** Hall and **B964** Carney measure standardized funnel flow, with a *no-flow* result retained rather than converted to an arbitrary rate. **B212** apparent and **B527** tap density constrain packing and its sensitivity to agitation; **B214** sieving and **B822** laser diffraction constrain size distributions, supplemented by imaging for irregular shape; controlled loss-on-drying/conditioning tests characterize moisture sensitivity, not particle cohesion directly. Specify the moisture-conditioning and sample-handling history for every comparison. Hall and Carney no-flow results can be poorly repeatable for cohesive powders; shear strength and its low-consolidation curvature are more informative for arching than a single flow time. (gibbons2024metalpowderfeedstock pages 13-14, gibbons2024metalpowderfeedstock pages 12-13, gibbons2024metalpowderfeedstock pages 14-14, ennis2007solidsolidoperationsand pages 16-17, gibbons2024metalpowderfeedstock pages 14-16)

## Transfer to powder-bed spreadability

Treat the dosing fingerprint as a **candidate covariate**, not a substitute for a recoating test. Measure deposited-layer density or coverage, roughness, streaks/voids, and—where relevant—segregation under the actual blade, roller, or other recoater, layer thickness, and speed. In a metal-powder comparison including AlSi10Mg, the relationships between conventional flow indices and layer roughness changed with powder, recoater, and speed; a rotating-drum cohesive index tracked trends for a *known* powder with a blade but did not predict an unknown powder’s spreadability. Another study found that moisturized powders could fail the Hall test while still being characterized by powder-layer applicators, and reported marked AlSi10Mg sensitivity to moisture and oxygen uptake. Thus, evaluate whether the inferred cohesion/packing posteriors improve prediction of **held-out** layer outcomes beyond size, shape, density, and humidity alone. (brocksieper2026investigationofflowability pages 9-10, cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 8-10, cordova2020measuringthespreadability pages 1-2)
