Question: Has anyone built or used a low-cost or open-source automated powder dispenser (gravimetric, auger, vibratory or tapping) to prepare drug or compound stock solutions, i.e. dispensing roughly 1-5 mg of solid into a vial followed by automated solvent addition and mixing, that then feed a downstream nanoliter/picoliter liquid dispenser or a microfluidic / lab-on-a-chip / organ-on-chip drug screening device?

No. ## Short answer

**Yes, closely related workflows have been built, but I did not find a published demonstration satisfying every condition at once:** a **low-cost or open-source** dispenser delivering **1–5 mg of an ordinary drug powder** into a vial, followed by **automated solvent addition and mixing**, with that newly prepared stock feeding a **nanoliter/picoliter liquid dispenser or drug-screening chip**. The strongest precedents establish different portions of that chain; they should not be described as one validated, inexpensive end-to-end system. (wakasawa2008solidstatecompatibilitystudies pages 2-4, wakasawa2008solidstatecompatibilitystudies pages 4-5, butler2014naturalproductlibraries pages 4-5, shinn2019highthroughputscreeningfor pages 4-9, lisowski2026highthroughputsolidmicrosampling pages 9-13)

| Published example | What was demonstrated | Where it falls short of the proposed system |
|---|---|---|
| **Astellas Powdernium/XCM, 2008 — closest match for the 5-mg preparation step.** | A commercial robot dispensed and weighed drug and excipient into glass vials; its companion workstation added solvent, stirred, filtered, diluted and tracked mass. One experiment dispensed **5 mg of an in-house compound into a 2-mL vial**, then used **1–1.5 mL solvent**. | This was a pharmaceutical compatibility/analytical workflow ending in **UPLC**, not acoustic dispensing or chip screening. Vial racks were transferred manually, and the cited 5-mg experiments specify **manual vortex mixing** before storage; it is not a fully unattended example or a low-cost/open-source build. (wakasawa2008solidstatecompatibilitystudies pages 1-2, wakasawa2008solidstatecompatibilitystudies pages 2-4, wakasawa2008solidstatecompatibilitystudies pages 4-5) |
| **Queensland Compound Library, described in 2014 — closest connection to acoustic screening.** | Barcoded samples were tracked from an **automated weighing station**, dissolved in dry DMSO, and subsequently transferred by an **acoustic dispenser** into screening microplates. | The description does not establish a **1–5 mg** dose, identify an inexpensive/open-source weighing mechanism, or explicitly say that **solvent addition and mixing** were automated. The endpoint was screening plates, not an organ-on-chip. (butler2014naturalproductlibraries pages 4-5) |
| **STORMS, 2026 — closest disclosed milligram-scale solid-handling architecture.** | Robotic **vacuum-assisted coring** fills individually weighed, sealable glass capsules. Its milligram coring tool covers approximately **1–10 mg**, and the authors highlight **3–5 mg** portions; calibration and capsule-selection software are publicly referenced. Capsules can subsequently be opened over reaction vessels. | It uses a robot, balance and laser-sealing apparatus; **open software is not evidence of low-cost or entirely open hardware**. The reported work does not demonstrate DMSO stock preparation followed by acoustic dispensing or biological chip screening. It is coring, not an auger, vibratory or tapping feeder. (lisowski2026highthroughputsolidmicrosampling pages 9-13, lisowski2026highthroughputsolidmicrosampling pages 18-21, lisowski2026highthroughputsolidmicrosampling pages 13-15) |
| **Miau Microbalance Autosampler — inexpensive solid-handling precedent.** | A review lists a roughly **US$900**, 3D-printed pick-and-place device that transfers tin capsules and powder for **sub-milligram** analytical samples. | Its documented application is elemental/isotope sample preparation; the reviewed description does **not validate 1–5 mg drug dosing**, solvent addition, or downstream screening. Its original *HardwareX* paper was not available for direct verification in this search. (doloi2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 18-19, doloi2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 19-20) |
| **Dual-arm biomimetic dispenser, 2023 — reusable robot-controlled weighing.** | Published code accompanies an ABB YuMi robot that manipulates spatulas, vials and a balance; its illustrated sequence shows solid delivery into vials. | Its stated **minimum dose is 20 ± 2 mg**: the **2 mg is a tolerance around 20 mg, not evidence of a 2-mg target**. The authors call it relatively inexpensive compared with commercial systems but give no demonstrated 1–5 mg drug-stock-to-screen workflow. (jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 2-3, jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 10-11, jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid media baa5662d) |

**What happens downstream is well established, but usually starts with an already prepared stock.** In an NCATS drug-combination-screening protocol, investigators first *weighed compound into a tube* to make **800 µL of 10 mM DMSO stock**, vortexed and checked dissolution; automation then prepared acoustic source plates and used an ATS-100 dispenser. That is a direct precedent for the liquid-screening half, **not automated initial powder weighing**. Separately, an Echo 550 cell-screening study dispensed **2.5–100 nL** from DMSO source plates. (shinn2019highthroughputscreeningfor pages 4-9, shinn2019highthroughputscreeningfor pages 1-4, grant2009achievingaccuratecompound pages 2-3)

**For a microfluidic endpoint**, a relatively low-cost, 3D-printed, Raspberry-Pi-controlled valve/micromixer has delivered premade camptothecin solutions to cell-culture wells for an IC₅₀ assay. Its demonstrated cells were in a **conventional plate**, not an organ cultured on-chip; powder-to-stock preparation was not automated. A human Liver-Chip study similarly prepared drug stocks from weighed powder or vendor vials and diluted them into perfusion medium, but did not report automated powder dispensing. Its reference to an “ECHO” instrument means an **imaging microscope**, not an Echo acoustic dispenser. (winkler2022automationofcell pages 1-2, winkler2022automationofcell pages 4-5, winkler2022automationofcell media 4dcdf57f, ewart2022performanceassessmentand pages 4-5, ewart2022performanceassessmentand pages 3-4)

**Practical interpretation.** The proposed integration is technically plausible, but its missing demonstration is the *validated interface* between an affordable 1–5 mg powder dispenser and automated, concentration-controlled stock production. Actual dispensed mass—not just the requested mass—should determine solvent volume; dissolution, precipitation after aqueous dilution, and compatibility with the destination assay need checking. For scale, **1 mg of a 500 g/mol compound requires 200 µL solvent for a nominal 10 mM stock**, while 5 mg requires 1 mL; this is a calculation, not a claim that any cited dispenser validated those stocks. The Astellas study illustrates why mass tracking matters: at its 5-mg validation point, mass-corrected recovery was **100.9%** versus **108.1%** without correction. (wakasawa2008solidstatecompatibilitystudies pages 4-5, laplante2021probingthefreestate pages 98-102, gillespie2013theprecipitationof pages 70-76)

**Bottom line:** *Yes* to automated ~5-mg drug dispensing followed by solvent processing, and *yes* to automated weighing followed eventually by acoustic screening **as separate published precedents**. *No verified example found* of their requested **low-cost/open-source, 1–5 mg, fully connected powder-to-stock-to-nanoliter-dispenser or organ-chip implementation**. (wakasawa2008solidstatecompatibilitystudies pages 2-4, wakasawa2008solidstatecompatibilitystudies pages 4-5, butler2014naturalproductlibraries pages 4-5, shinn2019highthroughputscreeningfor pages 4-9, lisowski2026highthroughputsolidmicrosampling pages 9-13)
