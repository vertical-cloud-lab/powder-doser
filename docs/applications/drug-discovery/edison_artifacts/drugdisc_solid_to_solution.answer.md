## The Solid-to-Solution Step in Drug Discovery: From Weighed Powder to Liquid Stock for Nanoliter Dispensing

### 1. Overview of the Compound Management Workflow

In modern drug discovery, the conversion of solid compounds into liquid stocks is the critical upstream bottleneck that feeds all downstream nanoliter and picoliter dispensing technologies. The canonical workflow proceeds as follows: a newly synthesized compound arrives as a dry solid (typically 1–10 mg), is weighed on an analytical balance, dissolved in anhydrous DMSO to produce a concentrated master stock (usually 10 or 20 mM), transferred into 2D-barcoded tubes or polypropylene microplates, and stored frozen under inert atmosphere until needed. From these master stocks, automated liquid handlers or acoustic dispensers produce "assay-ready plates" for high-throughput screening (HTS), cell-based assays, and microfluidic/organ-on-chip drug testing (bhatt2019nextgenerationcompounddelivery pages 2-4, lynch2024highthroughputscreeningto pages 3-4).

### 2. Typical Weighed Masses, Vessels, and Solvents

**Masses.** Published examples indicate that compound weighings for master stock preparation typically fall in the **1–5 mg range**, though 10 mg or more may be weighed when larger volumes of stock are required or when material is plentiful. For example, Gomez-Sanchez et al. (2020) reported using approximately 2 mg of solid to prepare a 1 mM stock in acetonitrile, while the updated workflow used pre-existing 10 mM DMSO stocks (gomezsanchez2020methoddevelopmentand pages 1-2). The Tox21 program dissolved each compound at 20 mM in DMSO but did not publicly report individual per-compound masses (richard2025analyticalqualityevaluation pages 3-4, tice2013improvingthehuman pages 1-2). Sou and Bergström (2018) described sub-milligram-scale dispensing into 96-well plates for automated solubility assays, where an automated solid weighing station dispensed small quantities of drug powder directly into each well (sou2018automatedassaysfor pages 3-5).

**Vessels.** Compounds traverse several vessel types: glass or plastic vials for incoming solids, 2D-barcoded tubes in automated stores (NCATS reports capacity for 289,000 powder vials and 1.5 million solution tubes), 384-well polypropylene mother plates, acoustic-compatible 384-well low-dead-volume (LDV) plates, and 1536-well assay-ready plates (bhatt2019nextgenerationcompounddelivery pages 2-4, lynch2024highthroughputscreeningto pages 3-4, dawes2016compoundtransferby pages 2-3).

**Solvents and target concentrations.** Anhydrous DMSO (≥99.9% purity) is the universal solvent because it dissolves structurally diverse compounds, has low volatility, and is compatible with automated liquid handling and acoustic dispensing (gillespie2013theprecipitationof pages 10-15, richard2025analyticalqualityevaluation pages 3-4). The standard master stock concentration is **10 mM** in pharmaceutical HTS (roberts2016implementationandchallenges pages 2-3, bhatt2019nextgenerationcompounddelivery pages 2-4), while the Tox21 program uses **20 mM** as the default, with poorly soluble compounds prepared at 5–10 mM (richard2025analyticalqualityevaluation pages 3-4, tice2013improvingthehuman pages 1-2). Water or aqueous buffer is substituted only when compounds are insoluble or unstable in DMSO (kulesskiy2016precisioncancermedicine pages 2-3).

**Solvent addition method.** Published descriptions generally indicate **volumetric** DMSO addition: a calculated volume of DMSO is pipetted into a vial or tube containing a recorded mass of solid to achieve a nominal molar concentration. True gravimetric solvent addition (weighing the DMSO to compute density-corrected volume) is not the standard practice in compound management, though it would improve accuracy. The literature rarely reports balance tolerance, delivered-volume uncertainty, or density corrections, meaning stock concentrations often remain nominal rather than analytically verified (gomezsanchez2020methoddevelopmentand pages 1-2, richard2025analyticalqualityevaluation pages 3-4).

### 3. Downstream Nanoliter/Picoliter Dispensing Requirements

The solid-to-solution step exists to feed downstream contactless dispensing platforms, primarily:

**Acoustic droplet ejection (ADE).** The Labcyte/Beckman Echo is the dominant platform. It ejects 2.5 nL droplets from source wells containing 100% DMSO stock. Roberts et al. (2016) described an AstraZeneca workflow where 30 µL of 10 mM stock is loaded into each 384-well source well, serial dilutions are made in DMSO to produce 100 µM, 1 µM, and 10 nM source concentrations, and the Echo transfers 5–120 nL into assay wells to generate 12-point dose-response curves while maintaining final DMSO at 0.1–0.3% (roberts2016implementationandchallenges pages 2-3). Dawes et al. (2016) described a Genentech ultra-HTS system where each compound is transferred at 10–40 nL (nominally 20 nL) from sealed library source plates into 1536-well assay-ready plates (dawes2016compoundtransferby pages 2-3). Kulesskiy et al. (2016) reported that FIMM's cancer drug sensitivity testing uses source stocks at 10 mM, 1 mM, 100 µM, and 10 µM to generate five- or nine-point dose-response curves, with Echo transfers of 2.5–25 nL into 384-well plates (kulesskiy2016precisioncancermedicine pages 2-3, kulesskiy2016precisioncancermedicine pages 3-7).

**Inkjet/piezoelectric dispensers.** The Tecan D300e uses inkjet printing technology to dispense picoliter-to-nanoliter volumes directly from compound stock solutions into microplates for antimicrobial susceptibility testing and other applications (eribol2016screeningapplicationsin pages 7-9).

**Microfluidic/organ-on-chip platforms.** Organ-on-chip and tumor-on-chip devices generally receive compounds from pre-made DMSO stocks (typically 1–10 mM), which are diluted into cell culture media before introduction into microfluidic channels. On-chip concentration gradient generators can produce multi-log dose-response series through diffusion-based mixing. For example, Eribol et al. (2016) described microfluidic gradient generators that produce 23 concentrations spanning five orders of magnitude in under 30 minutes, consuming only nanoliter reagent volumes (eribol2016screeningapplicationsin pages 7-9). These platforms do not independently weigh solid compounds; they depend entirely on upstream stock preparation.

### 4. DMSO Stock Solution Degradation and the Need for Re-Solubilization

Several well-documented problems with DMSO stocks create a recurring need to re-dissolve compounds from the solid state:

**DMSO water uptake.** DMSO is highly hygroscopic, absorbing atmospheric moisture during every exposure (gillespie2013theprecipitationof pages 25-29, sreeramulu2020nmrqualitycontrol pages 1-2). Increasing water content promotes compound precipitation and alters the solvent's properties, making stock concentrations unreliable.

**Freeze-thaw degradation.** Repeated freeze-thaw cycling reduces the fraction of compound remaining in solution, likely through precipitation of the least soluble fraction. Butler et al. (2014) reported that after storage at room temperature, 8% of compound concentrations had significantly decreased after 3 months, 17% after 6 months, and 48% after 1 year (butler2014naturalproductlibraries pages 3-4). Sou and Bergström (2018) confirmed that repeated freeze-thaw handling can reduce compound availability in concentrated stocks (sou2018automatedassaysfor pages 2-3).

**Precipitation and solubility failures.** When DMSO stocks are diluted into aqueous assay media, poorly soluble compounds frequently precipitate, forming nanoparticles that grow and ripen over time (gillespie2013theprecipitationof pages 70-76). This produces false-negative screening results because precipitated material is unavailable to interact with the biological target. Ladisic (2023) documented that precipitated samples accumulate as incompatible with acoustic droplet ejection and must be manually removed from the chemical library (ladisic2023theeffectof pages 19-23). Gillespie (2013) showed that kinetic solubility measurements from DMSO stocks can overestimate thermodynamic solubility because compounds remain transiently supersaturated (gillespie2013theprecipitationof pages 32-35, gillespie2013theprecipitationof pages 29-32).

**Incomplete dissolution.** Some compounds never fully dissolve in DMSO, even at the intended stock concentration. Tox21 reported that certain compounds could only be prepared at 5–10 mM rather than the target 20 mM due to DMSO insolubility (richard2025analyticalqualityevaluation pages 3-4).

These degradation pathways mean that compounds must periodically be **re-solubilized from solid** to restore stock integrity, creating a recurring demand for the solid-to-solution step rather than treating it as a one-time operation.

**Mettler Toledo Quantos** uses interchangeable dosing heads with spiral metal impellers and rotary tapping. Jiang et al. (2023) reported −0.41% mean error (1.80% SD) at a 200 mg target for non-challenging solids, but a 26.9% aggregate failure rate across 13 diverse test solids due to problems with abrasive, hygroscopic, and granular materials (jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 8-10, jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 10-11).

**Chemspeed platforms** (GDU-Pfd, GDU-S SWILE, FLEX Powderdose) use crescent-shaped valves or piston/capillary mechanisms. They can handle sub-milligram to gram-scale dispensing but showed a 26.5% aggregate failure rate and occasional severe over-dispensing errors (67–85% for sugar) alongside near-perfect performance on other materials (jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 7-8, jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 10-10). Nsouli et al. (2025) noted that these systems are constrained by high cost, large physical footprint, and maintenance requirements (nsouli2025advancingorganicchemistry pages 11-11). Tu and Wang (2026) reported that Chemspeed and Mettler Toledo systems require substantial capital investment and laboratory space, and instrument malfunctions can cause extended downtime (tu2026sticktothe pages 1-2, tu2026sticktothe pages 4-5).

**ChemBeads** (developed at AbbVie) circumvent powder-handling difficulties by coating reagents onto free-flowing glass beads at a known weight-to-weight loading ratio (typically 5% w/w). Automated dispensing of beads into 96- or 384-well plates achieved <5% RSD, effectively eliminating problems with bridging, static buildup, and clogging that plague neat-powder dispensing. However, ChemBeads require compound-specific bead preparation, loading calibration, and characterization (tu2026sticktothe pages 1-2, tu2026sticktothe pages 4-5).

**Dual-arm robotic dispensing.** Jiang et al. (2023) demonstrated a biomimetic dual-arm robot that manipulates a spatula above a balance with closed-loop gravimetric feedback. At 200 mg targets, it achieved 0.07% mean error (0.56% SD) for non-challenging solids and handled the widest material range of any tested system, but operated significantly more slowly (381 seconds vs. 31 seconds for Quantos per 200 mg dose) (jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 8-10, jiang2023autonomousbiomimeticsolid pages 1-2).

**General powder-handling bottlenecks.** Across all platforms, the fundamental challenges include electrostatic charging, hygroscopic behavior, variable bulk density, heterogeneous particle size, material adhesion to dispensing components, aerosolization, and cross-contamination between compounds (tu2026sticktothe pages 1-2, nsouli2025advancingorganicchemistry pages 11-11). These properties are inherently compound-specific and unpredictable, which is why manual weighing—despite being slow—remains the fallback for difficult materials.

### 6. Accuracy and Precision Requirements

The accuracy required for the solid-to-solution step is moderate by analytical standards. Because downstream acoustic dispensers produce dose-response curves spanning 3–5 orders of magnitude (typically in half-log or log steps), the biological interpretation of screening data is relatively tolerant of ±10–20% errors in stock concentration. The critical requirement is that the actual concentration be **known** (or at least consistently reproducible) rather than precisely matched to a nominal target. Post-preparation analytical QC by LC-MS, GC-MS, or NMR is therefore essential for high-value compound collections (richard2025analyticalqualityevaluation pages 3-4, lynch2024highthroughputscreeningto pages 3-4, sreeramulu2020nmrqualitycontrol pages 1-2).

For a gravimetric powder doser targeting the 1–5 mg range, achieving ±0.1 mg (i.e., ±2–10% at 1–5 mg) would be adequate for most compound management applications, provided the actual dispensed mass is recorded and used to calculate the correct DMSO volume for the target concentration. This is precisely the "weigh solid, then add calculated solvent volume" paradigm that gravimetric dosing enables.

### 7. Relevance to an Open-Source Gravimetric Doser

An inexpensive, open-source gravimetric powder doser operating in the milligram-to-gram range would directly address the solid-to-solution bottleneck in drug discovery compound management. The target mass range (1–100+ mg) matches the quantities typically weighed for DMSO master stock preparation. The gravimetric feedback loop (load-cell balance in the dosing loop) would provide the recorded mass needed to calculate the correct DMSO volume, potentially enabling more accurate stock concentrations than the typical "weigh approximately, add nominal volume" approach. Key applications would include:

1. **Initial stock preparation** for new compound libraries (1–5 mg weighed, dissolved in DMSO to 10–20 mM)
2. **Re-solubilization** of degraded DMSO stocks from retained solid archives
3. **Solid dispensing for thermodynamic solubility assays** requiring drug powder directly in assay wells (sou2018automatedassaysfor pages 3-5)
4. **Stock preparation for organ-on-chip/microfluidic drug screening**, where researchers typically work with commercial compounds at the bench scale and would benefit from automated weighing before manual dissolution

The open-source, low-cost design would be especially valuable for academic screening centers and microfluidic research groups that cannot justify the $100K+ investment in commercial Chemspeed or Mettler Toledo Quantos systems (tu2026sticktothe pages 1-2, nsouli2025advancingorganicchemistry pages 11-11).
