\section{Introduction}

In alloy discovery by additive manufacturing (AM, the 3D printing of metal parts), each candidate composition is made by blending elemental or pre-alloyed powders,\cite{Li2018Combinatorial,Vecchio2021Throughput,Moorehead2020Throughput,Mooraj2024Additive,Nelaturu2024Multi} so every experiment starts as a set of weighed powders. In our own workflow, dosed powder is melted into new alloy powder by ultrasonic atomization and then printed by laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF). Yet weighing out dry powders is still mostly done by hand, even in self-driving laboratories (SDLs). SDLs pair automated instruments with machine-learning planners that choose the next experiment, and they have sped up discovery in chemistry and materials science by an order of magnitude or more.\cite{Abolhasani2023Rise,Tom2024Self,Szymanski2023Autonomous,Seifrid2022Autonomous} Software for running them,\cite{Fei2024Alabos,Guzik2023Decade} methods for planning experiments,\cite{Baird2026Honegumi} and robots for handling liquids are now mature, but most SDLs either work only with liquids or rely on a person to weigh out powders.\cite{Maffettone2023What,Canty2025Science} Where the powders are the experiment, as in alloy discovery, weighing many different powders accurately and repeatably is the step that limits the whole workflow.

Commercial powder-dosing systems exist and work well, but their cost and size put them out of reach of most academic laboratories. Benchtop gravimetric (weight-based) dispensers such as the METTLER TOLEDO XPR/CHRONECT line, Labman dosing stations, and MTI's AM powder dispensers cost from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand dollars. They are built around pharmaceutical and quality-control work and usually dose one vial at a time from proprietary dosing heads.\cite{Besenhard2015Accuracy,Yang2007Metering} Pharmaceutical manufacturing meters powders at much higher flow rates with loss-in-weight feeders, which weigh the hopper continuously as it empties,\cite{Engisch2012Method,Engisch2015Feedrate,Hanson2018Control} and laboratory studies have dosed milligram quantities with capillaries, vibrating chutes, and augers.\cite{Besenhard2017Micro,Faulhammer2014Dose,Chianrabutra2014Powder,Alsenz2011Powderpicking} None of these is available as an open design that a laboratory can print, build, and modify. Open-source hardware has shown that 3D-printed instruments can reach research-grade performance at 1--10\% of the commercial price and keep improving through community contributions,\cite{Pearce2020Economic,Hohlbein2022Open,Wenzel2023Open,Stirling2022Hardops} and platforms such as Jubilee and low-cost SDL kits have brought that model to laboratory automation.\cite{Vasquez2020Jubilee,Subbaraman2024Duckbot}

This paper describes the first module of such an instrument, a single-channel powder doser built from 3D-printed parts and off-the-shelf electronics for under \$200, plus an analytical balance (Fig.~\ref{fgr:v2doser}). One printed tube is both the powder reservoir and the dosing screw. A stepper motor turns it, two servos tilt it, and a solenoid taps it over a cup on the balance, which closes the loop. A dose can be requested in plain language: a person writes the request in a GitHub comment, and an AI agent passes the target to the doser. Each powder lives in its own auger, filled once and stored loaded, so changing powder means swapping a tube rather than cleaning one.

The doser is also a documented case study of AI-assisted hardware design. It started at a hackathon in April 2026 and was built in the open, in the issues and pull requests of a public repository.\cite{NoteRepository} Its parts were first designed without conventional CAD software such as SolidWorks or Fusion~360. The engineers decided what to build, wrote the specifications, and eventually supplied fully dimensioned drawings. AI agents turned those decisions into geometry: large language model (LLM) coding agents wrote each part as code in the repository, and we also explored a chat-driven CAD tool, Zoo Design Studio. Research on generative CAD has produced large datasets and models,\cite{Wu2021Deepcad,Willis2021Fusion,Seff2021Vitruvion,Xu2024Brepgen} and LLMs can now write CAD code (scripts that CAD software turns into solid models) from written descriptions,\cite{Alrashedy2025Generating,Badagabettu2024Query2cad,Jansen2023Words,Rios2023Text} but published evaluations test benchmark shapes rather than the design of a working instrument from start to finish. Once the AI-modelled doser worked, from July 2026, the engineers redrew in Fusion~360 the parts that were built and tested, to obtain editable models for release, and recorded those sessions too; the agents moved on to running the tests, analysing the data, and assembling the CAD. The doser used for the tests in this paper therefore runs on the engineers' versions of most parts, including the auger and its drive gears. Every prompt, generated part, review comment, and failure is preserved, so we can describe the workflow that worked best, the ways the AI tools failed, and what the approach cost and gained compared with conventional design.

The paper follows the doser from design to use. We describe the doser (Section~\ref{sec:doser}) and how its parts were designed (Section~\ref{sec:design}), then how a dose runs and how the doser is used day to day (Sections~\ref{sec:dose} and \ref{sec:use}). We then test it on 13 powders with very different flow behaviour, first open loop and then closed loop at three target masses (Sections~\ref{sec:openloop} and \ref{sec:closedloop}), and report 116 repeated doses with a revised procedure, the doser's first use in our alloy workflow, and where the current design fails (Sections~\ref{sec:stepped}--\ref{sec:limits}). We close with what it takes to build one (Section~\ref{sec:build}) and the next step, a doser for many powders that needs nobody to swap augers (Section~\ref{sec:future}). A project page collects the figures, videos, build files, and talks (\url{https://vertical-cloud-lab.github.io/powder-doser/}).

\section{Results and discussion}

\subsection{The doser}
\label{sec:doser}

One printed auger tube is both the powder reservoir and the dosing screw, so there is no separate hopper (Fig.~\ref{fgr:v2doser}). Powder is poured in through the open upper end of the tube, which is then closed with a screw-on cap. The tube (25~mm outer diameter, 21~mm bore, 250~mm long) is printed as one piece. Along the third of its length nearest the outlet, a single-start screw (the flight, 10.4~mm pitch) winds around an 8~mm central core. The rest of the tube is plain and holds the powder (Fig.~\ref{fgr:v2doser}c). The whole piece turns together, like an Archimedes screw, rather than as a screw inside a fixed barrel.

The doser moves the tube in three ways. A NEMA-11 stepper motor turns it through a printed 20-tooth pinion and a 44-tooth gear printed on the outside of the tube, a 2.2:1 reduction. Two servos tilt it: the tube rides on a hinged plate that the servos turn through a 2:1 gear, so 45$^\circ$ of tilt is 90$^\circ$ at the servo. The hinge axis runs about 12~mm behind the outlet, so the dose lands in nearly the same place at every angle: the tube can be parked horizontally, where nothing flows, and tipped up to dispense. The test protocols used tilts of 0--45$^\circ$, but the servos can tilt the tube further: the repeated doses of Section~\ref{sec:stepped} started at 60$^\circ$, and one attempt to clear a clog went to 75$^\circ$. A push--pull solenoid taps the tube through a printed collar to shake cohesive powders loose. A small eccentric-rotating-mass (ERM) vibration motor was also planned, but its driver never worked (Section~\ref{sec:openloop}), so the doser as tested relies on rotation, tilt, and tapping. An analytical balance (0.1~mg readability) weighs the collection cup and reports to a \$6 Raspberry Pi Pico~W microcontroller, which runs the dosing procedure (Section~\ref{sec:dose}). The parts cost under \$200 for a single channel, excluding the balance, roughly two orders of magnitude below a commercial gravimetric dosing head; Section~\ref{sec:build} gives the bill of materials and the build files.

\subsection{Engineers design, AI agents model}
\label{sec:design}

AI agents modelled the first parts from text, sketches, and dimensioned drawings. From July 2026 the engineers redrew in conventional CAD the parts that were built and tested, and the agents moved on to running the tests, analysing the data, and assembling the CAD. Fig.~\ref{fgr:v2timeline} places the key designs of these seven months on one date axis, with the AI-modelled designs above it and the engineers' work below, and shows when each agent and model was in use. Every step is also recorded in a design log in the repository, which had 183 entries by mid-November 2026.

The project began at a hackathon in April 2026 as a hand-sketched mechanical scoop (a ``powder excavator'') meant to ride on an existing computer-controlled (CNC) gantry. An AI-assisted survey of small-scale dosing mechanisms\cite{Besenhard2017Micro,Faulhammer2014Dose} produced eight candidates, among them a tap sieve, a metering wheel, a capillary wiper, and an auger. We chose the auger for its dose resolution, its printability, and its tolerance of different powders, in line with its wide use in commercial micro-feeders.\cite{Yang2007Metering,Besenhard2015Accuracy} The mechanical design then went through four versions in its first six weeks. Before the second version, the team printed four outlet geometries for the auger (SI Fig.~\siref{fgr:nozzles}). The second version packaged the auger, frame, drive, and actuators as a self-contained channel, the repeating unit of a future multi-powder doser. The third version kept the mechanism and changed how we asked for the parts: instead of asking the AI tools for the whole module at once, we specified each part separately (auger, geared auger, bracket, tap collar, mounting plate, baseplate, and servo hinge). The AI tools modelled each part, and the engineers reviewed and printed it. The fourth version assembled these parts on the hinged baseplate with servo tilt. In June 2026, at the team's request, the coding agent also modelled a storage version of the auger, with the flight on only the third of the tube nearest the outlet, an open upper end, and a screw-on cap.

\subsubsection{What replaced the AI parts}
The parts the agents modelled were a starting point rather than the finished design: the tap collar, the auger, and the bracket were all revised or replaced, before the tests or since (Fig.~\ref{fgr:v2redesign}). Every AI-modelled part was reviewed, printed, and tested, but the doser in this paper ran on later versions. The agent's tap collar was revised over several iterations, remodelled in Zoo Design Studio, and has since been replaced by a solenoid bracket that lets an auger be swapped without removing the tapper. The engineers redrew the storage auger in Fusion~360 with a 20:44 gear pair in place of the AI model's 16:48, a uniform 0.5~mm flight, and a slightly longer pitch (SI Table~\siref{tbl:augercompare}), and that auger turned in every test. The bracket went from the team's dimensioned drawing and the agent's model of it to a bolted split clamp and then a flexible clip. One AI-modelled part is still on the doser: the tap-collar base, a small plate whose raised stop keeps the collar from turning with the auger (see Experimental).

\subsubsection{How the agents worked}
The specifications given to the AI grew steadily more detailed. The first requests described what the whole system had to do. Later requests described what each part had to do and how it met its neighbours: bolt patterns, shaft diameters, and mating faces. After that, the team supplied annotated engineering drawings, several of them fully dimensioned with the geometric relations between features (the bracket in Fig.~\ref{fgr:v2timeline}a). By the end, the AI's job had narrowed to modelling, that is, turning drawings into parametric CAD and working out derived dimensions and clearances. Every design decision stayed with the team.

Most parts were produced by LLM coding agents (the GitHub Copilot coding agent, mostly running Claude Opus models) working directly in the repository (Fig.~\ref{fgr:v2timeline}b; the tools, dates, and models are listed in the Experimental section). Given a task written as a GitHub issue, the agent wrote the part as parametric code (Python with CadQuery, or OpenSCAD). In the same session it ran scripts that rendered views of the part, exported printable STL mesh files, and checked the geometry. The agent then opened a pull request, and we reviewed the part from the rendered images. SI Section~\siref{sec:aicad} sets out this loop with an example drawing, the checklist of interfaces that requests specified, and when a part counted as finished. We also explored two commercial text-to-CAD tools on the same parts: CADSmith, and Zoo Design Studio, which pairs a code-based CAD engine, scripted in the KittyCAD Language (KCL), with a conversational design agent called Zookeeper. In Zoo Design Studio, too, parts were built mainly by chatting with the agent rather than by editing the model by hand.


\subsubsection{One part at a time}
Early attempts asked the agent for the complete module from a description of its layout. The results looked plausible and did not work. Given the same whole-doser prompt, the coding agent, CADSmith, and the Zoo Text-to-CAD service each returned parts that did not make a working assembly (SI Fig.~\siref{fgr:v2cadcompare}a--c). The first tap collar merged several functions into one body, with overlapping features, wrong tolerances, and no room for the actuators it existed to carry, and an early bracket arrived with its two mounting plates unconnected. Requests at the level of the assembly repeatedly produced overlapping solids, floating parts, and changes nobody had asked for, and fixing one defect often introduced another. The agent produced usable parts once each request covered a single part and listed where it connected to its neighbours. This fits the known weakness of current LLMs at reasoning about how shapes fit together in space.\cite{Alrashedy2025Generating} Splitting a design into parts with defined interfaces is standard practice for human engineers, and when AI does the modelling it currently appears to be required. SI Table~\siref{tbl:aifail} lists the failure modes we met and what caught each one. Of the 76 entries up to October 2026 that made or changed a part model, the coding agent wrote 72 and 16 were printed. People reviewing renders caught 30 of the 41 recorded defects, printing or bench tests caught 6, and the agent's own checks caught 4 (SI Table~\siref{tbl:designlog}).

\subsubsection{Checking the output}
Most of the effort went into checking. Because any iteration could change geometry outside the part being edited, every generated part had to be re-inspected in full. In one case the agent produced four miniature test nozzles and swapped the labels on two of them, which would have invalidated a dispensing comparison had a person not noticed. Automated checks (that a part is a single solid, that its surface has no holes, and that key interface dimensions are correct) caught a useful share of defects cheaply. Small errors still escaped both the checks and visual review: a height mismatch of one print layer was found only when the part peeled off the print bed. AI-generated alternatives were valuable early, while the design was still open, but late in the project the agent could not tell how close a part was to finished and kept changing features that were already right. AI-modelled parts therefore tended to stop at ``good enough'', because each further round of edits cost time and risked breaking something that worked.

\subsubsection{Comparing the tools}
We used the coding agent from April to June 2026, CADSmith in May and June, and Zoo Design Studio from May to early July, mostly in June, so the comparison below describes the tools as they were then. The coding-agent workflow was fully traceable and needed nothing beyond a web browser, but each iteration took minutes and every change had to be reviewed. CADSmith, which we tried on the mounting plate and baseplate, returned only simplified, blocky geometry and was no more useful than the coding agent (SI Fig.~\siref{fgr:v2cadcompare}g,h). Zoo Design Studio was the team's clear favourite. Its Zookeeper agent reasoned noticeably better about space, ran its own checks (such as counting solid bodies) before returning a result, and worked inside a live CAD view, so the team could inspect an editable model as it was built. Each iteration was slower, of the order of an hour, but on the parts we tried the team usually reached an acceptable design in fewer Zoo iterations, although we did not compare the two under controlled conditions. The final tap collar reached a usable design in three Zoo iterations after the same number of coding-agent iterations had failed to orient the solenoid correctly (SI Fig.~\siref{fgr:v2cadcompare}d--f). At the time of testing, Zoo also had limits: imported reference parts could not be edited, there were no constraints between parts in an assembly, and manual edits were largely limited to changing parameter values, so the work stayed conversational. These rankings are the team's impressions from one project, and we did not measure them. Because these tools are changing quickly, the ranking may not last. In our more recent work, agents that drive a full CAD package through its application programming interface (API) have been among the most successful: an agent working only through Onshape's API assembled the current design from the team's part files, and the exploded views in Fig.~\ref{fgr:v2doser}b and SI Fig.~\siref{fgr:v2exploded} were rendered from that assembly. Open-source projects such as text-to-cad\cite{TextToCad} are promising newer examples.

We also judged that every AI route was slower, and gave lower-quality geometry, than experienced mechanical engineers working in conventional CAD software would have. The Fusion~360 recreations give a rough check. The narrated recordings for nine parts total about three hours of edited video,\cite{FusionPlaylist} roughly the length of a single Zoo Design Studio session. The AI route did leave documentation behind as a by-product of the work. Every design intent, constraint, and revision exists as text, so the design can be reproduced, its history traced, and the project handed over more easily than with conventional CAD files alone.

\subsection{How a dose runs}
\label{sec:dose}

A dose can be asked for in plain language in a GitHub comment. The AI agent passes the target to a three-phase controller on the doser's microcontroller, which reads the balance after every step and acts on the mass still to go: bulk, then fine, then tap (Fig.~\ref{fgr:v2dosing}). In the bulk phase the tube is tilted and the auger turns continuously; in the fine phase the tube is tilted less and the auger turns in small steps, with a settled reading after each; and in the tap phase the tube is level and solenoid taps release the last milligrams. Because the controller acts on the mass still to go, the same procedure serves every target. The agent also reports back in the same thread, so the request, the run, and the result stay together in the repository. The settings of each procedure are given in the Experimental section.

\subsection{Open-loop delivery of 13 powders}
\label{sec:openloop}

We tested seven food-safe surrogates and six research powders, including gas-atomized AlSi10Mg and two grades of silicon (Table~\ref{tbl:v2powders}). Open loop, under one fixed set of settings, the powders spanned more than an order of magnitude in mass per revolution, and the flow came in waves, one surge per revolution (Fig.~\ref{fgr:v2openloop}). The surrogates were chosen to cover a wide range of flow behaviour cheaply and safely: table salt (NaCl, the control), calcium lactate, xanthan gum, carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), sodium alginate, and white and brown rice flour. The six research powders are relevant to the alloy-discovery work the doser is meant to serve: a gas-atomized AlSi10Mg alloy powder (near-spherical particles made by spraying molten metal), a coarse and a fine grade of silicon, sodium sulfate, barium chloride, and fumed silica, a very light flow aid. We ran each powder through the same fixed sequence of test protocols with frozen settings (Table~\ref{tbl:protocols}), so that differences between powders reflect the powders rather than the settings. In the first test round (4--21 August 2026), the food-safe powders were tested on an open laboratory bench and the other powders in a shared fume hood. The balance became much noisier from 20 August: with nothing moving, eight successive readings varied by 0.0~mg on every run up to 12 August and by up to 88~mg afterwards (SI Fig.~\siref{fgr:bench}). Bench conditions are therefore confounded with powder group, because every research-relevant powder was measured in the noisier period.

Delivery is not smooth. Balance readings streamed during continuous rotation rise in steps that recur about every 4~s at 15~rpm, once per auger revolution (Fig.~\ref{fgr:v2openloop}c). Across ten powders, the median period from autocorrelation was 3.94~s against a revolution time of 4.00~s. A single-start flight should behave this way, because each revolution carries one pocket of powder over the outlet. The pulsing limits how finely rotation alone can meter powder, and it is why the final approach to a requested mass uses taps. A single tap released between 0.2~mg (sodium alginate) and 20~mg (calcium lactate), or 0.6--10\% of one revolution's delivery (Fig.~\ref{fgr:v2openloop}b). The size of a tap does not simply follow throughput: barium chloride and calcium lactate delivered similar amounts per revolution (185 and 198~mg) but released 2.1 and 20~mg per tap. Tap increments could be resolved only on the quieter runs; on most fume-hood runs they were smaller than the bench noise.

Tilt is the strongest setting the controller has. For the nine powders measured at all three angles, raising the tube from horizontal to 22.5$^\circ$ increased delivery by a median factor of 4.2, and raising it further to 45$^\circ$ added a median factor of 1.2 (SI Table~\siref{tbl:v2openloop}). The cohesive powders depended most on gravity: sodium alginate delivered 15 times more per revolution at 45$^\circ$ than horizontally and white rice flour 10 times more, against about 5 times for calcium lactate and sodium sulfate. Tilt was also the only setting that moved one of the non-conveying powders at all (fine silicon, at 45$^\circ$). With the auger stopped, nothing flowed on its own: in 33 fifteen-second holds, covering all three tilts on the 11 runs recorded up to 12 August, the balance reading changed by 0.0~mg every time (protocol B). Parking the tube horizontally therefore gives a clean shut-off, at least to the resolution of the quiet-bench runs. The vibration motor could not be characterized: its driver returned an input/output error in every run that asked for protocol F. The speed sweep (protocol D) suggests that delivery rate grows more slowly than speed, with a median exponent of 0.78 across ten powders for rate $\propto$ speed$^{\alpha}$ over 15--90~rpm, but each speed was measured once, in a fixed order, and a firmware timing error let the auger turn 15--54\% further than commanded, so we treat these exponents as an indication only.

The two ends of the range are consistent with particle shape. Gas-atomized AlSi10Mg consists of near-spherical particles in a narrow size range, the properties L-PBF requires, and it was the fastest powder tested. The fine silicon is milled, angular, and smaller than 44~$\mu$m (it passes a 325-mesh sieve). It did not move at all at 0$^\circ$ or 22.5$^\circ$, and at 45$^\circ$ it produced a single 7~mg pulse in six revolutions. The coarse silicon, the same material at roughly 75--150~$\mu$m, delivered 211~mg per revolution. Forces that make particles stick together matter more relative to their weight as particles get smaller, which fits this sharp difference between the two silicon grades. We read the three powders that conveyed no measurable amount as a limit of this auger geometry rather than a fixed property of the powders; a wider flight, a larger pitch, or a taller fin would move that limit. The failures also differ in kind. Fine silicon moved slightly at the steepest tilt, so for it the driving force was too weak to overcome cohesion. Fumed silica, which is extremely light and fluffy, never delivered more than a few milligrams per revolution at any tilt and varied wildly from one revolution to the next, which points instead to trapped air.

\subsection{Closed-loop dosing at three targets}
\label{sec:closedloop}

Closed loop, the 1~g doses were the most reliable (Fig.~\ref{fgr:v2closedloop}). Most of the misses at 50 and 200~mg trace to the controller rather than to the hardware, and the controller is what we are now improving (Section~\ref{sec:future}). With the controller settings tuned by hand on salt and then frozen for every powder, we ran three doses of 1~g (protocol G) on each powder whose bench conditions allowed it in the first test round. A second round, from 3 to 15 September 2026, added three doses each of 50~mg and 200~mg (protocol H) and the 1~g doses that bench noise had prevented for the research-relevant powders. Doses are scored against acceptance limits fixed before testing: $\pm$10\% of the requested mass below 100~mg and $\pm$5\% at or above it. If dose errors are spread evenly within these limits, the composition error of an equimolar five-component blend\cite{Vecchio2021Throughput,Moorehead2020Throughput} is 0.5 atomic percent (one standard deviation), with a worst case of 1.6 atomic percent; the aluminium-rich blends planned for our alloy work, in batches of 10--500~g, stay near 0.4 atomic percent even in the worst case (SI Section~\siref{sec:comperror}). Each delivered mass is also recorded, so the as-dosed composition is known to within 0.1 atomic percent. Processing can shift composition far more: directed energy deposition from elemental powders has shifted it by 5--10 atomic percent.\cite{Moorehead2020Throughput,Chen2020Elemental} Failed doses stay in every count, and SI Table~\siref{tbl:doseaccount} accounts for every attempt, including those excluded and the combinations never run.

At 1~g, 24 of the 39 valid doses finished within the $\pm$5\% limit (Fig.~\ref{fgr:v2closedloop}; Table~\ref{tbl:v2closedloop}). They were every dose on the seven free-flowing and moderately slow powders: salt, calcium lactate, xanthan gum, CMC, AlSi10Mg, coarse silicon, and sodium sulfate. The three research-relevant powders among them were the most accurate: all nine of their 1~g doses finished within 10~mg (1\%) of the target, with a median time of 111~s. The four powders that failed at 1~g failed for two different reasons. Fine silicon and brown rice flour delivered almost nothing, as in the open-loop tests. White rice flour and sodium alginate did move, but too slowly: they used up the controller's budget of 200 small rotations and stopped 14\% and 29\% short of the target after about 14 minutes.

Smaller doses were harder, in different ways for fast and slow powders. At 200~mg, 18 of 30 doses finished within $\pm$5\%, including all three for AlSi10Mg, xanthan gum, and white rice flour. At 50~mg, 14 of 30 finished within $\pm$10\%, although most of these passes lie within the balance noise of the limit (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). The fast powders now overshot: of the 14 doses on free-flowing powders that missed the limit at 50 or 200~mg, 13 were overshoots, including all three sodium sulfate doses at 50~mg. For these powders a single 45$^\circ$ turn of the auger delivered a median of 30~mg, against 2~mg for the slow powders, and delivery arrives in pulses. The last step of a 50~mg dose on a free-flowing powder therefore added a median of 46~mg, enough to step over the whole acceptance band. The controller also had a flaw at small targets. It was meant to skip continuous rotation, but because the threshold for that phase equalled the target, a tared reading a fraction of a milligram below zero started a brief continuous spin. This happened in 19 of the 60 valid 50 and 200~mg doses and delivered up to 103~mg (SI Fig.~\siref{fgr:dosetraces} shows representative traces). Slow powders undershot instead, for the same reason as at 1~g. Overshoot cannot be undone in powder dosing, so for small targets a finer final increment matters more than a faster one.

Time was the main cost of accuracy. Doses that finished within the limit took a median of 52~s at 50~mg, 152~s at 200~mg, and 185~s at 1~g (Table~\ref{tbl:v2closedloop}). Slow powders took much longer: white rice flour needed 11--13 minutes for each 200~mg dose. Two features of the controller explain most of the remaining failures. First, it never returns to an earlier phase. Once the tap phase begins, it cannot rotate the auger to refill the outlet, apart from 5$^\circ$ nudges that proved too small for the slower powders. Second, its stopping band was $\pm$5~mg at every target. At 1~g that is ten times tighter than the $\pm$50~mg acceptance limit, so the firmware logged some doses as stalled although they met the specification. We report every dose against the acceptance limit and keep the firmware's own label in the per-dose record (SI Table~\siref{tbl:doses}).

\subsection{Dosing for the alloy workflow}
\label{sec:alloy}

The doser is already used in our alloy workflow. In late September 2026, with settings from a separate optimization of the controller, it weighed 7.993~g of AlSi10Mg for an 8~g request into the cup of a custom charge, which the lab's ultrasonic atomizer then turned into new powder. It then dosed Al~4047 powder made by the same atomizer into an 8~g charge and into two cups for a composition spread, at 7.5 and 15 weight percent of the filled cup (SI Section~\siref{sec:al4047}). Each container was filled by a sequence of closed-loop doses, ten in all, run by an AI agent on request in the repository. Unlike the gas-atomized feedstock, this powder contained coarse agglomerates that lodged in the 3~mm outlet. Steeper tilt (60$^\circ$ and 75$^\circ$), faster rotation, and faster tapping did not clear the blockage, but about 13 reverse turns of the auger with the tube level did, after which the tube fed 60--80~mg per turn in either direction. Once a team member had cleared the larger particles from the auger, a single bulk dose fed 1.10~g at about 25~mg~s$^{-1}$ without stalling. With hand-driven steps between closed-loop doses where needed, the three containers finished at 8.0010~g (target 8.0000~g), 3.0432~g (3.0407~g), and 6.5676~g (6.5674~g), that is, within 2.5~mg or 0.08\% of the target. Two lessons carry over: a powder made in the laboratory should be sieved before it is loaded, and reversing the auger is a simple, effective way to clear an outlet.

Before the test campaign, on 24 July 2026, the doser also had a short qualitative trial inside an inert-atmosphere glove box in a polymer chemistry laboratory at the University of Utah. Team members typed requests to an AI agent in a GitHub issue, and the agent sent the commands to the doser over the network, so nobody had to reach into the glove box to run it. The first 1~g dose of salt needed two runs of the controller and ended 4.4~mg short. In the smallest auger, chunks of a solid norbornene monomer dispensed well, whereas fine, hair-like azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN, a polymerization initiator) appeared to clog and delivered nothing. Static charge in the dry atmosphere made salt cling to the inside of the auger, and vibration slowly loosened the nuts until the gears no longer meshed; locknuts, and brackets printed in one piece with the plate, address the loosening.

\subsection{Limits of the current design}
\label{sec:limits}

Several limits follow from these results. First, the design covers doses from tens of milligrams to grams. Doses of 0.1--10~mg, which some chemistry applications need, are out of reach for now; they would require per-powder calibration, better isolation from vibration, and a smaller auger. Second, three of the 13 powders conveyed no measurable amount, so the current geometry sets a floor on how cohesive or how light a powder can be, and coarse agglomerates can block the outlet. Third, the outcome of a dose depends on how much powder sits in the screw near the outlet when the dose starts, and on how full and how compacted the tube is. There is no defined starting state, and in routine use the auger would never be reset between doses, so this variability is part of the real performance. Fourth, all masses were weighed in the cup, so powder that left the tube but missed the cup is not counted, and the tube's fill level was not controlled. Fifth, the doser ran each set of doses unattended, but people filled and mounted the augers, emptied the cup between runs, and recovered from faults, most of which involved the balance or its surroundings (SI Table~\siref{tbl:operator}). Finally, the balance sets the noise floor. Idle readings drifted by up to tens of milligrams in late August. In the second round, readings at rest scattered by less than 0.4~mg, but readings taken right after the doser moved scattered by 5--19~mg on half of the runs with small doses (SI Fig.~\siref{fgr:noiseact}). Only one of the 14 passing 50~mg doses lay more than twice that noise inside its limit, so the 50~mg pass rate is uncertain, while the failures are clear. Isolating the balance from the doser's motion is therefore as important as refining the controller.

\subsection{Building one}
\label{sec:build}

The current design is documented so that others can build it. SI Fig.~\siref{fgr:v2exploded} shows every part, screws and nuts included, numbered as in the bill of materials, and SI Fig.~\siref{fgr:steps} shows the assembly in 19 steps, from the exploded view to the finished doser. The structural parts are printed in polylactic acid (PLA), most of them with support material, and print in under 24~h on a consumer printer; the remaining parts are commodity items. The CAD for each part is a public Onshape document with its print settings, the team's Fusion~360 parts that ran in the tests are linked in SI Table~\siref{tbl:parts}, and the firmware, schematics, and board files are in the repository. Anyone loading a powder that is not food-safe should first read the safety notes in the Experimental section.

The single-channel module (printed parts, motors, drivers, and microcontroller) costs under \$200 in single quantities, excluding the balance, which most laboratories already own, and the custom circuit board, which a breadboard can replace (SI Table~\siref{tbl:bom}). Commercial automated gravimetric dosing modules are quoted at \$30k--\$300k depending on configuration. Even where commercial instruments are more accurate, a cost gap of two orders of magnitude changes what a laboratory can do. It can dedicate one printed auger to each powder so that powders never mix, throw away and reprint the parts the powder touches, and replace a failed channel within a day. This is the economic argument for open-source laboratory hardware\cite{Pearce2020Economic,Hohlbein2022Open} applied to the solid-handling gap in SDLs. % TODO (human): DD's hardware editorial asks for a side-by-side comparison with commercial and open alternatives (dose range, accuracy, time per dose, powders, price, build time); SUBMISSION-DECISIONS.md item 12.

\subsection{Fabrication and build files}
We printed all parts in PLA on a Bambu Lab H2D printer (0.2~mm layers, four perimeters, 25\% infill), most of them with support material. The auger used in every test is a single printed piece modelled by the team in Fusion~360 (Fig.~\ref{fgr:v2doser}c). It is a tube 250~mm long, with a 25~mm outer diameter and a 21~mm bore. An 8~mm central core and a single-start flight, 0.5~mm thick with a 10.4~mm pitch, run along the 83~mm nearest the outlet, which tapers to a 3~mm exit hole. A 44-tooth gear (module 1) is printed on the outside of the tube 78--88~mm from the outlet, and an external thread at the upper end carries the screw-on cap through which the tube is filled. A 20-tooth pinion on the stepper drives the 44-tooth gear (2.2:1 reduction), and the servos drive the tilt plate through a 2:1 gear. The auger, its cap, and the stepper pinion were each modelled in a narrated recording.\cite{FusionPlaylist} The first AI-modelled geared auger (May 2026) instead had a flight along its whole length, four loading slots in its closed top end, and a 16:48 gear pair (3:1); the agent's later storage version already had the capped upper end and the flight confined to the outlet third, so the team's main changes were the gear pair, the flight, and the cap thread (SI Table~\siref{tbl:augercompare}). One AI-modelled part is still on the doser: the tap-collar base, which the coding agent wrote as parametric Python code in May 2026. SI Table~\siref{tbl:parts} links the design file of every printed part, and SI Section~\siref{sec:build} gives the build in 19 steps. The current parts are also public Onshape documents, one per part, with their print settings, and the AI-modelled parts' source files (CadQuery, OpenSCAD, and KCL), printable STL files, and renders for every iteration are in the repository. Purchased parts: a NEMA-11 stepper motor (auger drive), two MG996R-class servos (tilt), a 12~V push--pull solenoid (tapping), a coin-type ERM motor (vibration; unused), and an A\&D HR-100A analytical balance (0.1~mg readability). The auger turns in printed collars, without bearings.

\subsection{Electronics and firmware}
A Raspberry Pi Pico~W runs MicroPython firmware with commands to rotate by a given angle, tap, vibrate, tilt to an angle, and read the mass. A Pololu Tic T500 drives the stepper over a serial (UART) link, a DRV8871 H-bridge drives the solenoid, and a DRV2605L haptic driver runs the vibration motor over I$^2$C. The balance is read over RS-232 through an SP3232 transceiver board. The electronics began on a breadboard and now sit on a custom printed circuit board (PCB). Schematics, board files, the wiring guide, and the firmware are in the repository (\texttt{hardware/}). The dosing firmware includes both gear ratios, so its rotation commands and records are in auger revolutions, auger rpm, and tube degrees. The one exception is the test battery, whose raw records store the tilt as a servo setting; the 2:1 tilt gear halves that setting, so the battery's three tilts are tube angles of 0$^\circ$, 22.5$^\circ$, and 45$^\circ$. We report tube angles throughout and convert the raw settings once, in the analysis scripts (SI Section~\siref{sec:protocols}). The auger ratio is confirmed independently by the protocol-D traces, whose pulses recur once per commanded auger revolution (3.94 against 4.00~s).

\subsection{Powders and test protocols}
Table~\ref{tbl:v2powders} lists the 13 powders. Each powder had its own printed auger and cap, labelled with the powder's name, so no auger carried two powders. We filled each auger once through its capped upper end, without controlling the fill level or weighing it, and stored it loaded with tape over the outlet. A run began by mounting the filled auger and removing the tape, with occasional top-ups. Within a run nobody acted between doses: each dose was weighed from its own tare with earlier powder left in the cup, and the cup was emptied between runs. SI Table~\siref{tbl:operator} lists every task a person did in the campaign. The test protocols (Table~\ref{tbl:protocols}) ran in a fixed order with frozen settings, identically for every powder. Each protocol emitted machine-readable trial records over the serial link, and we archived them as versioned CSV files stamped with the powder, firmware version, and settings, so every number in this paper can be traced to its raw balance record. Protocol D streamed the balance every 0.25~s nominally (0.29--0.39~s in practice) while the auger turned continuously; its timing loop advanced by the nominal poll interval rather than the elapsed time, so masses and rates from protocol D use the elapsed time.

We screened each run for quality before analysis. For example, we excluded a run with a failed tilt servo, a deliberate stress test of the bench, and runs in which the balance could not be read. One run per powder is shown in Fig.~\ref{fgr:v2openloop}: the newest run that passed screening, preferring runs whose protocols C and E agreed. We report a mean as a measurement only if it exceeds twice its standard error, and otherwise report the powder as not having moved. Fumed silica is the one exception to screening. Its only run failed screening because the outlet could not be seen on camera to rule out a blockage, so its result (an average of at most 0.25~mg per revolution over 350 revolutions) is reported but is less certain than the others. The noise floor of each run is the spread of its eight protocol-A readings.

\subsection{Closed-loop dosing procedures}
\emph{Protocols G and H.} The controller has three phases, with settings that were the same for every powder and every dose. In the bulk phase it tilts the tube to 45$^\circ$ and turns the auger continuously at 55~rpm, stopping 0.12~g early to allow for powder still in flight, until less than 0.5~g remains. In the fine phase it tilts the tube to 22.5$^\circ$ and turns the auger in 45$^\circ$ steps, waiting 1.5~s for a settled reading after each, until less than 50~mg remains. In the tap phase it lowers the tube to horizontal and fires two 60~ms solenoid taps per cycle, again reading the balance after each cycle, until the reading is within $\pm$5~mg of the target. If three tap cycles in a row add no mass, it turns the auger forward 5$^\circ$ to refill the outlet, up to ten times. For the smaller protocol-H doses, the hand-over points were capped at the target mass (bulk) and at half the target (fine). A dose ends when the reading is within the $\pm$5~mg band (``ok''), when it exceeds the target by more than the band (overshoot), when tapping and nudging add nothing more (stalled), after 200 cycles of one phase (cycle budget), or after 900~s. We tuned the settings by hand on salt and then froze them, and no dose outcome was used to retune them. In the first round the controller used a reading only once the balance flagged it as stable; after the move to a second fume hood, the balance rarely reported stability while the rig was running, so the second round averaged a bracket of five readings taken 0.25~s apart instead. Before each set of doses, the balance had to stay within the dose tolerance while nothing moved, and runs that could not pass this check were postponed. Our reporting follows the gravimetric structure that ISO 8655-6 prescribes for piston pipettes, as adapted for an open-hardware pipette:\cite{Yoshikawa2023Pipette,Yoshikawa2026Commit} replicate doses at several target masses, each summarized by its systematic error (mean deviation from target), random error (standard deviation), overshoot rate, and time. Every dose, including failures and the firmware's own termination label, is listed in the per-dose record (\texttt{paper/figures/data/doses\_all.csv}) and summarized by powder and target in SI Table~\siref{tbl:doses}.

\emph{Stepped-tilt procedure.} A script running on the microcontroller wrapped the same three-phase controller. In the bulk phase the auger turned at 80~rpm with one 60~ms tap every 300~ms while the tube stepped down through 60, 55, 50, 45, and 40$^\circ$, one step per equal mass band or after 3~s without flow, until 0.25~g remained. In the fine phase the tube was at 30$^\circ$ and the auger turned steadily at 25~rpm with the same taps, stopping 20~mg before the hand-over at 25~mg to go. The tap phase was the controller's own, at 0$^\circ$: one tap and a 15$^\circ$ auger turn per cycle, a 1.5~s settle, and a 5$^\circ$ nudge after three cycles without gain, until the reading was within $\pm$1~mg. A trial stalled after 8~s without a 2~mg gain. The balance was logged at about 9--10 readings per second, and each trial was saved to its own CSV file. A run stopped itself after two failed trials in a row, at a cap on the mass in the beaker, or on a balance overload.

\emph{Atomizer doses.} The Al~4047 doses used a bulk-only mode added for them: the tube at 40$^\circ$, the auger at 100~rpm with two taps per second, slowing linearly over the last 100~mg and speeding up again whenever flow stopped for 3~s, and halting when the reading plus the recent flow rate times 0.83~s (the measured time for which powder keeps falling after the auger stops) reached the target. Between closed-loop doses, the agent ran stepwise doses and reverse turns through the same command interface, with a stable balance reading after each step (SI Section~\siref{sec:al4047}).

\subsection{Safety}
Several of the powders are hazardous, and anyone repeating this work should handle them as their safety data sheets direct. Fine metal and semimetal powders, such as AlSi10Mg, Al~4047, and the $-$325 mesh silicon, are combustible dusts: keep the quantities small, avoid raising dust clouds, and keep them away from sparks. The stepper motor, the solenoid, and static charge on the printed PLA parts are possible ignition sources. Barium chloride is toxic if swallowed and harmful if inhaled. Fumed silica, fine silicon, and the flours form fine dusts that should not be breathed in, so handle them in a fume hood or another ventilated enclosure. The AIBN used in the glove-box trial can catch fire if heated. The gears and the tilt hinge are pinch points while the doser is moving.

\section{Conclusions}

A single-channel powder doser with closed-loop weighing can be built from printed parts and commodity electronics for under \$200 plus a balance. With one fixed set of settings, it delivered 10--231~mg per auger revolution for ten of 13 powders and no measurable amount of the other three. In closed loop, 24 of 39 doses of 1~g finished within $\pm$5\% of the target, including every dose on the seven fastest-flowing powders dosed, while doses of 50 and 200~mg met their limits in only 14 and 18 of 30. A revised procedure that steps the tilt down as the cup fills landed 87 of 103 repeated doses within $\pm$5\%, including all 20 of 2~g on AlSi10Mg, 11 of them within $\pm$1~mg. The doser has filled atomizer charges with AlSi10Mg (7.993~g for 8~g) and with Al~4047 from our own atomizer (each container within 2.5~mg of its target). Its main limits are an auger geometry that cannot move very fine or very light powders and can be blocked by coarse agglomerates, a controller whose fixed stopping margin lets fast powders overshoot, and a balance whose noise sets the floor for small doses.

The design process is the second result, although it comes from one team and one project. Generating a whole assembly with AI did not work. Generating one part at a time against dimensioned drawings, with automated checks and human review, worked better, and it left behind a complete written record of every decision. AI was most effective as a modelling assistant rather than an autonomous design agent, and the engineers redrew the parts that were built and tested. The design log, with 183 entries and every failure included, is itself a dataset for studying AI-assisted hardware design.

\label{sec:future}
This module is the first of several planned stages. The doser holds one powder at a time, and changing powder means swapping the auger by hand. The first extension is a multi-powder doser that needs nobody to swap augers. The team is designing two in conventional CAD, both built on the single doser: each powder still lives in its own auger on a hinged plate, turned by a stepper motor and tapped by a solenoid. One carries the auger modules on roller chains past a single station over the balance, and the other holds three modules on one fixed base around one cup. Both exist only in CAD so far and will be reported separately. The second is automatic calibration for each powder: Bayesian optimization of the phase thresholds, tilt angles, speeds, and tap schedules, subject to the constraint that an overshoot cannot be undone, is under way and should make small doses more reliable and set each powder's stopping margin from the powder that keeps falling after the auger stops. The third is a public database of tuned settings for many powders, linked to measured powder properties. The test protocols may help build it: the mass per revolution and its variation come from protocol C alone, which takes about 2~min per powder, so if they track independent measurements such as bulk and tapped density, the doser could classify a new powder as a side effect of dosing it, although that idea is still untested. We also plan changes to the auger geometry aimed at the powders that did not convey, transparent augers to watch the flight fill and empty, and continued evaluation of generative design tools, including AI tools for laying out printed circuit boards like the one that now carries the doser's electronics.
