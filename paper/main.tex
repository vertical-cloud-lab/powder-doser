\section{Introduction}

Self-driving laboratories (SDLs) pair automated instruments with machine-learning planners that choose the next experiment, and they have sped up discovery in chemistry and materials science by an order of magnitude or more.\cite{Abolhasani2023Rise,Tom2024Self,Szymanski2023Autonomous,Seifrid2022Autonomous} Software for running them,\cite{Fei2024Alabos,Guzik2023Decade} methods for planning experiments,\cite{Baird2026Honegumi} and robots for handling liquids are now mature. Handling solids is not: most SDLs either work only with liquids or rely on a person to weigh out powders.\cite{Maffettone2023What,Canty2025Science} The gap matters most where the powders are the experiment. In alloy discovery by additive manufacturing (AM, the 3D printing of metal parts), each candidate composition is made by blending elemental or pre-alloyed powders,\cite{Li2018Combinatorial,Vecchio2021Throughput,Moorehead2020Throughput,Mooraj2024Additive,Nelaturu2024Multi} so weighing many different powders accurately and repeatably is the step that limits the whole workflow.

Commercial powder-dosing systems exist and work well, but their cost and size put them out of reach of most academic laboratories. Benchtop gravimetric (weight-based) dispensers such as the METTLER TOLEDO XPR/CHRONECT line, Labman dosing stations, and MTI's AM powder dispensers cost from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand dollars, are built around pharmaceutical and quality-control work, and usually dose one vial at a time from proprietary dosing heads.\cite{Besenhard2015Accuracy,Yang2007Metering} Pharmaceutical manufacturing meters powders at much higher flow rates with loss-in-weight feeders, which weigh the hopper continuously as it empties,\cite{Engisch2012Method,Engisch2015Feedrate,Hanson2018Control} and laboratory studies have dosed milligram quantities with capillaries, vibrating chutes, and augers.\cite{Besenhard2017Micro,Faulhammer2014Dose,Chianrabutra2014Powder,Alsenz2011Powderpicking} None of these is available as an open design that a laboratory can print, build, and modify. Open-source hardware has shown that 3D-printed instruments can reach research-grade performance at 1--10\% of the commercial price and keep improving through community contributions,\cite{Pearce2020Economic,Hohlbein2022Open,Wenzel2023Open,Stirling2022Hardops} and platforms such as Jubilee and low-cost SDL kits have brought that model to laboratory automation.\cite{Vasquez2020Jubilee,Subbaraman2024Duckbot}

This paper describes the first module of such an instrument: a single-channel powder doser in which a 3D-printed auger delivers powder into a cup on an analytical balance, and a microcontroller turns, tilts, and taps the auger until the balance reads the requested mass (Fig.~\ref{fgr:overview}). Each channel is a self-contained printable unit, and later versions will dose several powders (Section~\ref{sec:future}). We report the design, a test campaign on 13 powders with very different flow behaviour, and closed-loop dosing results, including where the current design fails.

This study's second contribution concerns how the doser was designed. Its parts were first designed without conventional CAD software such as SolidWorks or Fusion~360. They were modelled by generative artificial intelligence instead: large language model (LLM) coding agents wrote each part as code in the project's public repository, and we also explored a chat-driven CAD tool, Zoo Design Studio. The division of labour was the same throughout. People decided what to build, wrote the specifications, eventually supplied fully dimensioned drawings, and reviewed, printed, and tested every part. The AI tools turned those decisions into geometry. Research on generative CAD has produced large datasets and models,\cite{Wu2021Deepcad,Willis2021Fusion,Seff2021Vitruvion,Xu2024Brepgen} and LLMs can now write CAD code (scripts that CAD software turns into solid models) from written descriptions,\cite{Alrashedy2025Generating,Badagabettu2024Query2cad,Jansen2023Words,Rios2023Text} but published evaluations test benchmark shapes rather than the design of a working instrument from start to finish. Every prompt, generated part, review comment, and failure in this project is preserved in a public repository,\footnote{All issue threads, pull requests, and design-log entries cited in this paper are preserved at \url{https://github.com/vertical-cloud-lab/powder-doser}. ``The repository'' refers to this address throughout.} so the doser doubles as a documented case study of AI-assisted hardware design. Once the AI-designed doser worked, the team recreated its parts in Fusion~360 to obtain editable models for release. Those sessions were recorded too, so they are documented in the same way and give a rough comparison between the two routes. The doser used for the tests in this paper carries some of these recreated parts, including the auger and the stepper pinion that drives it (see Experimental). We describe the workflow that worked best, the ways the AI tools failed, and what the approach cost and gained compared with conventional design.

\section{Results and discussion}

\subsection{The dosing module}

Fig.~\ref{fgr:overview}a,b shows the module as designed and as built. The printed auger tube is also the powder reservoir, so there is no separate hopper. Powder is poured in through the open upper end of the tube, which is then closed with a screw-on cap. The tube (25~mm outer diameter, 21~mm bore, 250~mm long) is printed as one piece. Along the third of its length nearest the outlet, a single-start screw (the flight, 10.4~mm pitch) winds around an 8~mm central core. The rest of the tube is plain and holds the powder (Fig.~\ref{fgr:overview}c). Two smaller augers, whose narrower inner channel is fed through a funnel at the top, hold about 3.3 and 9.2~mL, so the reservoir can be sized to the sample (SI Table~\siref{tbl:parts}). The whole piece turns together, like an Archimedes screw, rather than as a screw inside a fixed barrel. A NEMA-11 stepper motor turns it through a printed 20-tooth pinion and a 44-tooth gear printed on the outside of the tube, a 2.2:1 reduction. This auger and pinion are the Fusion~360 recreations described below. A push--pull solenoid taps the tube through a printed collar to help cohesive powders move. A small eccentric-rotating-mass (ERM) vibration motor was also planned, but its driver never worked (Section~\ref{sec:tilttap}), so the doser as tested relies on rotation, tilt, and tapping. The tube rides on a hinged plate that two servos tilt through a 2:1 gear. The hinge axis runs just behind the outlet, about 12~mm from it in the current design, so the dose lands in nearly the same place at every angle (Fig.~\ref{fgr:overview}d): the tube can be parked horizontally, where nothing flows, and tipped up to 45$^\circ$, its maximum tilt, to dispense, which moves the outlet about 3~mm horizontally and 8~mm down.

An analytical balance (A\&D HR-100A, 0.1~mg readability) weighs the collection cup and reports to a \$6 Raspberry Pi Pico~W microcontroller. The microcontroller runs a three-phase dosing procedure: fast continuous rotation, then small rotations, then solenoid taps until the reading is within tolerance of the requested mass (Fig.~\ref{fgr:overview}e; see Experimental). The structural parts are printed in PLA, most of them with support material, and the remaining parts are commodity items. The bill of materials comes to under \$200 for a single channel, excluding the balance, which most laboratories already own, and the custom circuit board, which a breadboard can replace (SI Table~\siref{tbl:bom}). That is roughly two orders of magnitude below a commercial gravimetric dosing head.

\subsection{How the parts were designed}

The mechanical design went through four versions in its first six weeks. Every step, then and since, is recorded in a design log in the repository, which now has 128 entries. % TODO (human): name the hackathon if wanted (the repository README links a "Presentation at POSE 2026"); dates from the first commits, 23--24 April 2026.
The project began at a two-day hackathon in April 2026 as a hand-sketched mechanical scoop (a ``powder excavator'') meant to ride on an existing computer-controlled (CNC) gantry. An AI-assisted survey of small-scale dosing mechanisms\cite{Besenhard2017Micro,Faulhammer2014Dose} produced a list of eight candidates, among them a tap sieve, a metering wheel, a capillary wiper, and an auger. We chose the auger for its dose resolution, its printability, and its tolerance of different powders, in line with its wide use in commercial micro-feeders.\cite{Yang2007Metering,Besenhard2015Accuracy} Before the second version, the team also printed four outlet geometries for the auger (SI Fig.~\siref{fgr:nozzles}). The second version packaged the auger, frame, drive, and actuators as a self-contained channel, the repeating unit of a future multi-powder doser. The third version changed the method rather than the mechanism. Instead of asking the AI tools for the whole module at once, we specified each part separately (auger, geared auger, bracket, tap collar, mounting plate, baseplate, and servo hinge). AI tools modelled each part, and we reviewed and printed it. The fourth version assembled these parts on the hinged baseplate with servo tilt. After this AI-designed doser worked, the team recreated its parts in Fusion~360 and changed some of them. The tested auger is filled through a capped end rather than through slots in the tube wall, and its flight no longer runs the full length of the tube. The tap collar was also redesigned to hold a different solenoid.

The specifications given to the AI grew steadily more detailed. The first requests described what the whole system had to do. Later requests described what each part had to do and how it met its neighbours: bolt patterns, shaft diameters, and mating faces. After that, the team supplied annotated engineering drawings, several of them fully dimensioned with the geometric relations between features. By the end, the AI's job had narrowed to modelling, that is, turning drawings into parametric CAD and working out derived dimensions and clearances. Every design decision stayed with the team.

Most parts were produced by LLM coding agents (the GitHub Copilot coding agent, mostly running Claude Opus models) working directly in the repository. Given a task written as a GitHub issue, the agent wrote the part as parametric code (Python with CadQuery, or OpenSCAD). In the same session it ran scripts that rendered views of the part, exported printable STL mesh files, and checked the geometry. The agent then opened a pull request, and we reviewed the part from the rendered images. SI Section~\siref{sec:aicad} sets out this loop with an example drawing, the checklist of interfaces that requests specified, and when a part counted as finished. We also explored two commercial text-to-CAD tools on the same parts: CADSmith, and Zoo Design Studio, which pairs a code-based CAD engine, scripted in the KittyCAD Language (KCL), with a conversational design agent called Zookeeper. In Zoo Design Studio, too, parts were built mainly by chatting with the agent rather than by editing the model by hand. Every prompt, generated file, render, and review comment is preserved in the repository's pull requests and in a dedicated discussion thread.

\subsubsection{One part at a time}
Early attempts asked the agent for the complete module from a description of its layout. The results looked plausible and did not work. The first tap collar merged several functions into one body, with overlapping features, wrong tolerances, and no room for the actuators it existed to carry (Fig.~\ref{fgr:genai}a). An early bracket arrived with its two mounting plates unconnected. Requests at the level of the assembly repeatedly produced overlapping solids, floating parts, and changes nobody had asked for, and fixing one defect often introduced another (Fig.~\ref{fgr:genai}d). The agent produced usable parts once each request covered a single part and listed where it connected to its neighbours (Fig.~\ref{fgr:genai}c). This fits the known weakness of current LLMs at reasoning about how shapes fit together in space,\cite{Alrashedy2025Generating} and it suggests that splitting a design into parts with defined interfaces, standard practice for human engineers, is currently required rather than optional when AI does the modelling. Table~\ref{tbl:aifail} lists the failure modes we met and what caught each one. Each design-log entry records what triggered the change, the reasoning, and the outcome (built and worked, built with issues, failed, or superseded). Of the 76 entries that made or changed a part model, the coding agent wrote 72 and 16 were printed. People reviewing renders caught 30 of the 41 recorded defects, printing or bench tests caught 6, and the agent's own checks caught 4 (SI Table~\siref{tbl:designlog}).

\subsubsection{Checking the output}
Most of the effort went into checking. Because any iteration could change geometry outside the part being edited, every generated part had to be re-inspected in full. In one case the agent produced four miniature test nozzles and swapped the labels on two of them, which would have invalidated a dispensing comparison had a person not noticed. Automated checks (that a part is a single solid, that its surface has no holes, and that key interface dimensions are correct) caught a useful share of defects cheaply. Small errors still escaped both the checks and visual review: a height mismatch of one print layer was found only when the part peeled off the print bed. One pattern held across the project. AI-generated alternatives were valuable early, while the design was still open, but late in the project the agent could not tell how close a part was to finished and kept changing features that were already right. AI-modelled parts therefore tended to stop at ``good enough'', because each further round of edits cost time and risked breaking something that worked.

\subsubsection{Comparing the tools}
We used the coding agent from April to June 2026, CADSmith in May and June, and Zoo Design Studio from May to early July, mostly in June, so the comparison below describes the tools as they were then. The coding-agent workflow was fully traceable and needed nothing beyond a web browser, but each iteration took minutes and every change had to be reviewed. CADSmith, which we tried on the mounting plate and baseplate, returned only simplified, blocky geometry and was no more useful than the coding agent. Zoo Design Studio was the team's clear favourite. Its Zookeeper agent reasoned noticeably better about space, ran its own checks (such as counting solid bodies) before returning a result, and worked inside a live CAD view, so the team could inspect an editable model as it was built. Each iteration was slower, of the order of an hour, but on the parts we tried the team usually reached an acceptable design in fewer Zoo iterations, although we did not compare the two under controlled conditions. The final tap collar reached a usable design in three Zoo iterations after the same number of coding-agent iterations had failed to orient the solenoid correctly. At the time of testing, Zoo's limits mirrored its strengths: imported reference parts could not be edited, there were no constraints between parts in an assembly, and manual edits were largely limited to changing parameter values, so the work stayed conversational. These rankings are the team's impressions from one project, and we did not measure them. A controlled comparison with identical prompts and parts is planned. Because these tools are changing quickly, the ranking may not last. In our more recent work, agents that drive a full CAD package through its application programming interface (API) have been among the most successful: an agent working only through Onshape's API assembled the current design from the team's part files, and the exploded view and build steps in SI Figs.~\siref{fgr:exploded} and \siref{fgr:steps} were rendered from the same assembly. Improvements are visible across all of these tools, and open-source projects such as text-to-cad\footnote{\url{https://github.com/earthtojake/text-to-cad}} are promising newer examples. We also judged that every AI route was slower, and gave lower-quality geometry, than experienced mechanical engineers working in conventional CAD software would have. A rough check came later, when two team members recreated the parts in Fusion~360 to produce editable models for release, changing some of them along the way. The narrated recordings for nine parts total about three hours of edited video,\footnote{Playlist ``Human redesign of AI-made parts'', \url{https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLZTWCFxzhTQv42fTuW2Tjs9o1KuWA_b-I} (repository issue \#120).} roughly the length of a single Zoo Design Studio session. The recreations were not exact copies (the drive gears, for example, went from 16:48 to 20:44 teeth), and the recreated auger and pinion turned during every test reported here. What the AI route bought instead was documentation as a by-product of the work. Every design intent, constraint, and revision exists as text, which makes the design reproducible, its history traceable, and the project easy to hand over, in a way that conventional CAD files are not.

\subsection{How much powder one turn delivers}

We tested the doser on 13 powders (Table~\ref{tbl:powders}). Seven are food-safe surrogates, chosen to cover a wide range of flow behaviour cheaply and safely: table salt (NaCl, the control), calcium lactate, xanthan gum, carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), sodium alginate, and white and brown rice flour. The other six are relevant to the alloy-discovery work the doser is meant to serve. They are a gas-atomized AlSi10Mg alloy powder (near-spherical particles made by spraying molten metal), a coarse and a fine grade of silicon, sodium sulfate, barium chloride, and fumed silica, a very light flow aid. We ran each powder through the same fixed sequence of test protocols with frozen settings (Table~\ref{tbl:protocols}), so that differences between powders reflect the powders rather than the settings. In the first test round (4--21 August 2026), the food-safe powders were tested on an open laboratory bench and the other powders in a shared fume hood. The balance became much noisier from 20 August: with nothing moving, eight successive readings varied by 0.0~mg on every run up to 12 August and by up to 88~mg afterwards (SI Fig.~\siref{fgr:bench}). Bench conditions are therefore confounded with powder group, because every research-relevant powder was measured in the noisier period.

With one set of settings, the mass delivered per auger revolution at a 22.5$^\circ$ tilt varied 24-fold across the ten powders that conveyed a measurable amount, from 10~mg for sodium alginate to 231~mg for AlSi10Mg (Fig.~\ref{fgr:conveyance}a). Seven powders (AlSi10Mg, coarse silicon, sodium sulfate, calcium lactate, barium chloride, xanthan gum, and salt) delivered 146--231~mg per revolution, and CMC, white rice flour, and sodium alginate delivered 10--26~mg. Three powders conveyed no reliably measurable amount at any tilt: brown rice flour, fine silicon, and fumed silica. The powder mattered far more than the settings. Splitting the variation in mass per revolution across all measured revolutions (on a log scale), 70\% came from which powder was loaded, 20\% from tilt, 4\% from how each powder responded to tilt, and 6\% from scatter between revolutions.

Delivery is not smooth. Balance readings streamed during continuous rotation rise in steps (Fig.~\ref{fgr:conveyance}b) that recur about every 4~s at 15~rpm, once per auger revolution. Across ten powders, the median period from autocorrelation was 3.94~s against a revolution time of 4.00~s. A single-start flight should behave this way, because each revolution carries one pocket of powder over the outlet. The pulsing limits how finely rotation alone can meter powder, and it is why the final approach to a requested mass uses taps (Section~\ref{sec:closedloop}).

Plotting each powder's throughput against its variation from one revolution to the next (Fig.~\ref{fgr:conveyance}c) separates the powders into a readily dosed group, which delivers 150--230~mg per revolution with 2--18\% variation, and a slow group that delivers 10--26~mg. Both numbers come from about two minutes of bench time per powder, which makes the plot a candidate for sorting new powders quickly. For now it is a grouping rather than a validated diagnostic, because we have not independently measured particle size, particle shape, or bulk density.

The two ends of the range are consistent with particle shape. Gas-atomized AlSi10Mg consists of near-spherical particles in a narrow size range, the properties laser powder-bed fusion requires, and it was the fastest powder tested. The fine silicon is milled, angular, and smaller than 44~$\mu$m (it passes a 325-mesh sieve). It did not move at all at 0$^\circ$ or 22.5$^\circ$, and at 45$^\circ$ it produced a single 7~mg pulse in six revolutions. The coarse silicon, the same material at roughly 75--150~$\mu$m, delivered 211~mg per revolution. Forces that make particles stick together matter more relative to their weight as particles get smaller, which fits this sharp difference between the two silicon grades. We read the three powders that conveyed no measurable amount as a limit of this auger geometry rather than a fixed property of the powders. A wider flight, a larger pitch, or a taller fin would move that limit. The failures also differ in kind. Fine silicon moved slightly at the steepest tilt, so for it the driving force was too weak to overcome cohesion. Fumed silica, which is extremely light and fluffy, never delivered more than a few milligrams per revolution at any tilt and varied wildly from one revolution to the next, which points instead to trapped air.

\subsection{Tilt and tapping}
\label{sec:tilttap}

Tilt is the strongest setting the controller has. For the nine powders measured at all three angles, raising the tube from horizontal to 22.5$^\circ$ increased delivery by a median factor of 4.2, and raising it further to 45$^\circ$ added a median factor of 1.2 (Fig.~\ref{fgr:knobs}a). The cohesive powders depended most on gravity: sodium alginate delivered 15 times more per revolution at 45$^\circ$ than horizontally and white rice flour 10 times more, against about 5 times for calcium lactate and sodium sulfate. Tilt was also the only setting that moved one of the non-conveying powders at all (fine silicon, at 45$^\circ$).

With the auger stopped, nothing flowed on its own. In 33 fifteen-second holds, covering all three tilts on the 11 runs recorded up to 12 August, the balance reading changed by 0.0~mg every time (protocol B). From 20 August, hold readings scattered by tens of milligrams in both directions, including at 0$^\circ$ where gravity cannot empty the tube, so they reflect the bench rather than the powder. Parking the tube horizontally therefore gives a clean shut-off, at least to the resolution of the quiet-bench runs.

A single solenoid tap released between 0.2~mg (sodium alginate) and 20~mg (calcium lactate), or 0.6--10\% of one revolution's delivery (Fig.~\ref{fgr:knobs}b). The size of a tap does not simply follow throughput. Barium chloride and calcium lactate delivered similar amounts per revolution (185 and 198~mg) but released 2.1 and 20~mg per tap, a tenfold difference. Tap increments could be resolved only on the quieter runs. On most fume-hood runs they were smaller than the bench noise.

The vibration motor could not be characterised: its driver (DRV2605L) returned an input/output error in every run that asked for protocol F, so the doser as tested relies on rotation, tilt, and tapping alone. The speed sweep (protocol D) suggests that delivery rate grows more slowly than speed, with a median exponent of 0.78 across ten powders for rate $\propto$ speed$^{\alpha}$ over 15--90~rpm (above 1 only for white rice flour, CMC, and sodium alginate). Each speed was measured once, in a fixed order, without re-zeroing the balance, and a firmware timing error let the auger turn 15--54\% further than commanded, so we treat these exponents as an indication only and do not plot them.

\subsection{Closed-loop dosing}
\label{sec:closedloop}

The controller doses in three phases: continuous rotation until the cup is within 0.5~g of the requested mass, then 45$^\circ$ rotations with a settled balance reading after each, and finally pairs of solenoid taps until the reading is within $\pm$5~mg (see Experimental). All doses used one set of phase settings, tuned by hand on salt and frozen for every powder. In the first test round we ran three doses of 1~g (protocol G) on each powder whose bench conditions allowed it. A second round, from 3 to 15 September 2026, added three doses each of 50~mg and 200~mg (protocol H) and the 1~g doses that bench noise had prevented for the research-relevant powders. Doses are scored against acceptance limits fixed before testing: $\pm$10\% of the requested mass below 100~mg and $\pm$5\% at or above it. These limits were chosen so that dosing error adds less than one atomic percent of composition error to a typical five-component alloy blend.\cite{Vecchio2021Throughput,Moorehead2020Throughput} Failed doses stay in every count, and SI Table~\siref{tbl:doseaccount} accounts for every attempt, including those excluded and the combinations never run.

At 1~g, 24 of the 39 valid doses finished within the $\pm$5\% limit (Fig.~\ref{fgr:closedloop}c). They were every dose on the seven free-flowing and moderately slow powders: salt, calcium lactate, xanthan gum, CMC, AlSi10Mg, coarse silicon, and sodium sulfate. The three research-relevant powders were the most accurate: all nine of their 1~g doses finished within 10~mg (1\%) of the target, with a median time of 111~s. The four powders that failed at 1~g failed for two different reasons. Fine silicon and brown rice flour delivered almost nothing, as in the open-loop tests. White rice flour and sodium alginate did move, but too slowly: they used up the controller's budget of 200 small rotations and stopped 14\% and 29\% short of the target after about 14 minutes.

Smaller doses were harder, in different ways for fast and slow powders (Fig.~\ref{fgr:closedloop}a,b). At 200~mg, 18 of 30 doses finished within $\pm$5\%, including all three for AlSi10Mg, xanthan gum, and white rice flour. At 50~mg, 14 of 30 finished within $\pm$10\%, although most of these passes lie within the balance noise of the limit (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). The fast powders now overshot: of the 14 doses on free-flowing powders that missed the limit at 50 or 200~mg, 13 were overshoots, including all three sodium sulfate doses at 50~mg. For these powders a single 45$^\circ$ turn of the auger delivered a median of 30~mg, against 2~mg for the slow powders, and delivery arrives in pulses (Fig.~\ref{fgr:conveyance}b). The last step of a 50~mg dose on a free-flowing powder therefore added a median of 46~mg, enough to step over the whole acceptance band. The controller also had a flaw at small targets. It was meant to skip continuous rotation, but because the threshold for that phase equalled the target, a tared reading a fraction of a milligram below zero started a brief continuous spin. This happened in 19 of the 60 valid 50 and 200~mg doses and delivered up to 103~mg (SI Fig.~\siref{fgr:dosetraces} shows representative traces). Slow powders undershot instead, for the same reason as at 1~g. Overshoot cannot be undone in powder dosing, so for small targets a finer final increment matters more than a faster one.

Time was the main cost of accuracy (Fig.~\ref{fgr:closedloop}d). Doses that finished within the limit took a median of 53~s at 50~mg, 152~s at 200~mg and 185~s at 1~g, far longer than the 30~s we had aimed for. Slow powders took much longer: white rice flour needed 11--13 minutes for each 200~mg dose. The fastest ``doses'' were failures: fine silicon and brown rice flour stopped after 6--52~s, when the controller detected that nothing was arriving.

Two features of the controller explain most of the remaining failures. First, it never returns to an earlier phase. Once the tap phase begins, it cannot rotate the auger to refill the outlet, apart from 5$^\circ$ nudges that proved too small for the slower powders. When the flight nearest the outlet runs empty, taps alone cannot finish the dose. This is how the first-round 1~g doses of calcium lactate, xanthan gum, and CMC ended: each came to rest 15--49~mg short after 66--166 taps, inside the acceptance limit but outside the controller's own stopping band. Second, that stopping band was $\pm$5~mg at every target. At 1~g it is ten times tighter than the $\pm$50~mg acceptance limit, so the firmware logged these doses as stalled although they met the specification. We report every dose against the acceptance limit and keep the firmware's own label in the per-dose record (\texttt{paper/figures/data/doses\_all.csv}, summarised in SI Table~\siref{tbl:doses}). A controller that can return to rotation when taps stop working, and whose stopping band scales with the target, is the most direct improvement.

\subsection{A first trial in a glove box}
\label{sec:glovebox}

Before the test campaign, on 24 July 2026, the doser had a short qualitative trial inside an inert-atmosphere glove box in a polymer chemistry laboratory at the University of Utah (Fig.~\ref{fgr:overview}b). Team members typed requests to an AI agent in a GitHub issue, and the agent sent the commands to the doser over the network, so nobody had to reach into the glove box to run it. The first 1~g dose of salt needed two runs of the controller, because salt fed slowly with the settings then in use, and it ended 4.4~mg short. In the smallest auger, chunks of a solid norbornene monomer dispensed well with the three-phase procedure. A mid-sized auger loaded with about 2~g of fine, hair-like azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN, a polymerization initiator) appeared to clog and delivered nothing. Recrystallizing AIBN into coarser grains may be a simpler fix than changing the doser. Two problems were specific to the glove box. Static charge in the dry atmosphere made salt cling to the inside of the auger, and vibration from the glove box and the motor slowly loosened the nuts until the gears no longer meshed. Locknuts, and brackets printed in one piece with the plate, address the loosening. An ionizing fan, which was on hand but not used, may help with the static. The chemists who hosted the trial asked to build several dosers of their own.

\subsection{Limits of the current design}
\label{sec:limits}

Several limits follow from these results. First, the design covers doses from tens of milligrams to grams. Doses of 0.1--10~mg, which some chemistry applications need, are out of reach for now. They would require per-powder calibration, better isolation from vibration, and a smaller auger. Second, three of the 13 powders conveyed no measurable amount, so the current geometry sets a floor on how cohesive or how light a powder can be. Third, the outcome of a dose depends on how much powder happens to sit in the screw near the outlet when the dose starts. There is no defined starting state, and in routine use the auger would never be reset between doses anyway, so this variability is part of the real performance. Fourth, all masses were weighed in the cup, so powder that left the tube but missed the cup is not counted, and the tube's fill level was not controlled. Finally, the balance sets the noise floor. Idle readings drifted by up to tens of milligrams in late August. In the second round, readings at rest scattered by less than 0.4~mg, but readings taken right after the doser moved scattered by 5--19~mg on half of the runs with small doses (SI Fig.~\siref{fgr:noiseact}). Only one of the 14 passing 50~mg doses lay more than twice that noise inside its limit, so the 50~mg pass rate is uncertain, while the failures are clear. Isolating the balance from the doser's motion is therefore as important as refining the controller.

\subsection{Cost and accessibility}

The single-channel module (printed parts, motors, drivers, and microcontroller) costs under \$200 in single quantities, excluding the balance (SI Table~\siref{tbl:bom}), and prints in under 24~h on a consumer printer. Commercial automated gravimetric dosing modules are quoted at \$30k--\$300k depending on configuration. Even where commercial instruments are more accurate, a cost gap of two orders of magnitude changes what a laboratory can do. It can dedicate one printed channel to each powder so that powders never mix, throw away and reprint the parts the powder touches, and replace a failed channel within a day. This is the economic argument for open-source laboratory hardware\cite{Pearce2020Economic,Hohlbein2022Open} applied to the solid-handling gap in SDLs.

\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Fabrication}
We printed all parts in PLA on a Bambu Lab H2D printer (0.2~mm layers, four perimeters, 25\% infill), most of them with support material. The auger used in every test is a single printed piece modelled by the team in Fusion~360 (Fig.~\ref{fgr:overview}c). It is a tube 250~mm long, with a 25~mm outer diameter and a 21~mm bore. An 8~mm central core and a single-start flight, 0.5~mm thick with a 10.4~mm pitch, run along the 83~mm nearest the outlet, which tapers to a 3~mm exit hole. A 44-tooth gear (module 1) is printed on the outside of the tube 78--88~mm from the outlet, and an external thread at the upper end carries the screw-on cap through which the tube is filled. We measured these dimensions from the part file. A 20-tooth pinion on the stepper drives the 44-tooth gear (2.2:1 reduction), and the servos drive the tilt plate through a 2:1 gear. The auger, its cap, and the stepper pinion were each modelled in a narrated recording (\url{https://youtu.be/sKrvGBTUc8U}, \url{https://youtu.be/EID3ppxmE1U}, and \url{https://youtu.be/EO9qnYssKRQ}), as described in repository issues \#117 and \#120. The earlier AI-modelled auger in the repository instead had a flight along its whole length, four loading slots in the tube wall, and a 16:48 gear pair (3:1). One AI-modelled part is still on the doser: the tap-collar base, a small plate under the tap collar whose raised stop keeps the collar from turning with the auger. The coding agent wrote it as parametric Python code in May 2026 (repository pull request \#51), and SI Table~\siref{tbl:parts} links its file.

\subsection{Electronics and firmware}
A Raspberry Pi Pico~W runs MicroPython firmware with commands to rotate by a given angle, tap, vibrate, tilt to an angle, and read the mass. A Pololu Tic T500 drives the stepper over a serial (UART) link, a DRV8871 H-bridge drives the solenoid, and a DRV2605L haptic driver runs the vibration motor over I$^2$C. The balance is read over RS-232 through an SP3232 transceiver board. The electronics began on a breadboard and now sit on a custom printed circuit board (PCB). Schematics, board files, the wiring guide, and the firmware are in the repository (\texttt{hardware/}).

The dosing firmware includes both gear ratios, so its rotation commands and records are in auger revolutions, auger rpm, and tube degrees. The one exception is the test battery, whose raw records store the tilt as a servo setting rather than an angle. The 2:1 tilt gear halves that setting, so the battery's three tilts are tube angles of 0$^\circ$, 22.5$^\circ$, and 45$^\circ$, the last being the maximum. We report tube angles throughout and convert the raw settings once, in the analysis scripts. SI Section~\siref{sec:runs} maps the raw settings to angles. The auger ratio is confirmed independently by the protocol-D traces, whose pulses recur once per commanded auger revolution (3.94 against 4.00~s).

\subsection{Powders and test protocols}
Table~\ref{tbl:powders} lists the 13 powders. Each powder had its own printed auger and cap, labelled with the powder's name, so no auger carried two powders. We began each run by filling the auger tube through its capped upper end, without controlling the fill level or weighing the loaded tube. The test protocols (Table~\ref{tbl:protocols}) ran in a fixed order with frozen settings, identically for every powder. Each protocol emitted machine-readable trial records over the serial link, and we archived them as versioned CSV files stamped with the powder, firmware version, and settings, so every number in this paper can be traced to its raw balance record. A trial is one recorded measurement: in protocol C one trial is a single auger revolution, so its 18 trials are six revolutions at each of three tilts. Protocol D streamed the balance every 0.25~s nominally (0.29--0.39~s in practice) while the auger turned continuously. Its timing loop advanced by the nominal poll interval rather than the elapsed time, so the auger turned 15--54\% further than the three revolutions commanded per speed. Masses and rates from protocol D use the elapsed time.

We screened each run for quality before analysis. For example, we excluded a run with a failed tilt servo, a deliberate stress test of the bench, and runs in which the balance could not be read. One run per powder is shown in Figs.~\ref{fgr:conveyance} and \ref{fgr:knobs}: the newest run that passed screening, preferring runs whose protocols C and E agreed. We report a mean as a measurement only if it exceeds twice its standard error, and otherwise report the powder as not having moved. Fumed silica is the one exception to screening. Its only run failed screening because the outlet could not be seen on camera to rule out a blockage, so its result (an average of at most 0.25~mg per revolution over 350 revolutions) is reported but is less certain than the others. The noise floor of each run is the spread of its eight protocol-A readings.

\subsection{Closed-loop dosing procedure}
The controller has three phases, with settings that were the same for every powder and every dose. In the bulk phase it tilts the tube to 45$^\circ$ and turns the auger continuously at 55~rpm, stopping 0.12~g early to allow for powder still in flight, until less than 0.5~g remains. In the fine phase it tilts the tube to 22.5$^\circ$ and turns the auger in 45$^\circ$ steps, waiting 1.5~s for a settled reading after each, until less than 50~mg remains. In the tap phase it lowers the tube to horizontal and fires two 60~ms solenoid taps per cycle, again reading the balance after each cycle, until the reading is within $\pm$5~mg of the target. If three tap cycles in a row add no mass, it turns the auger forward 5$^\circ$ to refill the outlet, up to ten times. For the smaller protocol-H doses, the hand-over points were capped at the target mass (bulk) and at half the target (fine). At 50~mg, for example, the controller is meant to skip fast rotation and to switch from small rotations to tapping when 25~mg remains. A tared reading slightly below zero could still start a brief continuous spin, because the bulk threshold equalled the target (Section~\ref{sec:closedloop}). A dose ends when the reading is within the $\pm$5~mg band (``ok''), when it exceeds the target by more than the band (overshoot), when tapping and nudging add nothing more (stalled), after 200 cycles of one phase (cycle budget), or after 900~s. We tuned the settings by hand on salt and then froze them, and no dose outcome was used to retune them. In the first round the controller used a reading only once the balance flagged it as stable. After the move to a second fume hood, the balance rarely reported stability while the rig was running, so the second round averaged a bracket of five readings taken 0.25~s apart instead. Before each set of doses, the balance had to stay within the dose tolerance while nothing moved. Runs that could not pass this check were postponed. Our reporting follows the gravimetric structure that ISO 8655-6 prescribes for piston pipettes, as adapted for an open-hardware pipette:\cite{Yoshikawa2023Pipette,Yoshikawa2026Commit} replicate doses at several target masses, each summarised by its systematic error (mean deviation from target), random error (standard deviation), overshoot rate, and time. Every dose, including failures and the firmware's own termination label, is listed in the per-dose record in the repository (\texttt{paper/figures/data/doses\_all.csv}) and summarised by powder and target in SI Table~\siref{tbl:doses}.

\subsection{AI design workflow and logging}
LLM coding agents carried out tasks written as GitHub issues: the GitHub Copilot coding agent from April to June 2026, when the AI-modelled parts were made, and Anthropic's Claude Code agent from the end of June 2026. Where the records name the model, the Copilot agent mostly ran Claude Opus~4.7 or 4.8, and Claude Code ran Claude Opus~4.8, Opus~5, Opus~5.5, Fable~5, and Fable~5.1 (SI Table~\siref{tbl:aitools}). Each design session produced a pull request containing the generated CAD code, rendered orthographic and isometric views, and STL files, which the team reviewed in the browser. We ran literature and design-review queries through Edison Scientific. We explored CADSmith (May and June 2026) and Zoo Design Studio (mainly June 2026, after trials of Zoo's text-to-CAD service from May) on the same part specifications. In Zoo Design Studio, modelling was done through its Zookeeper agent. No conventional CAD software was used in first designing the parts. The Fusion~360 recreations described in the Results came afterwards, and the tested rig includes some of them (see Fabrication). In line with \textit{Digital Discovery}'s guidance on the use of LLMs, the complete prompt and response history (issues, pull-request threads, agent session logs, and Zoo Design Studio transcripts) is public in the repository, with the date of every exchange, and is summarised in the SI. Over the project, team members addressed the two coding agents 754 times, and the agents authored 90\% of all commits, including those for firmware, analysis, and writing (SI Fig.~\siref{fgr:aiusage}).

\section{Conclusions}

A single-channel powder doser with closed-loop weighing can be built from printed parts and commodity electronics for under \$200 plus a balance. With one fixed set of settings, it delivered 10--231~mg per auger revolution for ten of 13 powders and no measurable amount of the other three. In closed loop, 24 of 39 doses of 1~g finished within $\pm$5\% of the target, including every dose on the seven fastest-flowing powders dosed, but doses of 50 and 200~mg met their limits in only 14 and 18 of 30 doses, and a typical dose took minutes rather than seconds. Its main limits are an auger geometry that cannot move very fine or very light powders, a controller that cannot recover once the outlet runs empty, and a balance whose noise sets the floor for small doses.

The module is already in use beyond the tests reported here. In July 2026 it dosed salt and a solid monomer inside a glove box, run by an AI agent over the network (Section~\ref{sec:glovebox}). In late September, with settings from the controller optimization described below, it weighed about 8~g of AlSi10Mg into the cup of a custom charge, which the lab's ultrasonic atomizer then turned into new powder. It also dispensed 8~g of an Al~4047 powder that the same atomizer had made.

The way the doser was designed may prove as useful as the hardware, although it comes from one team and one project. Generating a whole assembly with AI did not work. Generating one part at a time against dimensioned drawings, with automated checks and human review, worked better, and it left behind a complete written record of every decision. The 128-entry design log, failures included, is itself a dataset for studying AI-assisted hardware design.

\label{sec:future}
This module is the first of several planned stages. The first extension is a multi-powder doser. An early concept arranged several printed channels around a shared cup. The current design instead carries passive auger cartridges on a roller chain past a single fixed station that turns, taps, and weighs them, and a first printed prototype exists (Fig.~\ref{fgr:future}). Its parts were modelled by the team in conventional CAD software rather than by AI tools. The second is automatic calibration for each powder. This paper sets the phase thresholds, tilt angles, speeds, and tap schedules by hand. Bayesian optimization could tune them for each powder, subject to the constraint that an overshoot cannot be undone, and a formulation of that problem is already in the repository. Work on optimizing the controller is under way in the lab, and better control should make small doses of 50 and 200~mg more reliable. The third is a public database of tuned settings for many powders, linked to measured powder properties. The test protocols may help build it. The two numbers in Fig.~\ref{fgr:conveyance}c come from protocol C alone, which takes about 2~min per powder, and if they track independent measurements such as bulk and tapped density, the doser could classify a new powder as a side effect of dosing it, although that idea is still untested. We also plan experiments on the mechanism itself, including pausing the auger part-way through a revolution and printing transparent augers to watch the flight fill and empty, and changes to the auger geometry aimed at the powders that did not convey. Finally, we will keep evaluating generative design tools, including AI tools for laying out printed circuit boards like the one that now carries the doser's electronics.
