\section{Construction guide}
\label{sec:build}

Table~\ref{tbl:parts} lists the printed parts of the current doser and where to
download each one. Fig.~\ref{fgr:exploded} shows every part of the mechanical
assembly, numbered, with its bill of materials, and Fig.~\ref{fgr:steps} shows
the build in 19 steps. Both come from a CAD assembly of the team's part files,
% TODO (human): the Onshape document is still private (8 Oct 2026); set it to public.
which is also a public Onshape document
(\url{https://cad.onshape.com/documents/ae9f107d3972fc9d390e541f}); the same
steps are animated in the repository README. The wiring guide and firmware are
in \texttt{hardware/} in the repository. The build, in outline (step numbers
refer to Fig.~\ref{fgr:steps}, item numbers to Fig.~\ref{fgr:exploded}):

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Print} the parts in Table~\ref{tbl:parts} in PLA (0.2~mm layers,
four perimeters, 25\% infill). Most parts need support material. Print the
auger upright.
\item \textbf{Base} (steps 1--5): screw the baseplate to a flat board or bench
top (the CAD assumes 38~mm, the depth its front legs are made for), fit the two
servos into its posts, and fit a 14-tooth pinion on each servo.
\item \textbf{Tilt stage} (steps 6--7): mount the mounting plate on the hinge
with M5 screws and locknuts, so that the servo pinions mesh with the plate's
28-tooth gears (2:1).
\item \textbf{Drive} (steps 8--10): bolt the NEMA-11 stepper to its plate, fit
the 20-tooth pinion, and bolt the tap-collar base to the plate.
\item \textbf{Auger} (steps 11--17): slide one bracket and then the tap collar
onto the tube from the outlet end, lower the auger onto the plate so that its
44-tooth gear meshes with the stepper pinion (2.2:1), slide the second bracket
on from the cap end, and tighten the bracket screws and the tap-collar clamp.
The collar's ears rest against the raised stop on its base, so the collar
stays put while the tube turns inside it.
\item \textbf{Solenoid} (step 18): screw the solenoid to the tap collar so that
its plunger strikes the tube. (A vibration motor was also planned for the
collar but was not used in this work.)
\item \textbf{Electronics:} wire the Pico~W, Tic T500 (serial), DRV8871,
DRV2605L (I$^2$C), the RS-232 board (to the HR-100A balance), and the servos as
shown in the schematic in \texttt{hardware/test-module/}, then flash the
MicroPython firmware.
\item \textbf{Fill} (step 19): with the auger standing upright in the printed
filling stand, unscrew the cap, pour the powder into the upper end of the tube,
and screw the cap back on. There are no loading slots; the tube is its own
reservoir. For small or costly samples, one of the two smaller augers in
Table~\ref{tbl:parts} can take its place.
\item \textbf{Checkout:} run the firmware self-test (rotate, tap, tilt,
tare, and read), then the test protocols listed in the main text.
\item \textbf{First test (``hello world''):} fill an auger with about 50~g of
table salt, tilt it to 22.5$^\circ$, and turn it through six single revolutions at
30~rpm, reading the balance after each (protocol~C). On our doser each revolution
delivered 146 $\pm$ 11~mg (mean $\pm$ standard error; Table~\ref{tbl:v2openloop}),
arriving as one surge per revolution. Then request a 1~g dose with the frozen
three-phase settings (protocol~G): all six of our 1~g salt doses finished within
$\pm$5\%, and on the stepped-tilt procedure 23 of 24 did, in a median of 27~s
(Section~\ref{sec:steppedtilt}). A doser that delivers far less per revolution
probably has a packed or nearly empty tube, a blocked outlet, or a slipping gear.
\end{enumerate}

\section{AI-assisted CAD workflow}
\label{sec:aicad}

Fig.~\ref{fgr:workflow} shows how the AI-modelled parts were made. A team member
opened a GitHub issue for one part, often with an annotated sketch
(Fig.~\ref{fgr:workflow}a). The GitHub Copilot coding agent wrote the part as
CadQuery or OpenSCAD code and opened a pull request. Scripts in its session
rendered views, exported STL and STEP files, and checked that the part was one
closed solid (pull request~\#53) that did not overlap its neighbours (pull
request~\#66). We reviewed the renders and replied in the pull request, often
with marked-up screenshots. Each reply started a new agent session, with a
median length of 11~min. We printed accepted parts, fitted them, and tested them
on the doser, and problems went back to the agent. Issue~\#34 summed up the loop
as ``AI design $\rightarrow$ printing $\rightarrow$ testing $\rightarrow$ feedback
$\rightarrow$ AI design''. From June 2026, some parts, including the final tap
collar, were modelled instead by chatting with the Zookeeper agent in Zoo Design
Studio.

Each request set out how the part met its neighbours:
\begin{itemize}
\item mating parts and their files (the first auger for the bracket, pull
request~\#47; the stepper motor of issue~\#24 for the pinion, issue~\#48);
\item holes and fasteners (clamp screw and mounting holes, issue~\#46; M5 holes
on a square, pull request~\#66);
\item bores and clearances (the auger diameter ``with some tolerance'',
issue~\#46);
\item gear ratios and axes (2:1 servo gear, issue~\#65; hinge axis through the
dosing point, issue~\#56);
\item the face to print on (issue~\#46);
\item features that must not change (the auger's internal screw, issues~\#48
and \#67);
\item checks to run first (no interference and no floating bodies, issue~\#65).
\end{itemize}
Material was rarely stated, and all parts were printed in PLA.

We wrote no formal acceptance criteria but applied four in practice. The agent's
checks had to pass. A team member reviewed the renders and approved a print
(``We'll print and test this version'', pull request~\#68). The printed part had
to fit its neighbours and work (issue~\#46, pull request~\#49). Finally, we
stopped asking for changes and froze the geometry. The type-4 auger, for
example, became the main design after a bench test (issue~\#48). Because each
request risked changing features that were already right, parts often stopped
at ``good enough'' (discussion~\#39).

\section{Bench noise}
\label{sec:bench}

Protocol~A takes eight balance readings with nothing moving at the start of
every run. Their spread sets the smallest change that run can resolve
(Fig.~\ref{fgr:bench}). It was 0.0~mg on every run up to 12 August 2026 and
rose to tens of milligrams from 20 August. Runs are compared against their own
noise floor throughout the main text.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/figS2_bench}
\caption{Balance noise floor for every first-round run: the spread (maximum
minus minimum) of eight readings taken with nothing moving (protocol~A), in
date order. Runs whose eight readings were identical are drawn at the axis
floor. The dashed line is the controller's $\pm$5~mg stopping band.}
\label{fgr:bench}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Noise after actuation}

The controller acts on readings taken 1.5~s after it moves, so we also measured
the noise of those readings (Fig.~\ref{fgr:noiseact}a). For each run, we took
the robust standard deviation (1.4826 times the median absolute deviation) of
the change between consecutive tap-phase readings. Tapping adds only a few
milligrams per cycle and the cup cannot lose mass, so on the noisier runs this
scatter is balance noise. On quiet runs it is an upper bound, because it also
contains the powder that each tap delivered. The noise after actuation was at
most 0.9~mg in all six first-round runs with a tap phase, but 5--19~mg in five
of the ten second-round runs with small doses. On those same runs, the first
reading of each dose, taken at rest, scattered by at most 0.4~mg, so the
stability check made before each set of doses could not detect this noise. On
the noisy runs, lowering the tube to horizontal alone shifted the reading by a
median of 5--16~mg, which suggests that the doser's own motion disturbed the
balance.

Table~\ref{tbl:noiseact} compares each small dose's margin to its acceptance
limit with the noise of its own run. Of the 14 doses of 50~mg that finished
within the limit, only one lies more than twice its run's noise inside it, and
12 lie within one noise width of the limit. Of the 16 that missed, 13 lie more
than twice the noise outside it. At 200~mg, 6 of the 18 passes and 10 of the 12
failures clear twice the noise in the same way. The failures are therefore real, while
most 50~mg passes cannot be told apart from noise. The controller also stops at
the first reading inside its band, so on noisy runs the recorded masses lean
high. All small doses come from the second round, in one fume hood with
bracketed readings, so this comparison cannot be split by round or location.

\centering

\section{Stepped-tilt trials}
\label{sec:steppedtilt}

The repeated doses of the main text (18--20 November 2026) ran up to 30
consecutive closed-loop doses per powder, unattended, on a script that wrapped the
microcontroller's own three-phase controller. A team member chose the settings
in the repository's pull-request thread, and an AI agent wrote the script,
ran it on the doser over the network, and logged the balance at about 9--10
readings per second, one CSV file per trial.

\paragraph{Procedure.} Bulk: auger at 80~rpm with one 60~ms solenoid tap every
300~ms; the tube starts at 60$^\circ$ and steps down through 55, 50, 45, and
40$^\circ$, one step per equal band of mass (0.15~g for a 1~g target, 0.35~g for
2~g) or after 3~s without flow at an angle, until 0.25~g remains. Fine: tube at
30$^\circ$, auger turning steadily at 25~rpm with the same taps, stopped 20~mg
before the hand-over at 25~mg to go. Tap: the controller's stock tap phase at
0$^\circ$ (one tap and a 15$^\circ$ auger turn per cycle, a 1.5~s settle, and a
5$^\circ$ nudge after three cycles without gain, up to ten), finishing within
$\pm$1~mg. A trial stalled after 8~s without a 2~mg gain. A run stopped itself
after two failed trials in a row, at a cap on the mass in the beaker, or on a
balance overload. Targets were 1~g (NaCl), 1.5~g (sodium sulfate and barium
chloride), and 2~g (AlSi10Mg, calcium lactate, and xanthan gum); only the target
and what scales with it (the tilt bands, the stall margins, and the beaker cap)
changed between powders.

\paragraph{Development on salt.} Before the settings were frozen, 17 trials on
salt changed them step by step. With a tube filled to the brim, the salt plugged
at 60--40$^\circ$ and the auger turned without delivering. Bulk angles of
50--30$^\circ$, and then a newly printed tube, fed very little. Adding the summer
tap rate to the bulk phase got the salt moving, and raising the bulk speed from
55 to 80~rpm on the original, partly emptied tube gave the frozen settings. The
same tube then delivered 24 doses of 1~g in a row before it ran dry. Comparing
these runs with summer runs at the same angles and speeds, the settings meant the
same thing in both; the salt in the tube (how full, how packed, and how caked it
was) had changed.

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tbl:steppedsupp} lists the trials that did not
reach their target and the stage in which the completed doses ended. Powder
that kept falling after the fine phase stopped the auger (a median of 28--40~mg
for the four powders that reached that phase) exceeded the fixed 20~mg
anticipation, so 43 doses ended in the fine phase at or above the target. The
doses that reached the tap phase were the accurate ones: for AlSi10Mg, 9 of the
11 finished within $\pm$1~mg. Every sodium sulfate dose ended in the bulk phase,
which fed 0.36~g~s$^{-1}$ against 0.09--0.17~g~s$^{-1}$ for the others. The data
are in \texttt{paper/v2/data/stepped\_tilt/}: \texttt{trials.csv} (all 133
trials), \texttt{summary.csv}, and byte-identical copies of the per-launch
summaries from the run folders, which also hold every per-trial balance record,
bench-camera frames, and the scripts. \texttt{build.sh} there rebuilds the main-text
figure and table with gawk and pdflatex.

\section{Composition error from dosing error}
\label{sec:comperror}

The acceptance limits apply to each feedstock in a blend, and a feedstock built
from several passing doses of 100~mg or more is also within $\pm$5\%. Let
feedstock $f$ have mass $m_f$, relative mass error $\varepsilon_f$, and element
mass fractions $c_{fk}$, and let $M_k$ be the atomic weight of element $k$. With
$n_k=\sum_f m_f c_{fk}/M_k$ and $N=\sum_k n_k$, the mole fraction $x_k=n_k/N$
changes to first order by
\begin{equation}
\delta x_k=\sum_f S_{kf}\,\varepsilon_f,\qquad
S_{kf}=\frac{m_f}{N}\left(\frac{c_{fk}}{M_k}-x_k\sum_j\frac{c_{fj}}{M_j}\right).
\label{eq:comperror}
\end{equation}
Mass fractions follow by setting every $M$ to one. For pure elements,
$\delta x_i=x_i(\varepsilon_i-\sum_j x_j\varepsilon_j)$. If every error sits at a
common limit $L$ with the least favourable signs, $|\delta x_i|=2x_i(1-x_i)L$.
For an equimolar five-component blend ($x_i=0.2$, $L=5\%$), this is 1.6 atomic
percent, or 1.65 without the linear approximation. If the errors are
independent and uniform within the limits, the standard deviation is 0.52
atomic percent.

Table~\ref{tbl:comperror} applies Eq.~\ref{eq:comperror} to batches of 10, 100,
and 500~g, the range planned for our alloy work. Batch size only decides which
limit applies, so the dosing columns barely change with it. With uniform errors,
each element of the equimolar blend stays within one atomic percent in 97\% of
simulated blends. The measured errors of accepted doses give a standard
deviation of 0.40 atomic percent, and 0.08 atomic percent for the three
research-relevant powders. The aluminium-rich blends we plan to make, such as
Al-5Mn-2Cr-2Zr-0.5Ti, stay near 0.4 atomic percent even in the worst case. The
Al-24Si test blend behaves like the equimolar blend.

The balance also records each delivered mass, so the composition actually dosed
is known to within the balance uncertainty. For a 10~g batch, this is 0.002
atomic percent at 0.1~mg and 0.08 atomic percent at 5~mg. A dose that misses its
target can then be corrected, by re-targeting later feedstocks to the recorded
masses or topping up earlier ones. Feedstock chemistry adds an error of similar
size: supplier ranges for the silicon and AlSi10Mg powders put the 100~g
Al-24Si blend anywhere from 23.6 to 24.8~wt\% Si. Processing can add far more,
and directed energy deposition from elemental powders has shifted compositions
by 5--10 atomic percent.\cite{Moorehead2020Throughput,Chen2020Elemental} The
dosing limits are therefore suited to screening compositions. They do not by
themselves fix the composition of an atomized or printed alloy, which still has
to be measured.
