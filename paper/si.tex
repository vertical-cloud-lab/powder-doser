\section{Construction guide}
\label{sec:build}

Table~\ref{tbl:parts} lists the printed parts of the current doser and where to
download each one. Fig.~\ref{fgr:exploded} shows every part of the mechanical
assembly, numbered, with its bill of materials, and Fig.~\ref{fgr:steps} shows
the build in 19 steps. Both come from a CAD assembly of the team's part files,
% TODO (human): the Onshape document is still private (8 Oct 2026); set it to public.
which is also a public Onshape document
(\url{https://cad.onshape.com/documents/ae9f107d3972fc9d390e541f}); the same
steps are animated in the repository README. The wiring guide and firmware are
in \texttt{hardware/} in the repository. The build, in outline (step numbers
refer to Fig.~\ref{fgr:steps}, item numbers to Fig.~\ref{fgr:exploded}):

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Print} the parts in Table~\ref{tbl:parts} in PLA (0.2~mm layers,
four perimeters, 25\% infill). Most parts need support material. Print the
auger upright.
\item \textbf{Base} (steps 1--5): screw the baseplate to a flat board or bench
top (the CAD assumes 38~mm, the depth its front legs are made for), fit the two
servos into its posts, and fit a 14-tooth pinion on each servo.
\item \textbf{Tilt stage} (steps 6--7): mount the mounting plate on the hinge
with M5 screws and locknuts, so that the servo pinions mesh with the plate's
28-tooth gears (2:1).
\item \textbf{Drive} (steps 8--10): bolt the NEMA-11 stepper to its plate, fit
the 20-tooth pinion, and bolt the tap-collar base to the plate.
\item \textbf{Auger} (steps 11--17): slide one bracket and then the tap collar
onto the tube from the outlet end, lower the auger onto the plate so that its
44-tooth gear meshes with the stepper pinion (2.2:1), slide the second bracket
on from the cap end, and tighten the bracket screws and the tap-collar clamp.
The collar's ears rest against the raised stop on its base, so the collar
stays put while the tube turns inside it.
\item \textbf{Solenoid} (step 18): screw the solenoid to the tap collar so that
its plunger strikes the tube. (A vibration motor was also planned for the
collar but was not used in this work.)
\item \textbf{Electronics:} wire the Pico~W, Tic T500 (serial), DRV8871,
DRV2605L (I$^2$C), the RS-232 board (to the HR-100A balance), and the servos as
shown in the schematic in \texttt{hardware/test-module/}, then flash the
MicroPython firmware.
\item \textbf{Fill} (step 19): with the auger standing upright in the printed
filling stand, unscrew the cap, pour the powder into the upper end of the tube,
and screw the cap back on. There are no loading slots; the tube is its own
reservoir. For small or costly samples, one of the two smaller augers in
Table~\ref{tbl:parts} can take its place.
\item \textbf{Checkout:} run the firmware self-test (rotate, tap, tilt,
tare, and read), then the test protocols listed in the main text.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Bench noise}
\label{sec:bench}

Protocol~A takes eight balance readings with nothing moving at the start of
every run. Their spread sets the smallest change that run can resolve
(Fig.~\ref{fgr:bench}). It was 0.0~mg on every run up to 12 August 2026 and
rose to tens of milligrams from 20 August. Runs are compared against their own
noise floor throughout the main text.

\subsection*{Noise after actuation}

The controller acts on readings taken 1.5~s after it moves, so we also measured
the noise of those readings (Fig.~\ref{fgr:noiseact}a). For each run, we took
the robust standard deviation (1.4826 times the median absolute deviation) of
the change between consecutive tap-phase readings. Tapping adds only a few
milligrams per cycle and the cup cannot lose mass, so on the noisier runs this
scatter is balance noise. On quiet runs it is an upper bound, because it also
contains the powder that each tap delivered. The noise after actuation was at
most 0.9~mg in all six first-round runs with a tap phase, but 5--19~mg in five
of the ten second-round runs with small doses. On those same runs, the first
reading of each dose, taken at rest, scattered by at most 0.4~mg, so the
stability check made before each set of doses could not detect this noise. On
the noisy runs, lowering the tube to horizontal alone shifted the reading by a
median of 5--16~mg, which suggests that the doser's own motion disturbed the
balance.

Table~\ref{tbl:noiseact} compares each small dose's margin to its acceptance
limit with the noise of its own run. Of the 14 doses of 50~mg that finished
within the limit, only one lies more than twice its run's noise inside it, and
12 lie within one noise width of the limit. Of the 16 that missed, 13 lie more
than twice the noise outside it. At 200~mg, 6 of the 18 passes and 10 of the 12
failures are robust in the same sense. The failures are therefore real, while
most 50~mg passes cannot be told apart from noise. The controller also stops at
the first reading inside its band, so on noisy runs the recorded masses lean
high. All small doses come from the second round, in one fume hood with
bracketed readings, so this comparison cannot be split by round or location.

\section{Closed-loop dose record}
\label{sec:doses}

Table~\ref{tbl:doseaccount} accounts for every closed-loop dose attempt in both
test rounds, including the attempts we excluded and the combinations of powder
and target that were never run. A dose is valid when its run passed quality
screening, the dose ended in a defined controller state, and the auger or
solenoid moved. Two 50~mg doses, on calcium lactate and on coarse silicon, were
first excluded because the firmware logged no auger revolution, but their serial
logs show a short continuous spin that delivered 62 and 55~mg, so we count them
as valid overshoots. Table~\ref{tbl:doses} summarizes the valid doses by powder
and target mass.

Fig.~\ref{fgr:dosetraces} shows balance readings during representative doses of
a fast and a slow powder. Doses that finished within the limit spent about half
their time in the tap phase (median 46\% at 50~mg, 50\% at 200~mg, and 53\% at
1~g). On the free-flowing powders, one 45$^\circ$ auger turn delivered a median of
30~mg, against 2~mg on the slow powders. The last step of a 50~mg dose on a
free-flowing powder added a median of 46~mg (range 8--100~mg), more than the
whole $\pm$5~mg acceptance band, so a dose could step right over it. On the slow
powders the last step added at most 9~mg, and doses stopped near the lower edge
of the band. In 19 of the 60 valid 50 and 200~mg doses, the controller began
with a brief continuous spin that delivered up to 103~mg. Its bulk phase started
whenever the tared reading sat a fraction of a milligram below zero, because the
threshold for that phase equalled the target. Per-dose phase times and final
increments are in \texttt{paper/figures/data/dose\_phase\_summary.csv}.

\section{Run inventory}
\label{sec:runs}

Table~\ref{tbl:runs} lists every run recorded in the two test rounds, including
runs that were aborted, postponed, or excluded, so that each number in the main
text can be traced to the run it came from.

The test battery's raw records store tilt as a servo setting (0, 45, or 90)
rather than as an angle. The tilt plate is geared 2:1, so these settings are
tube angles of 0$^\circ$, 22.5$^\circ$, and 45$^\circ$ above horizontal, and
45$^\circ$ is the steepest tilt reached in any test. The main text and its
figures report tube angles; the conversion is made
once, in \texttt{paper/figures/make\_data\_figures.py}.

\section{AI usage log}
\label{sec:ailog}

In line with \textit{Digital Discovery}'s guidance on the use of LLMs, all AI
interactions are preserved publicly in the repository:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{CAD and code generation:} every coding-agent session (GitHub
Copilot coding agent and Claude-family models, 2026) is recorded as a pull
request containing the prompt (issue text and review comments), the generated
CAD code, rendered views, and the human review thread.
\item \textbf{Design reviews and literature queries:} Edison Scientific query
scripts, task identifiers, raw answers, and extracted references are archived
with the corresponding branches; the consolidated bibliography, with the
verification status of each entry, is \texttt{paper/references\_validation.md}.
\item \textbf{Text-to-CAD evaluations:} CADSmith run artifacts and the Zoo
Design Studio (Zookeeper agent) session transcripts and notes are preserved on
their branches.
\item \textbf{Analysis and writing:} the data-analysis scripts behind every
figure, and the AI-drafted revisions of this manuscript, are in
\texttt{paper/} with their review threads.
\item \textbf{Qualitative observations:} the team's running journal of AI
behaviour, good and bad, is discussion~\#39 in the repository.
\end{itemize}

Fig.~\ref{fgr:aiusage} shows how agent use was spread over the project. Team
members addressed the two coding agents 754 times between April and September
2026, and the agents authored 789 of the 877 commits (90\%). The counts cover
all work in the repository (CAD, firmware, data analysis, and writing), not only
the design of the parts. The team moved from the GitHub Copilot coding agent to
Claude at the start of July 2026.
