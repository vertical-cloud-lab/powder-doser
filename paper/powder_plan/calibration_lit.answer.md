# Calibration and Performance Validation of a Gravimetric Powder Micro-Dosing Device: Literature-Supported Protocols and Metrics

## 1. Standards-Based Gravimetric Test Design

No single ISO or pharmacopoeial standard directly prescribes a validation protocol for discrete-dose gravimetric powder dispensing in the 20 mg–5 g range. However, several frameworks converge to provide a defensible analogue.

**ISO 8655 as the open-hardware precedent.** ISO 8655-6 describes the gravimetric reference measurement procedure for piston-operated volumetric apparatus, and ISO 8655-2 specifies maximum permissible systematic error and random error at the nominal volume, 50%, and 10% of nominal volume, with n ≥ 10 replicates (brennan2018opendesign3dprintable pages 3-7, brennan2018opendesign3dprintable pages 7-9). This exact framework has been adopted by multiple open-source hardware papers published in Digital Discovery and related venues. The digital pipette v2 (Yoshikawa et al., 2026) evaluated dispensing at 1 mL, 5 mL, and 10 mL with n = 10 replicates per volume, reporting systematic error (hs, %) and random error (Cv, %); the device achieved systematic errors of −0.09% to −0.49% and random errors of 0.10% to 0.58%, well below the ISO 8655-2 maximum permissible errors of ±0.6% systematic / ±0.3% random at 10 mL and ±6.0% / ±3.0% at 1 mL (yoshikawa2026commitdigitalpipette pages 3-4). Brennan et al. (2018) validated a 3D-printed micropipette at four volumes per configuration with n = 5 replicates, with ISO 8655 limits of ±8 µL systematic and ±3 µL random error for a 1000-µL nominal pipette (brennan2018opendesign3dprintable pages 3-7, brennan2018opendesign3dprintable pages 7-9). The FINDUS open-source liquid handler (Barthels et al., 2020) tested at 5–6 nominal volumes per pipette with n = 5 and confirmed compliance with ISO 8655 limits of 1.4–2.8% permissible error (barthels2020findusanopensource pages 5-7, barthels2020findusanopensource pages 7-9).

**Adaptation to powder dosing.** For powder, the closest analogues are: (i) the ISO 5725 framework (accuracy = trueness + precision, decomposed into repeatability and reproducibility); (ii) pharmaceutical loss-in-weight (LIW) feeder qualification, which uses relative standard deviation (RSD) and relative error (RE, the relative deviation of mean feed rate from setpoint) as standard performance metrics (bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 6-10, bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 14-18); and (iii) USP ⟨41⟩ / ⟨1251⟩ for balance qualification, which defines the minimum weighable quantity as the mass at which the balance's repeatability contribution to measurement uncertainty does not exceed a defined fraction (typically 0.10% of the reading for USP). For a 0.1 mg readability balance, the USP minimum weight at 0.10% tolerance is approximately 82 mg (assuming a standard deviation of repeated measurements ~0.041 mg and a 2× safety factor). Doses approaching 20 mg will therefore require careful uncertainty assessment.

**Recommended target-mass ladder.** By analogy with the ISO 8655 structure (100%, 50%, 10% of nominal range), and recognizing the logarithmic span from 20 mg to 5 g, a five- or six-point target-mass ladder is recommended: 20 mg (lower limit), 50 mg, 100 mg, 500 mg, 1 g, and 5 g (upper limit), with n = 10 replicates per target per powder. Not all powders will be testable at all masses; the protocol should allow justified exclusion of target masses below the practical operating limit with documentation of the reason (e.g., balance noise dominating, auger jamming).

**Acceptance limits.** Drawing from LIW feeder literature where RSD < 5% is considered acceptable for steady-state feeding (li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 53-58), and from ISO 8655 where permissible errors widen at smaller fractions of nominal volume, we recommend: systematic error ≤ ±5% for target masses ≥ 100 mg, ≤ ±10% for target masses < 100 mg; random error (CV) ≤ 5% across the range; overshoot rate < 10% of deliveries.

## 2. Per-Powder Feed-Factor Calibration

**Displacement-feed-factor profiles.** The most directly analogous calibration approach for an auger-based doser is the "displacement-feed factor" method developed by Fathollahi et al. (2020), in which the effective displacement density (mass delivered per unit volumetric displacement) is measured as a function of displacement position along the powder column (fathollahi2020performanceevaluationof pages 1-2, fathollahi2020performanceevaluationof pages 2-3). For each material tested (lactose, dicalcium phosphate, croscarmellose sodium, GranuLac200, silicon dioxide, and two industrial APIs), a characteristic density-displacement curve was obtained. Critically, this curve was shown to be independent of feed rate (displacement speed) for a given material, meaning that once measured at one rate it can be used to control the system at any rate within the tested range (1–20 g/h) (fathollahi2020performanceevaluationof pages 7-11, fathollahi2020performanceevaluationof pages 11-12).

**Feed factor vs. conditioned bulk density.** Li (2020) demonstrated a linear relationship between conditioned bulk density and feed factor (maximum mass throughput at 100% drive command) for a K-Tron KT20 LIW feeder across 10 materials and four screw types, with R² values exceeding 0.90 (li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 49-53, li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 53-58). This means that for your auger doser, a single conditioned bulk density measurement on ~10 mL of powder should provide a first-order estimate of mass per revolution, which can then be validated experimentally.

**Fill-level drift and densification.** Feed factor decays as the hopper empties because the reduced powder mass above the screw inlet decreases compressive stress and therefore powder density at the screw (bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 6-10, bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 22-26). Bostijn et al. (2019) defined "feed factor decay" (FFdecay) as the hopper fill level at which the feed factor drops to 90% of FFmax, and found that it was positively correlated with powder compressibility (Hausner ratio, Carr index, compressibility at 15 kPa) (bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 22-26). Powders with high compressibility (e.g., Avicel 105, Hausner ratio 1.54; powder APAP, Hausner ratio 1.67) showed earlier and more pronounced feed factor decay (li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 53-58).

**Feed-forward and iterative learning control.** Fathollahi et al. (2021) demonstrated that fitting a polynomial to the displacement-feed-factor profile and using it to proactively adjust piston (or auger) speed reduced the relative deviation of mean from setpoint (RDMtS) to below 7% for feed-forward control, and to below 1.5% when combined with iterative learning control using a corridor of ±7.5% around the setpoint (fathollahi2021developmentofa pages 12-13, fathollahi2021developmentofa pages 10-12, fathollahi2021developmentofa pages 7-10).

**Recommended calibration procedure for your system.** For each powder: (1) measure conditioned bulk density to predict approximate mass per revolution; (2) perform a single calibration run at a mid-range auger speed recording mass delivered vs. cumulative auger revolutions, from which the displacement-feed-factor curve is obtained; (3) fit a polynomial to this curve; (4) validate at 2–3 additional speeds/tilt angles to confirm rate-independence; (5) document the fill level at which feed factor drops to 90% of its maximum (FFdecay equivalent). Recalibration should be performed whenever a new batch of powder is used or after extended storage that may alter moisture or packing state.

## 3. Statistical Metrics and Reporting

**Systematic vs. random error.** Following ISO 8655 convention and the pharmaceutical feeder literature, report at each target mass: (a) systematic error as the mean delivered mass minus target mass (in mg and as %), and (b) random error as the standard deviation (SD, mg) and coefficient of variation (CV, %) (brennan2018opendesign3dprintable pages 3-7, yoshikawa2026commitdigitalpipette pages 3-4). At small doses (< 100 mg), absolute SD becomes more meaningful than CV because CV inflates as the denominator shrinks; both should be tabulated.

**RSD and RE from feeder literature.** The standard metrics for continuous feeder qualification are RSD (relative standard deviation of feed rate) and RE (relative error: |mean − setpoint| / setpoint × 100%) (bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 6-10, bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 14-18). For your discrete-dose system, the analogues are CV and systematic error, respectively. Li (2020) identified an acceptable operating region where both RSD and RDM (relative deviation from mean) were minimized, typically at drive commands between 20–90% for most screw types (li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 49-53, li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 53-58).

**Overshoot as an asymmetric error metric.** Because powder cannot be removed once dispensed, overshoot is an irreversible error that must be reported separately from undershoot. Report the overshoot rate (fraction of doses exceeding target mass), mean overshoot magnitude when it occurs, and the maximum single-dose overshoot. This metric is not standard in ISO 8655 (which assumes symmetric liquid dispensing errors) but is essential for powder dosing credibility.

**Dose-time reporting.** Report median and 95th-percentile dose completion times for each powder × target mass combination, since throughput matters for SDL applications.

**Minimum weighable quantity.** With a 0.1 mg readability balance, the USP ⟨41⟩ minimum weight criterion (requiring the sample mass to be such that the balance's repeatability standard deviation contributes ≤ 0.10% measurement uncertainty) sets a practical lower bound near 80–100 mg for high-confidence gravimetric verification. At 20 mg, the balance repeatability (~0.05 mg SD typical for a 0.1 mg readability balance) contributes ~0.25% uncertainty, which is acceptable but should be explicitly stated in the uncertainty budget.

**Uncertainty budget.** For a complete uncertainty budget, combine: (i) balance repeatability (SD from ≥ 10 tare/weigh cycles); (ii) balance linearity/calibration uncertainty; (iii) buoyancy correction (negligible for powder but included for completeness); (iv) evaporation/moisture uptake during the dosing interval (record mass drift rate on an empty vessel); (v) vibration-coupled noise from the ERM motor and solenoid tap; (vi) electrostatic offset (observed as non-monotonic mass readings).

## 5. Environmental and Material Covariates

**Humidity and hygroscopicity.** Humidity affects cohesion through liquid bridging forces and is particularly relevant for NaCl (deliquescence above ~75% RH), rice flours, xanthan gum, and sodium alginate. Record ambient temperature and relative humidity for every test session, and condition powders at a defined RH (e.g., 40 ± 5% RH, 22 ± 2 °C) before testing. Fathollahi et al. (2020) noted that silicon dioxide was difficult to handle due to its "low density, high cohesion, and electrostatic properties," with potential material loss during experimental execution (fathollahi2020performanceevaluationof pages 5-7).

**Electrostatic charging.** Fine silicon powder and fumed silica are prone to triboelectric charging during auger transport, which causes powder adhesion to walls, erratic discharge, and balance reading artifacts. Schmidt (2022) noted that at low humidity, condensed water layers on particles are thin, aggravating charge accumulation and electrification during powder dosing and spreading (shah2023aconcisesummary pages 10-11). Grounding the auger housing and reporting qualitative static observations (adhesion to walls, airborne loss) is recommended.

**Fumed silica as glidant.** Abe et al. (2009) demonstrated that 0.5 wt% colloidal silicon dioxide was optimal for improving the flowability of API powders (reducing angle of repose from ~55° to ~47.5°), while 1.0 wt% was optimal for excipient blends (abe2009improvingpowderflow pages 3-5). Shah et al. (2023) reviewed that silica reduces van der Waals forces, liquid bridging, electrostatic charge, and mechanical friction between particles, with diminishing returns beyond an optimal concentration, and that hydrophobic silica provides superior flow enhancement compared to hydrophilic variants (shah2023aconcisesummary pages 10-11, shah2023aconcisesummary pages 8-10). For your fine silicon powder (~45 µm), testing at 0%, 0.5%, and 1.0 wt% fumed silica additions and reporting the corresponding dosing metrics would demonstrate the glidant dose-response.

**AlSi10Mg metal powder handling.** AlSi10Mg is susceptible to moisture absorption and surface oxidation (shah2023aconcisesummary pages 10-11). Report powder storage conditions (sealed container, desiccant, inert atmosphere if applicable), time since container opening, and any safety precautions (aluminum powder is combustible; refer to the material SDS). The high density (~2.67 g/cm³ bulk) means the feed factor will be large relative to organic powders, and overshoot risk at large target masses may be elevated.

## 6. Precedents for Validation of Open-Source Dosing Hardware in SDL Contexts

The Digital Discovery journal has established a clear precedent for validating open-source dispensing hardware using ISO 8655-style gravimetric protocols. Yoshikawa et al. (2026) validated their digital pipette v2 at three volumes (1, 5, 10 mL) with systematic errors below −0.49% and random errors below 0.58%, explicitly benchmarked against ISO 8655-2 maximum permissible errors (yoshikawa2026commitdigitalpipette pages 3-4, yoshikawa2026commitdigitalpipette pages 1-2). Brennan et al. (2018) validated a 3D-printed micropipette at four volumes per range with five replicates against ISO 8655 acceptance criteria, achieving compliance after applying a calibration correction scale (brennan2018opendesign3dprintable pages 3-7, brennan2018opendesign3dprintable pages 7-9). The FINDUS liquid handler (Barthels et al., 2020) similarly demonstrated ISO 8655 compliance at 5–6 volumes with permissible errors of 1.4–2.8% (barthels2020findusanopensource pages 5-7, barthels2020findusanopensource pages 7-9).

For powder dispensing specifically, Pelkie et al. (2025) described a ~$300 open-source powder dispensing module using a stepper-motor-driven auger with an integrated balance for feedback control, designed for SDL integration (pelkie2025democratizingselfdrivinglabs pages 3-6). However, detailed quantitative validation data for that device were not yet published at the time of their workshop report (manuscript noted as "in progress"). This represents a direct comparator for your device.

Hou (2024) developed micro-feeders for cohesive pharmaceutical powders that could feed at rates as low as 1 g/h across five different materials, using feeding performance statistics including RSD and relative deviation from setpoint as the primary validation metrics (hou2024developmentofa pages 1-5, hou2024developmentofa pages 5-7).

Collectively, what Digital Discovery-class reviewers expect is: (1) a systematic test at multiple operating points (analogous to volume ladder in ISO 8655), (2) separation of systematic and random error, (3) comparison against an established standard or acceptance criterion, (4) testing across multiple materials/conditions, and (5) open sharing of all design files, code, and raw data.

**Summary of total experimental effort:** The protocol comprises approximately 10 powders × 4–6 target masses × 10 replicates = 400–600 individual dose events, plus calibration runs (1–2 per powder for feed-factor characterization). For the fine silicon powder, additional runs with 0.5% and 1.0% fumed silica blends add ~60–120 further doses. Including NaCl as a daily inter-session repeatability control (e.g., 10 doses at 500 mg at the start of each test day) provides an internal quality control anchor.

**Published datasets for comparison.** The Fathollahi et al. (2020) displacement-feed-factor data for six excipients and two APIs (CapsuLac60, dicalcium phosphate, croscarmellose sodium, GranuLac200, silicon dioxide, API A, SDI B) provide the closest published comparator, including Hausner ratios, particle size distributions, and feeding curves at 1–20 g/h (fathollahi2020performanceevaluationof pages 7-11, fathollahi2020performanceevaluationof pages 5-7, fathollahi2020performanceevaluationof pages 11-12). The Li (2020) dataset of 10 materials across four screw types with RSD and RDM data at six drive command levels offers another benchmark (li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 49-53, li2020predictiveperformanceof pages 53-58). The Bostijn et al. (2019) dataset of 15 pharmaceutical powders with 25 material property descriptors and both volumetric and gravimetric feeding responses provides the most comprehensive material-property-to-feeding-performance correlation dataset available (bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 1-6, bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 22-26, bostijn2019amultivariateapproach pages 26-30).
