Abstract

The mass an auger delivers depends on how it is driven and on the powder, so
every calibration of an auger doser is also a small measurement of how that
powder flows. We treat the calibration of an open-source, 3D-printed auger
doser as multi-objective Bayesian optimization and ask what the calibration
data reveal about the powder. At fixed settings, 13 powders, from food-safe
stand-ins to AlSi10Mg and silicon, delivered between 10 and 231 mg per auger
revolution, and three delivered none; across 12 of them, delivery ranked like
the angle of repose (Spearman ρ = −0.84). On sodium chloride, an optimizer
tuned eight controller settings in an unattended campaign of 42 doses. Its
recommended settings dosed 0.5 g in a median 78 s with a median error
of 1.8 mg over eight repeats, three times faster than hand-tuned settings,
and dosed 8 g of AlSi10Mg to within 0.1% without change. We extend the
formulation to many powders by treating each powder as a related task of
a multi-task model, with added objectives for repeatability and accessible
dose range. A discrete element model of the auger with no fitted parameters
reproduces the measured delivery of salt at the campaign’s centre point (105
versus 105 ± 11 mg per revolution); with measured particle-size distributions,
it is used to infer an effective cohesion and friction for each powder from its
dosing response. [Multi-task calibration cut the doses needed for a new powder by

 1  1. Introduction

 2     Alloy discovery by additive manufacturing (AM) starts with weighing
 3  powders. Each candidate composition is made by blending elemental or
 4  pre-alloyed powders, either in a bed for laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) or in
 5  the feeders of a directed energy deposition (DED) system [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. In
 6  a self-driving laboratory (SDL), where a planning algorithm chooses each next
 7  composition and robots make and test it [7, 8, 9], that weighing step must be
 8  automatic, accurate, and fast for every powder in the palette [10, 11, 12, 13].
 9 The same powders must later spread into thin, uniform layers, and how well
10  they do so depends on the same properties that make them easy or hard to
11  dose: particle size and shape, cohesion, and friction [14, 15, 16, 17].
12     Measuring those properties is slow and often uninformative. Hall and
13  Carney funnels report a single flow time and cannot test cohesive powders,
14  which do not flow through them at all [18, 19].  Shear cells and powder
15  rheometers give richer data, but each test needs tens of millilitres of powder,
16  a dedicated instrument, and a trained operator [20, 21]. Rotating drums
17  and spreadability tests come closer to the conditions in an AM machine
18  [22, 23, 24, 25], yet different flow tests often rank the same powders differently,
19  and none of them predicts spreading reliably on its own [18, 19, 24]. For
20  an SDL that cycles through dozens of feedstocks, including small batches of
21  reactive or expensive powders, a separate characterization campaign for each
22  powder is rarely affordable.
23    Powder feeders, by contrast, are characterized all the time, because each
24 new powder must be calibrated before it can be dosed. In pharmaceutical
25  manufacturing, the feed factor of a screw feeder, the mass it delivers per screw
26  revolution, has been predicted from bulk density, compressibility, and shear-
27  cell data [26, 27, 28], and loss-in-weight feeders have standard methods for
28  measuring how their output varies [29, 30, 31]. If material properties predict
29  feeder behaviour, the inverse should also hold: a feeder that is driven through
30  a designed set of conditions, and weighed, measures the powder. Small-scale

31  dosers make this attractive because the measurement uses milligrams to
32  grams of powder and happens anyway, every time a new powder is loaded
33  [32, 33, 34, 35, 36].
34     Calibration of this kind is an optimization problem with several competing
35  goals. A dose should be accurate and repeatable, it should be fast, and the
36  settings should work across the range of doses the workflow needs. Overshoot
37  cannot be undone, because powder cannot be taken back out of the cup, so
38  error in one direction costs more than in the other. Bayesian optimization
39 (BO) suits such problems: it fits a probabilistic model to the doses made so far
40  and chooses the next settings where the expected gain is largest [37, 38, 39].
41  Multi-objective BO finds the trade-off, or Pareto front, between goals such as
42  time and error [40], and sparse priors make it practical with many settings
43  and few experiments [41]. Each new powder, however, still starts from scratch.
44  Multi-task BO treats related problems, here different powders on the same
45  doser, as tasks of one model, so that data from earlier powders inform the
46  first doses of a new one [42, 43, 44]. How strongly two powders are related
47  is then a learned quantity, and it is natural to ask whether it reflects their
48  physical properties.
49     Physics-based models can answer that question directly. The discrete
50  element method (DEM) simulates every grain and its contacts [45, 46], has
51  been used to predict the output of screw conveyors [47], and represents
52  cohesion and rolling friction with contact laws whose parameters are hard to
53  measure [48, 49, 50]. These parameters are usually calibrated by matching
54  simulations to bulk tests, which is itself an inverse problem [51, 52, 53, 54],
55  best treated with the tools of Bayesian calibration of computer models [55, 56].
56 DEM is also the standard tool for studying how powders spread into AM
57  layers [57, 58, 15, 59]. An auger that is turned at controlled speeds and tilts
58   is a well-defined shear and discharge test, so a DEM model of the auger could
59  convert dosing data into contact parameters that a spreading model can then
60  use.
61     In this work we build on an open-source, 3D-printed auger doser [60]
62  and make three contributions. First, we formulate per-powder calibration
63  as multi-objective BO over the doser’s controller settings and demonstrate
64   it on sodium chloride, where an eight-dimensional search ran unattended
65  (Section 3.2). Second, we show that the doser’s response at fixed settings
66  separates 13 powders over a 24-fold range, and we extend calibration to many
67  powders as a multi-task problem whose objectives also include repeatability
68  and the range of doses a powder can reach (Sections 3.1 and 3.3). Third,

 74  2. Materials and methods

 75  2.1. Doser and dose procedure
 76    The doser is a single channel of an open-source design described in detail
 77  elsewhere [60, 61]; the parts that matter here are summarized below. A
 78  one-piece auger, printed in PLA on a Bambu Lab H2D printer (0.2 mm
 79  layers), serves as both reservoir and screw: a tube of 21 mm bore and 250 mm
 80  length holds up to 58 mL of powder, and a single-start flight (0.5 mm thick,
 81  10.4 mm pitch, 8 turns) runs over the last 84 mm before a 12 mm conical
 82  outlet with a 3 mm exit hole. A NEMA-11 stepper motor (11HS18-0674S,
 83  Pololu Tic T500 driver) turns the auger through a 20:44 gear pair, up to about
 84  109 rpm. Two servos tilt the auger about a hinge near the outlet through a
 85  2:1 gear, and a push–pull solenoid (JF-0530B, 60 ms pulses) taps the tube to
 86  free powder held at the lip. Powder falls into a cup on an analytical balance
 87 (A&D HR-100A; 0.1 mg readability, 102 g capacity) that reports over RS-232.
 88 A Raspberry Pi Pico W runs the dosing firmware (MicroPython), and a
 89  Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W next to the rig runs each dose, records it, and uploads
 90   it to a MongoDB database. All tilts below are angles of the auger axis above
 91  horizontal.
 92   A dose has three stages (Fig. 1a).  In the bulk stage the auger turns
 93  continuously at a set tilt and speed, optionally while the solenoid taps at
 94  2 Hz, until the mass still to go falls below a switch threshold. The firmware
 95  stops the auger early by the amount it expects to arrive after the stop, using a
 96  first-order afterflow model with time constant τaf applied to a Kalman-filtered
 97  estimate of the flow rate. In the trickle stage the auger runs slowly (typically
 98  2–15 rpm) under a proportional–integral controller on flow rate. In the tap
 99  stage the auger stops, the tube moves to a third tilt, and single taps free a few
100  milligrams at a time until the reading lies within a stopping band below the
101  target. The dose is scored on a balance reading taken 2 s after the last action.
102  Doses that exceed the target by more than 100 mg are aborted, and doses that
103  use up their tap budget end as jams. Every dose writes one machine-readable

119  2.3. Gravimetric response at fixed settings
120    Each powder was first run through a fixed battery of test protocols,
121  described fully with the hardware [60]. The ones used here are protocol C,
122  six single auger revolutions at 30 rpm at each of three tilts (0, 22.5 and 45◦),
123  each followed by a settled balance reading; protocol D, three revolutions at
124  15, 45 and 90 rpm at 22.5◦; protocol E, single taps after a re-feed revolution;
125  and protocol G, three closed-loop doses of 1 g with the hand-tuned controller.
126  From these we extract a short dosing fingerprint for each powder: the delivery
127  per revolution at the three tilts, its scatter from revolution to revolution,
                             α128  the exponent α in ˙m ∝N  for auger speed N, the mass freed by a single
129  tap, and the afterflow time constant τaf fitted from the mass that arrives
130  after each stop. A full battery uses 4–10 g of powder and takes 40–50 min.
131 Two further descriptors were proposed for screening atomized batches [61]: a
132  gravity-assisted volumetric flow, the delivery per revolution at 45◦divided
133  by bulk density and normalized to a reference AlSi10Mg powder, and an
134  interruption fraction, the share of balance increments at 15 rpm that show
135  no flow, which is a doser analogue of the cohesive index of a rotating drum
136  [62, 22].

137  2.4. Calibration as multi-objective Bayesian optimization
138     For one powder and one target mass, calibration searches eight controller
139  settings (Table 2) to minimize two objectives: the total time of a dose, t, and
140  its absolute error, |e|, both measured on the scored balance reading. Doses
141  slower than 180 s or more than 20 mg off count as failures; these limits form
142  the reference point for the hypervolume, the area of objective space that the
143  best doses so far dominate. All other controller parameters, including the
144  controller gains and the overshoot guard, were frozen at the values used in
145  the hand-tuned controller.
146    The campaign ran in four phases. First, four doses at the hand-tuned
147  settings gave a baseline. Second, a two-level fractional factorial screening
148  design of resolution IV (28−4, 16 corners) and four centre points measured
149  main effects without confounding them with two-factor interactions. Third,
150  the afterflow constant τaf was refitted from the 26 stop events recorded so
151  far (a robust median-ratio estimate; τaf = 0.834 s), and the centre point
152  and hand-tuned settings were repeated with the new value.  Fourth, 14
153  doses were chosen sequentially by Bayesian optimization in Ax 0.4.3 [63] on
154  BoTorch [64]: a Gaussian-process model with a sparse axis-aligned subspace
155  prior (SAASBO) [41], suited to many settings and few observations, and the

173    Each powder is a task, and all tasks share the eight controller settings and
174  the dose record. A multi-task Gaussian process [42, 43] models the objectives

176  where the positive semi-definite matrix B is learned from the data and its
177  normalized entries are the correlations between powders. Two variants will
178  be compared: an index kernel, which learns B freely, and a descriptor kernel,
179  in which B is computed from each powder’s dosing fingerprint (Section 2.3)

190    The benchmark has two parts. Retrospectively, each completed single-task
191  campaign is held out in turn, a surrogate fitted to its data stands in for the
192  rig, and multi-task optimization on the held-out powder, informed by the
193  other powders, is compared with single-task SAASBO, quasi-random (Sobol)
194  search, and simply carrying over the best settings of the most similar powder.
195  Prospectively, one new powder, a freshly atomized batch, is calibrated with
196  the multi-task model. The figure of merit is the number of doses until the
197  recommended settings reach 90% of the best hypervolume known for that
198  powder. [Planned campaigns: AlSi10Mg, 316L stainless steel, sieved Al 4047

202  fine powder, a master alloy will be used instead.

203  2.6. Discrete element model of the auger
204   A discrete element model of the auger outlet was built in LIGGGHTS-
205 PUBLIC 3.8 [65, 45]. Grains interact through a Hertz normal contact with
206  history-dependent tangential friction and an elastic–plastic spring–dashpot
207  rolling resistance model [50], and cohesion is added as a simplified Johnson–
208  Kendall–Roberts (SJKR) term, a pull-off force proportional to the cohesion
209  energy density and the contact area [48]. For salt, the grains follow a three-
210  size distribution around a median diameter of 0.425 mm (number fractions
211  0.3/0.4/0.3 at 0.85, 1 and 1.15 times the median) with density 2165 kg m−3, a
212  literature value for the material class. Young’s modulus is reduced to 0.2 MPa
213  to allow a time step of 2.1 × 10−5 s, which keeps mean overlaps below 0.4%
214  of a diameter; Poisson’s ratio is 0.3, the coefficient of restitution 0.5, sliding
215  friction 0.5 between grains and 0.4 against the wall, and rolling friction 0.3.
216 None of these parameters was fitted to the dosing data. The geometry is

                                    8

217  meshed from the CAD model of the printed auger: the conical outlet with
218  its 3 mm exit, the solid core whose tip leaves a 1.07 mm annular gap at the
219  exit, and the lowest 1.5 turns of the flight, filled with about 29,000 grains and
220  topped up from a feed zone. Tilt is applied by rotating gravity, rotation by
221  turning the auger mesh, and the delivered mass is counted as grains leave the
222  exit.
223    To infer effective contact parameters for a powder, the model will be run
224  over a designed set of cohesion energy densities, sliding and rolling friction
225  coefficients, with the particle-size distribution fixed at the measured one, at
226  the tilts and speeds of the dosing fingerprint. A Gaussian-process emulator
227  of these runs then replaces the simulator in a Bayesian calibration [55, 56],
228  which returns a posterior distribution over the contact parameters given the
229  measured fingerprint and its scatter, with a discrepancy term for systematic
230  model error. Because dosing data alone may not separate cohesion from
231  friction [51, 52, 66], the identifiability of each parameter will be checked on
232  synthetic data before the inference is run on measured data, and grain counts
233  for the finer metal powders will be reduced by coarse-graining that preserves
234  the ratio of cohesive to gravitational forces [67]. [Emulator design (number of
235  runs, parameter ranges) to be fixed after the friction and cohesion sweeps in the
236  rig geometry.]

237  2.7. Independent powder characterization
238    To test whether the dosing-derived descriptors and inferred parameters
239  carry physical meaning, the same lots will be characterized by standard
240  methods [68]: particle-size distribution by laser diffraction [69] or sieving,
241  bulk and tapped density and the Hausner ratio [70], Hall-flow time [71] or,
242  for powders that do not pass the Hall funnel, Carney-flow time [72], angle
243  of repose, and the flow function coefficient ffc from a shear cell [73, 20].
244  Spreadability will be assessed by blade-spreading single layers of 50–100 µm
245  and measuring areal mass and coverage [24, 25]. [Shear-cell measurements are
246  expected to be outsourced; sieves have been ordered.]

260  3. Results

261  3.1. Dosing response separates powders
262    At fixed settings the auger delivered very different amounts of different
263  powders (Fig. 2a, Table 1). Ten of the 13 powders delivered between 9.6 mg
264 (sodium alginate) and 231 mg (AlSi10Mg) per revolution at 22.5◦, a 24-fold
265  range, and three delivered no measurable amount: fumed silica, brown rice
266  flour, and silicon finer than 44 µm. The two silicon grades are the clearest
267  case. They are the same material, yet the 75–150 µm grade delivered 211 mg


                                   10

=== PDF PAGE 13 ===
268  per revolution and the finer grade delivered nothing in 17 of 18 revolutions,
269  so particle size alone switched the flow on and off.
270     Across all powders and tilts, powder identity accounted for 70% of the
271  variance of the logarithm of the mass per revolution, tilt for 20%, their
272  interaction for 4%, and repeat-to-repeat scatter for 6%. Tilting the auger
273  from 0 to 22.5◦raised delivery by a median factor of 4.2, and tilting further
274  to 45◦by only 1.2 more; carboxymethyl cellulose was the only powder that
275  delivered less at 45◦than at 22.5◦. Delivery rose more slowly than auger speed
276  for most powders (median exponent α = 0.78), but faster than speed for the
277  three slowest (white rice flour 1.36, carboxymethyl cellulose 1.13, sodium
278  alginate 1.08). A single tap freed between 0.24 mg (sodium alginate) and
279  20 mg (calcium lactate), and the afterflow time constant ranged from 0.78 to
280  1.17 s across six powders. Each of these numbers comes from data that any
281  calibration of the doser collects anyway.
282    The same powders also differ in how well a fixed controller doses them
283  (Fig. 2b). With one set of settings tuned by hand on salt, seven powders
284  landed within ±5% of a 1 g target on every dose (24 of 24), and the three
285  alloy-relevant ones (AlSi10Mg, coarse silicon and sodium sulfate) within 1%.
286 The two slowest-flowing powders ran out of time: white rice flour delivered
287  0.84–0.89 g and sodium alginate 0.69–0.73 g. Even among powders that met
288  the limit, the median time for 1 g ranged from 65 s for AlSi10Mg to 11 min
289  for carboxymethyl cellulose. A controller tuned for one powder is therefore
290  accurate on similar powders but slow or short on different ones, which is the
291  case for calibrating each powder.

292  3.2. Bayesian calibration on salt
293    The salt campaign ran 42 doses of 0.5 g unattended in less than 2.5 h
294  (Fig. 3). The four doses at the hand-tuned settings took 180–343 s (median
295  245 s) and ended 3.6–4.8 mg short. The screening design showed which
296  settings mattered and why. Tapping during the bulk stage cut the average
297  time of successful doses from 186 to 79 s, but raised the number of overshoots
298  from two to five of eight corners, because taps free extra powder after the

397  4. Discussion

398  Calibration data as a flow measurement. The doser measures a powder under
399  conditions close to those of its use:  a small sample, moving through a
400  narrow gap under gravity, shear and vibration. Every quantity in the dosing
401  fingerprint is collected during routine calibration, from 4–10 g of powder and
402  in less than an hour, and the same measurement works inside a glove box,
403  where Hall funnels and shear cells are awkward to use. The fingerprint has
404  two further advantages over funnel tests. It does not fail on cohesive powders:
405  a powder that will not pass a Hall funnel still produces a tilt response, a
406  tap quantum and an afterflow constant, and complete absence of flow is
407  itself a result [18, 74]. And it has several dimensions, so it can separate
408  effects that a single flow time combines. The correlation with the angle of
409  repose and the failure of particle size to predict delivery across materials
410  (Section 3.5) agree with studies of pharmaceutical screw feeders, in which
411  bulk density and cohesion-related properties, rather than size, predicted feed
412  rate [26, 27, 28, 75]. The literature values used here are class estimates,
413  however, not measurements of our lots, and a correlation of ranks across 12
414  very different materials shows only that the doser orders powders sensibly.
415 Whether it can resolve the smaller differences between batches of one alloy,
416  which matter most for feedstock quality control in AM, remains to be shown
417  [(Section 3.5)].

418 What Bayesian optimization contributes. On salt, the optimizer found settings
419  three times faster than hand tuning with less error, in one unattended
420  afternoon. Two lessons from the campaign shape the multi-powder design.
421  First, repeatability has to be an objective, not an afterthought. The fastest
422  dose on the observed front came from settings whose time varied eightfold
423  between repeats, because the powder that falls after the auger stops varies
424  from dose to dose. A noise-aware acquisition function [40], a recommendation
425  based on the model’s averaged prediction, and a validation block together
426  guarded against this, but an explicit repeatability objective, estimated from
427  replicate doses, would let the optimizer trade it against speed directly. Second,
428  the recommended settings sat at the edges of the search space, so the ranges
429  were set too conservatively, which is easy to fix but should be expected for
430  every new powder. The screening design was worth its 20 doses: it explained,
431  in physical terms, why taps during the bulk stage speed doses up but cause
432  overshoots, and it gave the model a balanced starting set. Because overshoot


                                   15

=== PDF PAGE 18 ===
433  is irreversible, the current formulation’s symmetric absolute error should
434  become a one-sided penalty with a constraint on the probability of overshoot,
435  as the formulation for the four-objective campaigns does (Section 2.5).

436  Sharing calibration across powders. That the salt settings dosed AlSi10Mg
437  to within 0.1% suggests that powders which flow freely share most of their
438  calibration, and that a multi-task model can start a new free-flowing powder
439  close to its optimum. Cohesive and hygroscopic powders, which deliver an
440  order of magnitude less per revolution and respond differently to tilt and
441  speed, are less likely to benefit from the salt data, and a model that learns
442  task correlations decides this from data rather than by assumption. Using
443  the dosing fingerprint to compute the task correlations would also let the
444  model place a new powder before its first optimized dose [44]. [Results of the
445  multi-task benchmark (Section 3.3) to be discussed here.]

446  From dosing data to contact parameters. The discrete element model reproduced
447  salt delivery at one setting without calibration and followed its trend with
448   tilt, which suggests that its geometry and contact laws capture the main
449  mechanism. Its errors at other settings, and its strong sensitivity to the exit
450  gap and the grain size, mean that inference must start from a measured exit
451  geometry and a measured particle-size distribution, or those uncertainties
452  will be absorbed into the inferred cohesion and friction [51, 66]. The inferred
453  parameters will also be effective rather than intrinsic: with a softened Young’s
454  modulus, the cohesive force in the SJKR model depends on the contact
455  area and hence on the chosen stiffness, so inferred cohesion energies can be
456  compared across powders only at a fixed numerical stiffness [49]. Triboelectric
457  charging, which visibly affected the fumed silica and carboxymethyl cellulose,
458   is not represented in the model [76]. If these caveats are respected, the same
459  contact parameters can drive DEM models of powder spreading, which are the
460  standard tool for predicting layer quality in powder bed fusion [57, 58, 15, 59].

461  Limits of the present data. All doses so far were made in air, on one auger
462  geometry, and the optimization covers one powder at one target mass. Barium
463  chloride caked after humidity cycling, and the correlation of dose error
464  with moisture content shows that ambient humidity matters for hygroscopic
465  powders. The tap quantum and the noise of the balance set a floor on
466  accuracy: on salt at the recommended settings a single tap moved 1.5–3.6 mg,
467  so a tolerance of a few milligrams is near the limit of this outlet, and finer
468  doses need a smaller terminal increment rather than a better controller [61].

469  Finally, every powder was supplied commercially or made in our laboratory
470  without a recorded lot analysis, which is why measured properties of each lot
471  are part of the work that remains.

472  Implications for additive manufacturing. In an alloy-discovery workflow the
473  doser is calibrated on every powder anyway, so the measurements described
474  here come at no extra cost in powder or time. Stored with the dose records,
475  the fingerprint, the validated settings and the inferred contact parameters for
476  each lot form a feedstock record that can flag a batch that flows unlike its
477  predecessors, as the clogged Al 4047 batch would have been flagged before it
478  reached a printer. [Whether that record predicts spreading and packing in the
479  powder bed will be tested on blade-spread layers (Section 2.7).]

480  5. Conclusions

481   We treated the calibration of an open-source auger doser as multi-objective
482  Bayesian optimization and asked what the calibration data say about the
483  powder. On salt, an unattended campaign of 42 doses found settings that
484  dosed 0.5 g in a median 78 s with a median error of 1.8 mg over eight repeats,
485  three times faster than hand-tuned settings, and those settings dosed 8 g
486  of AlSi10Mg to within 0.1% without change. At fixed settings, 13 powders
487  spanned a 24-fold range in delivery per revolution, three did not flow at all,
488  and the delivery ranked powders like their angle of repose. A discrete element
489  model of the auger, with no fitted parameters, reproduced the measured
490  delivery of salt at one setting and its trend with tilt. [Multi-task calibration
491  across N powders reduced the doses needed for a new powder by X%, and effective
492  cohesion and friction inferred from dosing data tracked shear-cell and Hall-flow
493  measurements.] Because calibration is a step every dosing workflow already
494  performs, it can double as a characterization of each feedstock, at no extra
495  cost in powder or time.
