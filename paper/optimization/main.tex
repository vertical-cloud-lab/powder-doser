\begin{abstract}
Alloy development for powder bed fusion and directed energy deposition begins by weighing many feedstock powders, and an automated powder doser has to be recalibrated whenever the powder changes.
This work treats that calibration, for an open-source, 3D-printed auger doser, as multi-objective Bayesian optimization of eight controller settings that minimizes dose time and dose error, and asks what the calibration records reveal about the powder.
On sodium chloride, an unattended campaign of 42 doses found settings that, repeated eight times a week later, dosed 0.5~g in a median of 78~s (95\% confidence interval 69--110~s) with a median error of 1.8~mg, against 245~s and 4.6~mg for hand-tuned settings (exact Mann--Whitney $p = 0.004$ for both).
The best single screening dose did not repeat.
A second campaign that also searched the controller's hand-over margin found no effect of the margin, and was halted when the salt feed fell fivefold within one session.
Unchanged, the salt settings dosed 8~g of gas-atomized AlSi10Mg within 7.2~mg.
At the same settings an unsieved Al~4047 powder from the laboratory's atomizer fed 13 times more slowly and stalled until oversized particles were removed, so the dose record flagged a feedstock problem.
Across 12 powders, delivery per auger revolution ranked like the literature angle of repose (Spearman $\rho = -0.84$).
\ph{Multi-task calibration across metal powders, and contact parameters inferred with a discrete element model, will be reported here.}
Calibration records, which every automated dosing workflow already produces, can therefore double as a low-cost check of feedstock flow.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Alloy development by additive manufacturing (AM) starts with weighing powders. Each candidate composition is made by blending elemental or pre-alloyed powders, either in the bed of a laser powder bed fusion (PBF-LB) machine or in the feeders of a directed energy deposition (DED) system \cite{Mosallanejad2021Situ,Moorehead2020Throughput,Li2018Combinatorial,Vecchio2021Throughput,Mooraj2024Additive,Nelaturu2024Multi}, or as a charge that is melted and atomized into a new powder. In a self-driving laboratory (SDL), where a planning algorithm chooses each next composition and robots make and test it \cite{Abolhasani2023Rise,Tom2024Self,Seifrid2022Autonomous}, that weighing step must be automatic, accurate and fast for every powder the workflow uses \cite{Szymanski2023Autonomous,Lunt2024Modular,Jiang2023Autonomous,Maffettone2023What}. The same powders must later spread into thin, uniform layers, and how well they do so depends on the same properties that make them easy or hard to dose: particle size and shape, cohesion and friction \cite{Vock2019Powders,Meier2019Critical,Brika2020Influence,Haferkamp2021Influence}.

Measuring those properties is slow and often uninformative. Hall and Carney funnels report a single flow time and cannot test strongly cohesive powders, which do not flow through them at all \cite{Spierings2016Powder,Krantz2009Characterization}. Shear cells and powder rheometers give richer data, but each test needs tens of millilitres of powder, a dedicated instrument and a trained operator \cite{Schulze2021Powders,Freeman2007Measuring}. Rotating drums and spreadability tests come closer to the conditions inside an AM machine \cite{Lumay2012Measuring,Yablokova2015Rheological,Snow2019Development,Cordova2020Measuring}, yet different flow tests often rank the same powders differently, and none of them predicts spreading reliably on its own \cite{Spierings2016Powder,Krantz2009Characterization,Snow2019Development}. For an SDL that cycles through dozens of feedstocks, including small batches of reactive or expensive powders, a separate characterization campaign for each powder is rarely affordable.

Powder feeders, by contrast, are characterized all the time, because each new powder must be calibrated before it can be dosed. In pharmaceutical manufacturing, the feed factor of a screw feeder, the mass it delivers per screw revolution, has been predicted from bulk density, compressibility and shear-cell data \cite{Wang2017Predicting,Tahir2020Development,Bekaert2021Determination}, and loss-in-weight feeders have standard methods for measuring how their output varies \cite{Engisch2012Method,Engisch2015Feedrate,Hanson2018Control}. If material properties predict feeder behaviour, the reverse should also hold: a feeder that is driven through a designed set of conditions, and weighed, measures the powder. Small-scale dosers make this attractive because the measurement uses milligrams to grams of powder and happens anyway, every time a new powder is loaded \cite{Besenhard2015Accuracy,Besenhard2017Micro,Faulhammer2014Dose,Yang2007Metering,Alsenz2011Powderpicking}.

Calibration of this kind is an optimization problem with several competing goals. A dose should be accurate and repeatable, it should be fast, and the settings should work across the range of doses the workflow needs. Overshoot cannot be undone, because powder cannot be taken back out of the cup, so an error in one direction costs more than in the other. Bayesian optimization (BO) suits such problems. It fits a probabilistic model to the doses made so far and chooses the next settings with an acquisition function, a rule that scores candidate settings by their expected gain \cite{Shahriari2016Taking,Liang2021Benchmarking,Baird2026Honegumi}; it has already tuned AM processes autonomously \cite{Deneault2021Toward}. Multi-objective BO finds the trade-off between goals such as time and error, the Pareto front \cite{Daulton2021Parallel}, and priors that favour a few influential settings make it practical with many settings and few experiments \cite{Eriksson2021High}. Each new powder, however, still starts from scratch. Multi-task BO treats related problems, here different powders on the same doser, as tasks of one model, so that data from earlier powders inform the first doses of a new one \cite{Bonilla2008Multi,Swersky2013Multi,Taylor2023Accelerated}. How strongly two powders are related is then a quantity the model learns, and it is natural to ask whether it reflects their physical properties.

Physics-based models can answer that question directly. The discrete element method (DEM) simulates every grain and every contact between grains \cite{Cundall1979Discrete,Zhu2008Discrete}. It has been used to predict the output of screw conveyors \cite{Owen2009Prediction}, and it represents cohesion and rolling friction with contact laws whose parameters are hard to measure directly \cite{Johnson1971Surface,Thakur2014Micromechanical,Ai2011Assessment}. Those parameters are usually calibrated by matching simulations to bulk tests, which is itself an inverse problem \cite{Coetzee2017Review,Richter2020Development,Benvenuti2016Identification,Cheng2019Iterative} and is best treated with the statistical methods developed for calibrating computer models \cite{Kennedy2001Bayesian,Higdon2008Computer}. The closest precedent calibrated DEM parameters of pharmaceutical powders against several bulk responses extracted from auger dosing experiments \cite{ElKassem2021Semi,ElKassem2021Sensitivity}, so inferring contact parameters from a doser is not itself new. What is new here is a doser that optimizes its own settings for each powder and whose records are then reused as a flow measurement of AM feedstock. DEM is also the standard tool for studying how powders spread into AM layers \cite{Haeri2017Optimisation,Nan2018Jamming,Meier2019Critical,Shaheen2021Influence}, so contact parameters inferred from dosing could be passed on to a spreading model.

This work uses an open-source, 3D-printed auger doser whose design, construction and fixed-setting response to 13 powders are reported separately \cite{Charles2026Open}; Section~\ref{sec:doser} repeats the details needed to reproduce the present experiments. Four questions are addressed, each with its own evidence. First, can calibration for one powder run unattended as multi-objective BO, and do the recommended settings hold up in repeat doses (Section~\ref{sec:res-bo})? Second, what limits unattended calibration in practice (Section~\ref{sec:res-margin})? Third, do settings calibrated on one powder transfer to the metal feedstocks of an alloy workflow, and what do the records say when they do not (Section~\ref{sec:res-metal})? Fourth, do dosing records rank powders the way standard flow measures do (Section~\ref{sec:res-props})? Multi-task calibration across powders (Section~\ref{sec:res-mtbo}) and the inference of contact parameters with a DEM model of the auger (Section~\ref{sec:res-dem}) are set out with their first results and the data still needed. All firmware, campaign and analysis code, dose records and simulation inputs are open \cite{PowderDoserRepo}.

\section{Materials and methods}
\label{sec:methods}

Sections~\ref{sec:doser} to~\ref{sec:records} describe the doser, the powders and the dose records. Sections~\ref{sec:bo} to~\ref{sec:production} describe the two calibration campaigns on salt and the doses of metal feedstock. Sections~\ref{sec:fixed} to~\ref{sec:stats} describe the fixed-setting data used for the property correlations, the planned multi-task calibration, the DEM model, the planned characterization of the same powder lots, and the statistics.

\subsection{Doser and dose procedure}
\label{sec:doser}

The doser is one channel of the open-source design in \cite{Charles2026Open,PowderDoserRepo}. A one-piece auger, printed in PLA on a Bambu Lab H2D printer with 0.2~mm layers, serves as both reservoir and screw. Its tube, 21~mm in bore and 250~mm long, holds \ph{58--70~mL; to be measured} of powder, and a single helical flight (0.5~mm thick, 10.4~mm pitch, 8 turns) runs over the last 84~mm before a 12~mm conical outlet with a 3~mm exit hole. \ph{The tube used for each dataset is not recorded; record it and measure its as-printed exit hole and core-tip gap.} A NEMA-11 stepper motor (11HS18-0674S, Pololu Tic T500 driver) turns the auger through a 20:44 gear pair at up to 109~rpm. Two servos tilt the auger about a hinge near the outlet through a 2:1 gear, and a push--pull solenoid (JF-0530B, 60~ms pulses) taps the tube to free powder held at the lip of the outlet. Powder falls through a funnel into a cup on an analytical balance (A\&D HR-100A; 0.1~mg readability, 102~g capacity) that reports each reading over a serial (RS-232) link. A Raspberry Pi Pico~W runs the dosing firmware (MicroPython; firmware versions are recorded with every dose), and a Raspberry Pi Zero~2~W next to the rig runs each dose, records it and uploads the record to a database. All tilts below are angles of the auger axis above horizontal, and all speeds and revolutions are those of the auger.

A dose has three stages. In the \emph{bulk} stage the auger turns continuously at a set tilt and speed, optionally while the solenoid taps at 2~Hz, until the mass still to go falls to a switch threshold plus a fixed 50~mg allowance for powder that is still falling. In the \emph{trickle} stage the auger runs slowly (typically 2--15~rpm) under a proportional--integral controller on the flow rate, which a Kalman filter estimates from the noisy balance readings. The trickle stops when the predicted final mass comes within a hand-over margin of the target (35~mg unless stated); the prediction is the current mass estimate, plus the afterflow still expected (the flow rate times an afterflow time constant $\tau_\mathrm{af}$), plus one standard deviation of the estimate. In the \emph{tap} stage the auger stops, the tube moves to a third tilt, and single taps free a few milligrams at a time until the reading lies within a stopping band of $\pm\delta$ around the target; when taps stop freeing powder, the controller turns the auger by a small angle (a 5$^\circ$ ``nudge'') to re-feed the lip. The dose is scored on a balance reading taken 2~s after the last action.

Two rules end a dose early: a dose that exceeds the target by more than 100~mg is aborted, and a dose that uses up its allowance of tap cycles (120) or nudges (20), or makes no progress for 8~s, ends as a jam. The Kalman filter models the balance as a first-order lag, a reading that approaches the true mass with a single time constant. The controller used the datasheet value of 0.7~s for that constant, whereas drop tests on the rig gave 0.16~s. The optimized settings absorb this mismatch, but it should be removed before settings are compared across powders.

\label{sec:powders}

The metal feedstocks were a gas-atomized AlSi10Mg powder (\ph{supplier, lot and size grade not recorded}) and an Al~4047 (Al--12\,wt\% Si) powder made in the laboratory by ultrasonic atomization and used unsieved (Table~\ref{tab:powders}). \subsection{Dose records}
\label{sec:records}

Every dose writes one machine-readable record with the settings as executed, the firmware version, the time spent in each stage, every stop event, the outcome, and the balance readings: about three per second during the auger stages and one per tap cycle in the tap stage. Hand-driven steps, such as reverse turns of the auger used to clear a blocked outlet, were logged as separate records of the same campaign. The analysis uses a snapshot of all 125 records made between 29 September and 20 October 2026; there are no later calibration records. Each record ends in one of a fixed set of outcomes: \emph{ok} (within the stopping band), \emph{overshoot} (above it), \emph{jam} (an allowance used up, or no progress for 8~s; recorded as ``cycle-budget'' or ``stalled''), \emph{timeout} (600~s or a session limit), \emph{abort} (100~mg above the target), or an equipment error (balance not tared, or a firmware fault), which is flagged separately and excluded from the comparisons. Spills are flagged by an operator and were therefore not recorded in unattended runs.

\subsection{Calibration campaign 1: eight settings on salt}
\label{sec:bo}

For one powder and one target mass (0.5~g), calibration searches eight controller settings (Table~\ref{tab:space}) to minimize two objectives at once: the total time of a dose, $t$, and its absolute error, $|e|$, both measured on the scored balance reading. The campaign fixed no tolerance in advance: the stopping band $\delta$ is itself one of the searched settings, and the error objective measures where the dose actually ended. Doses slower than 180~s or more than 20~mg off count as failures. These two limits form the reference point for the hypervolume, the area of objective space between that reference point and the best doses so far, which is the usual single-number summary of a Pareto front. All other controller parameters, including the controller gains and the 100~mg abort limit, were frozen at the values of the hand-tuned controller.

The campaign ran in three phases, with four doses at the hand-tuned settings spread through it as a baseline. In the first phase, a two-level fractional factorial screening design of resolution~IV ($2^{8-4}$, 16 runs) estimated the main effect of each setting without confusing it with any two-factor interaction; each run sits at a corner of the search space. Four centre-point doses, with every setting at the middle of its range, measured the dose-to-dose scatter, and two doses ran at the hand-tuned settings. In the second phase, the afterflow constant was refitted from the 26 stop events recorded so far ($\tau_\mathrm{af} = 0.834$~s, the median ratio of afterflow mass to flow rate; a linear dependence on flow rate was tested and was not significant, $t = 1.1$), and the centre point (four doses) and the hand-tuned settings (two doses) were repeated with the new value. In the third phase, 14 doses were chosen one at a time in Ax~0.4.3 \cite{AxPlatform} on BoTorch \cite{Balandat2020Botorch}: two quasi-random (Sobol) points, then 12 chosen by the model. The hand-tuned baseline therefore consists of four single doses, two at the initial $\tau_\mathrm{af} = 0.30$~s and two at the fitted value.

The model was a fully Bayesian Gaussian process (GP) for each objective with a sparse axis-aligned subspace prior (SAASBO) \cite{Eriksson2021High}, which assumes that only a few of the eight settings matter strongly and so suits a search with many settings and few observations. The acquisition function was the logarithmic form of the noisy expected hypervolume improvement (qLogNEHVI) \cite{Daulton2021Parallel,Ament2023Unexpected}, which allows for the noise in each observed dose. Inputs were scaled to the unit cube and each objective was standardized. The screening and centre-point doses were given to the model as existing data, so it used all 28 earlier doses. Doses that jammed were reported to the model at the failure limits (180~s, 20~mg) or at their measured values if worse, and overshoots at their measured values. Each suggestion took 1--1.5~min to compute. A preset deadline stopped the optimization phase after 14 doses, short of the 30--40 planned.

Each dose ran unattended over a remote connection to the rig. The cup was not emptied during the campaign: it held 20.6~g at the end, against a 45~g limit set for the session. A restart never repeated a dose, because each dose carries a unique identifier and its stored result is returned instead. An AI coding agent (Claude Code, Anthropic) launched and monitored the campaigns and the validation blocks, and wrote much of the firmware, campaign, analysis and simulation code, which the authors reviewed. The agent started the campaign script, watched it and restarted it after interruptions, but the settings of every dose came from the screening design or the optimizer, not from the agent. Every prompt and response is public in the repository \cite{PowderDoserRepo}.

Because the observed Pareto front is built from single doses, a point can lie on it by chance. The recommended settings were therefore taken from the model's own Pareto set, computed from its posterior-mean predictions rather than from the observed doses, and validated a week later (6 October) with a block of eight repeated doses. A second block of eight tested the screening dose that lay on the observed front (\texttt{corner-09}). Two further doses at the recommended settings ran before a restart of the rig on that day and belong to neither block. On 6 and 8 October six more doses at the recommended settings, with targets from 5~mg to 10~g, gave a first view of the range of doses the settings can reach.

\subsection{Calibration campaign 2: nine settings, including the hand-over margin}
\label{sec:margin}

The tap stage took most of each dose's time in campaign~1, and the trickle handed over to the taps a median 54~mg short of the target. Campaign~2 therefore added the hand-over margin of the trickle (0--35~mg) as a ninth setting and scored overshoots like jams, at the failure limits, because an overshoot cannot be undone. It started from the 60 doses of campaign~1 as existing data, at their 35~mg margin, and re-dosed the 16 screening corners at a 0~mg margin on 13 October, so that each corner gives a paired comparison of the two margins. Two check doses at the recommended settings of campaign~1 followed, and then the optimizer (SAASBO with qLogNEHVI, as above, with one Sobol point). The campaign ran unattended within limits on the cup load (40~g) and on the number of consecutive stalls or jams (four), and resumed on 15 October. Three doses on 13 October and three on 15 October ended with a firmware fault: the dose itself finished, but the microcontroller ran out of contiguous memory while formatting the result. The fault was fixed during the session of 15 October (firmware \texttt{trickle\_tap/2026-10-15}) and did not recur. Doses with an equipment error were not given to the model.

\subsection{Doses of metal feedstock into atomizer charges}
\label{sec:production}

The same doser weighed feedstock into the crucibles of the laboratory's ultrasonic atomizer, which melts each charge and atomizes it into a new powder. These doses used the bulk settings of the recommended salt settings (40$^\circ$, 100~rpm, 2~Hz taps) and the afterflow constant fitted on salt, with no calibration on the metal. The AlSi10Mg charge (8~g requested) ran the full three-stage dose. The Al~4047 charges (8~g on 30 September; 3.04~g and 6.57~g on 19 and 20 October, targets computed from the weight fractions of a composition series) ran in a bulk-only mode, in which the dose ends after the bulk stage and the remainder is topped up. When a dose stalled, the outlet was cleared remotely by turning the auger backwards at 0$^\circ$ with bursts of taps, or, once, by an operator who removed large particles from the tube by hand. How these charges were completed, and their final masses, are reported with the hardware \cite{Charles2026Open}; here only the feed rate in the bulk stage is used, as a measure of how the powder flowed at fixed settings.

\subsection{Fixed-setting records used for the property correlations}
\label{sec:fixed}

The companion study ran every powder through the same test protocols with frozen settings \cite{Charles2026Open}. Two of them are used here. Protocol~C turned the auger through six single revolutions at 30~rpm at each of three tilts (0, 22.5 and 45$^\circ$), with a settled balance reading after each revolution, and gives the delivery per revolution. Protocol~G made three closed-loop doses of 1~g with an earlier three-stage controller whose settings were tuned by hand on salt and frozen for every powder, and gives a dose error per powder. Delivery at 45$^\circ$ and the mean 1~g dose error were correlated with literature values of the angle of repose, moisture content, particle size and Hausner ratio. Two further descriptors were computed from the same records and are proposed for screening atomized batches \cite{PowderDoserRepo}: a gravity-assisted volumetric flow index (delivery per revolution at 45$^\circ$ divided by bulk density, normalized to a reference AlSi10Mg powder), and an interruption fraction (the share of balance increments at 15~rpm that show no flow), a doser analogue of the cohesive index measured in a rotating drum \cite{Brocksieper2026Drum,Lumay2012Measuring}.

\subsection{Multi-task calibration across powders (planned)}
\label{sec:mtbo}

Each powder is a task, and all tasks share the eight controller settings and the dose record. A multi-task GP \cite{Bonilla2008Multi,Swersky2013Multi} models the objectives of task $k$ at settings $\mathbf{x}$ with a product kernel $K\big((\mathbf{x},k),(\mathbf{x}',k')\big) = B_{kk'}\,k_x(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')$, in which the positive semi-definite matrix $B$ is learned from the data and its normalized entries are the correlations between powders. Two variants will be compared. An index kernel learns $B$ freely from the dose data. A descriptor kernel computes $B$ from each powder's fixed-setting fingerprint and, where available, its measured properties, so that a new powder can borrow from similar ones before its first optimized dose \cite{Taylor2023Accelerated}. The model will also take covariates of the powder's state, at least the mass dispensed since the tube was filled and the relative humidity, because campaign~2 showed that the response drifts with them (Section~\ref{sec:res-margin}).

The objectives will be the time per dose, a one-sided error with a constraint on the probability of overshoot, which cannot be undone, and the accessible dose range: the smallest target that the settings deliver within tolerance with probability at least 0.95. Repeatability will not be optimized; it will be reported afterwards from blocks of repeated doses, as for campaign~1. \ph{Constraint thresholds, dose targets and the replicate count of the validation blocks to be fixed before the multi-task campaigns.}

The benchmark has two parts. In the retrospective part, each completed single-task campaign is held out in turn, and a statistical model fitted to its doses (a surrogate) stands in for the rig. Multi-task optimization on the held-out powder, informed by the other powders, is then compared with single-task SAASBO, with quasi-random (Sobol) search, with a pooled GP that ignores task identity, with a descriptor-only model, and with simply carrying over the best settings of the most similar powder, using the same initial designs and paired random seeds, and without letting the held-out data define the best known result. In the prospective part, one new powder, a freshly atomized batch, is calibrated with the multi-task model. The figure of merit is the number of doses until the recommended settings reach 90\% of the best hypervolume known for that powder. \ph{Planned campaigns: AlSi10Mg, sieved Al~4047, and one further alloy feedstock (630 bronze or another laboratory-atomized alloy), each with about 42 doses, an 8-dose validation block and an 8-dose hand-tuned block.} All doses will be made in air, in a dehumidified enclosure.

\subsection{Discrete element model of the auger}
\label{sec:dem}

A discrete element model of the auger outlet was built in LIGGGHTS-PUBLIC~3.8 \cite{Kloss2012Models,Cundall1979Discrete}. Grains interact through a Hertz normal contact with history-dependent tangential friction and an elastic--plastic spring--dashpot rolling resistance model \cite{Ai2011Assessment}. Cohesion is added as a simplified Johnson--Kendall--Roberts (SJKR) term, a pull-off force proportional to the cohesion energy density and the contact area \cite{Johnson1971Surface}. For salt, the grains follow a three-size distribution around a median diameter of 0.425~mm (number fractions 0.3/0.4/0.3 at 0.85, 1 and 1.15 times the median) with a density of 2165~kg\,m$^{-3}$, a literature value for the material class. Young's modulus is reduced to 0.2~MPa to allow a time step of $2.1\times10^{-5}$~s, which keeps mean overlaps below 0.4\% of a diameter; Poisson's ratio is 0.3, the coefficient of restitution 0.5, sliding friction 0.5 between grains and 0.4 against the wall, and rolling friction 0.3. None of these values was fitted to the dosing data. The geometry is meshed from the CAD model of the printed auger: the conical outlet with its 3~mm exit, the solid core whose tip leaves a 1.07~mm annular gap at the exit, and the lowest 1.5 turns of the flight, filled with about 29{,}000 grains and topped up from a feed zone. Tilt is applied by rotating gravity, rotation by turning the auger mesh, and the delivered mass is counted as grains leave the exit. Each case was run for 0.3--3 revolutions, mostly from a single random packing. Because the SJKR pull-off force scales with the contact area, which depends on the softened stiffness, any cohesion energy density inferred with this model is specific to the simulator and its numerical stiffness, and comparable across powders only at that fixed stiffness. Adhesion between particles and the printed PLA wall is a separate parameter that dosing data alone may not resolve.

To infer effective contact parameters for a powder, the model will be run over a designed set of cohesion energy densities and sliding and rolling friction coefficients, with the particle-size distribution fixed at the measured one, at the tilts and speeds of the fixed-setting protocol. A GP emulator, a fast statistical stand-in fitted to these runs, then replaces the simulator in a Bayesian calibration \cite{Kennedy2001Bayesian,Higdon2008Computer}. The calibration returns a posterior distribution over the contact parameters, the range of values consistent with the measured response and its scatter, with a discrepancy term for systematic model error. Dosing data alone may not separate cohesion from friction \cite{Coetzee2017Review,Richter2020Development,ElKassem2021Sensitivity}, so whether each parameter can be recovered on its own will first be checked on synthetic data. For the finer metal powders, the grain count will be reduced by coarse-graining, which simulates fewer, larger grains while preserving the ratio of cohesive to gravitational forces \cite{Girnth2024Dimensionless}. \ph{Emulator design (number of runs, parameter ranges) to be fixed after the friction and cohesion sweeps in the rig geometry.}

\subsection{Independent powder characterization (planned)}
\label{sec:charac}

To test whether dosing-derived descriptors and inferred parameters carry physical meaning, the lots that are dosed will be characterized on samples from the same jars \cite{ASTMF3049}. In the laboratory: bulk and tapped density and the Hausner ratio \cite{ASTMB527}; angle of repose from photographs of a poured pile; moisture by loss on drying, with temperature and relative humidity logged; Hall-flow time \cite{ASTMB213} or, for powders that do not pass the Hall funnel, Carney-flow time \cite{ASTMB964}, with a purchased funnel set; and the particle-size distribution from scanning electron micrographs, by sieving, or by laser diffraction \cite{ASTMB822}. In an outside laboratory: the flow function coefficient $ff_c$ from a shear cell \cite{ASTMD7891,Schulze2021Powders}. Spreadability will be assessed by blade-spreading single layers of 50--100~\si{\micro\metre} and measuring their areal mass and coverage \cite{Snow2019Development,Cordova2020Measuring}. \ph{Shear-cell provider, funnel set and SEM time to be arranged.}

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
\label{sec:stats}

Samples are small (4--16 doses per group) and dose times have a long right tail, so comparisons use exact or resampling methods. Medians are reported with percentile bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals (20{,}000 resamples). Two groups are compared with the exact two-sided Mann--Whitney test, with the Hodges--Lehmann shift and its exact confidence interval, and paired corners with the exact Wilcoxon signed-rank test and, for counts of overshoots, the exact McNemar test. A success fraction is bounded with one-sided 95\% Clopper--Pearson limits. Associations between dosing descriptors and powder properties are Spearman rank correlations, because $n \le 12$ and many property values are class estimates; their $p$-values are not corrected for the 42 predictor--response pairs examined and are treated as leads rather than findings. The campaign's GP model was checked by leave-one-out cross-validation against three simpler models (Section~\ref{sec:res-bo}; the full mapping to the journal's checklist for machine-learning studies is in Table~S7). All records, including aborted and jammed doses, are kept in the analysis; doses with an equipment error are reported but excluded from comparisons. The scripts that compute every number in this paper from the dose records are in the repository \cite{PowderDoserRepo}.

\subsection{Calibration on salt}
\label{sec:res-bo}

Campaign~1 ran 42 doses of 0.5~g unattended in 2.3~h (Fig.~\ref{fig:salt}a). The four doses at the hand-tuned settings took 180--343~s (median 245~s) and ended 3.6--4.8~mg short of the target (median 4.6~mg). Of the 42 doses, 31 ended inside their stopping band, 8 overshot by 21--83~mg and 3 ended in a jam when the allowance of tap cycles ran out.

The screening design showed which settings mattered and why. Tapping during the bulk stage cut the average time of successful doses from 186 to 79~s, but five of the eight runs with bulk taps on ended in an overshoot or a jam, against two of the eight with taps off, presumably because taps free extra powder after the auger stops. Raising the tap-stage tilt from 0 to 15$^\circ$ cut the average time from 223 to 92~s. Switching to the trickle stage earlier, with 0.30~g rather than 0.05~g to go, cut the mean absolute error from 34 to 11~mg, and two runs at the larger threshold ended in an overshoot or a jam, against five at the smaller. Six of the eight overshoots were screening runs, every one at the highest bulk speed, the smallest switch threshold, or both. Time varied most at a fixed setting: the eight centre-point doses took 24--391~s.

In the third phase, none of the 14 doses beat the best single screening doses, so the observed hypervolume did not grow. The settings the model recommended (\texttt{bo-005}, Table~\ref{tab:space}) had been predicted to take 91~$\pm$~66~s with a 3~$\pm$~9~mg error, and took 100~s with a 2.4~mg error in the campaign. Repeated eight times a week later, all eight doses ended inside the 3~mg band, with a median time of 78~s (range 60--114~s; bootstrap 95\% interval of the median 69--110~s) and a median error of 1.8~mg (1.3--2.3~mg); every dose ended 0.8--2.6~mg under the target, and the delivered masses had a standard deviation of 0.56~mg (Fig.~\ref{fig:salt}b). The block's mean time, 85~s, lay within a tenth of a predicted standard deviation of the model's prediction. Against the four hand-tuned doses, the recommended settings were faster in every dose (exact Mann--Whitney $p = 0.004$; Hodges--Lehmann shift $-166$~s, 95\% interval $-262$ to $-104$~s; ratio of medians 3.1, bootstrap 95\% interval 2.1--4.4) and more accurate ($p = 0.004$). The hand-tuned baseline is weak in two ways: it has four doses, and two of them ran at the initial afterflow constant. Against the two hand-tuned doses at the fitted constant alone (median 209~s), the ratio is 2.7. Eight successes in eight doses bound the probability of a dose inside the band only from below, at 0.69, and no overshoot in eight bounds its probability from above at 0.31 (one-sided 95\% Clopper--Pearson limits); showing a success rate of 0.95 would take 59 doses without a failure.

The best single dose of the campaign did not repeat. The screening run \texttt{corner-09} had dosed in 52~s with a 0.7~mg error, but eight more doses at its settings took 47--385~s (median 202~s, 95\% interval 99--356~s), depending on how much powder happened to fall after the bulk stage stopped, and missed by a median 2.6~mg. The recommended settings were more accurate than \texttt{corner-09} ($p = 0.005$) and probably faster, although the difference in time is not significant at this sample size ($p = 0.065$; Hodges--Lehmann shift $-115$~s, 95\% interval $-268$ to $+3$~s), and \texttt{corner-09} was no faster than hand tuning ($p = 0.81$). Picking the best observed dose would have been worse than taking the model's recommendation.

Most of the time saved came from the tap stage (Fig.~\ref{fig:salt}c). A hand-tuned dose spent 152--318~s, 84--93\% of its time, tapping out the last 54--66~mg at 0.6--1.1~mg per tap, whereas at the recommended settings each tap freed 1.5--3.6~mg, consistent with the tube rising from a 10$^\circ$ trickle tilt to a 15$^\circ$ tap tilt instead of dropping to it. The optimizer changed seven of the eight settings, and all six numeric settings ended at an edge of their search range (Fig.~\ref{fig:salt}d). Some of these edges are hardware limits, such as the auger speed near its maximum, and others are choices, such as the 3~mg stopping band; the rest suggest that the next campaign should search a wider range.

Leave-one-out cross-validation tested how well the campaign's model predicts doses it has not seen (Table~S5, Fig.~S2). For time per dose, no model predicted held-out doses well. Refitted on the 42 doses, the SAAS GP ranked them with a Spearman correlation of 0.24 ($p = 0.13$) and a root-mean-square error of 129~s, barely below the 134~s of predicting the mean of the other doses, and a linear model of the eight settings did about as well ($\rho = 0.34$, 131~s). Fitted to the logarithm of time, the same GP ranked held-out doses better ($\rho = 0.44$, $p = 0.004$). For the error, the linear model ranked held-out doses best ($\rho = 0.54$) and the SAAS GP second ($\rho = 0.41$, $p = 0.007$); no model predicted the size of the overshoots. What set the SAAS GP apart was the calibration of its uncertainty: 95\% of held-out doses fell inside its 95\% predictive intervals for time and 93\% for error, whereas a standard GP fitted by maximum a posteriori was overconfident (90\% and 71\%). Its relevance weights agreed with the screening design, with bulk speed, the switch threshold and bulk taps weighing most for time, and bulk taps, then the switch threshold, for error. Refitted on all 42 doses, it predicted 96~$\pm$~108~s and 1.1~$\pm$~8.3~mg at the recommended settings (mean $\pm$ predictive standard deviation, including dose-to-dose noise), and all eight validation doses fell inside both 95\% intervals. At this sample size, then, the data do not show that a flexible model predicts better than a linear one; the benefit of the GP lay in its calibrated uncertainty, on which the acquisition function and the recommendation rely.

At the recommended settings, six further doses with targets from 5~mg to 10~g all ended 0.3--2.7~mg under target, inside the 3~mg band, in 25--305~s (Fig.~\ref{fig:metal}c). Because the error is set by the band rather than by the target, the relative error fell from 54\% at 5~mg to 0.03\% at 10~g, so these settings meet a $\pm$5\% tolerance only above about 60~mg. These are single doses and indicate the accessible dose range only roughly.

\subsection{What limits unattended calibration: campaign~2}
\label{sec:res-margin}

Campaign~2 ran 43 doses on 13 and 15 October (Fig.~\ref{fig:margin}). Eight ended with an equipment error (two before the balance was tared, six with the firmware fault), leaving 35 usable doses, of which 19 ended inside their band, 5 overshot, and 11 jammed or stalled.

The hand-over margin made no detectable difference. At a 0~mg margin, the taps took over a median 32~mg short of the target instead of 54~mg, and two corners that had needed long tap stages finished much faster (\texttt{corner-03} in 28~s instead of 227~s, \texttt{corner-10} in 72~s instead of 352~s). Over the 16 corners as a whole, however, the change was not systematic (Fig.~\ref{fig:margin}a): for the seven corners that ended inside their band at both margins, the median change in time was $-12$~s (exact Wilcoxon $p = 0.58$), and the error changed by a mean of 0.2~mg; 6 corners overshot at 35~mg and 5 at 0~mg (exact McNemar $p = 1.0$).

The powder changed more than the margin did. In the bulk stage, which the margin does not affect, the same 16 corners fed a median 7\% less on 13 October than on 29 September (exact Wilcoxon $p = 0.03$), and the taps freed about 0.2--0.3~mg each instead of 1.2--1.7~mg. As a result the two check doses at the recommended settings of campaign~1, which had dosed eight times out of eight inside 114~s on 6 October, both used up their 120 tap cycles and ran for more than 400~s. On 15 October the bulk feed at 40$^\circ$ and 20~rpm fell from a median of 41~mg/s, the same in the three earlier sessions, to 7.6~mg/s (range 1.0--23~mg/s; Fig.~\ref{fig:margin}b), and the last two doses stalled after delivering 184 and 155~mg, the second at settings that had dosed inside the band minutes before. The campaign was halted there. The most likely causes are that the tube was running low or that the salt had caked in the two days since the previous session; nobody inspected it. Because the loop cannot tell an empty or caked tube from a jam, those doses were scored as failures of the settings. The optimizer's doses therefore mix the effect of the settings with that of a drifting powder: seven of them ended inside a 3~mg band, the best in 72--92~s (Fig.~\ref{fig:margin}c), but none was validated, and the model's own Pareto set still consisted of points from campaign~1.

\subsection{Metal feedstock: transfer of the salt settings, and a feedstock problem}
\label{sec:res-metal}

The salt settings transferred to a free-flowing metal powder. Applied unchanged to gas-atomized AlSi10Mg, with the afterflow constant fitted on salt, they delivered 7.993~g for an 8~g request ($-$7.2~mg, $-$0.09\%) in 315~s, in a single dose that has not been repeated. Its bulk stage fed 144~mg/s (86~mg per revolution), about twice the rate measured for salt at the same settings in short bulk stages and 1.3 times the rate of a 1.5~g salt dose (Fig.~\ref{fig:metal}a).

The laboratory's Al~4047 powder did not transfer. At the same bulk settings, a first batch fed 11~mg/s (6.7~mg per revolution) on 30 September, 13 times less than AlSi10Mg, and a second batch fed 1.7~mg/s on 19 October before the dose stalled (Fig.~\ref{fig:metal}a). Steeper tilts (60 and 75$^\circ$) at the highest speed and faster tapping moved almost nothing (0.1~mg per revolution), whereas about 13 reverse turns at 0$^\circ$ freed the outlet, after which it fed 60--80~mg per revolution in either direction for a few turns. The next day, three doses at the same settings started at progressively lower rates and decayed within each dose (Fig.~\ref{fig:metal}b): averaged over their bulk stages, they fed 8.8, 7.6 and 2.4~mg/s. After an operator removed large particles from the tube by hand, the next dose fed 31~mg/s and finished inside its band. A blocked outlet that reverse turns can clear, a feed that decays within and between doses, and a recovery once large particles are removed point to oversized particles or agglomerates in the unsieved powder bridging at the 3~mm exit. The dose record therefore flagged a feedstock problem before the powder reached a printer or an atomizer, without any measurement beyond the calibration itself. \ph{Sieve analysis or micrographs of the removed particles would confirm the cause.}

\subsection{A discrete element model of the auger}
\label{sec:res-dem}

Without any parameter fitted to dosing data, the DEM model reproduced the delivery of salt at the centre-point settings of campaign~1: 105~mg per revolution at 27.5$^\circ$ and 60~rpm averaged over the run (116~$\pm$~20~mg after the first quarter revolution), against 105~$\pm$~11~mg measured over eight doses (Fig.~\ref{fig:dem}a). A second random packing at the same settings gave 102~mg. The model also reproduced the rise of delivery with tilt, but with larger residuals at the other settings: 33 against about 28~mg per revolution at 0$^\circ$ ($+18$\%), 153 against about 195~mg at 45$^\circ$ ($-22$\%), and 71 against 106--110~mg at 22.5$^\circ$ and 90~rpm ($-33$ to $-35$\%). The measured values at 0$^\circ$ and 45$^\circ$ were taken at 30~rpm and scaled to 60~rpm with a speed exponent that is itself uncertain \cite{Charles2026Open}.

The model is most sensitive to the geometry of the exit and to the grains. The tip of the auger core sits inside the exit and leaves a 1.07~mm annular gap, about 2.5 grain diameters, which restricts the flow: moving the tip back by 0.5, 1 and 2~mm raised delivery from 105 to 112, 224 and 427~mg per revolution. Reducing the median grain size from 0.425 to 0.35~mm raised it by 29\%. In a smaller 10~mm auger, adding cohesion (20~kJ\,m$^{-3}$) cut delivery from 46.8 to 0.2~mg per revolution, a switch from flow to no flow of the kind seen between coarse and fine powders of the same material. The narrow exit is also where the Al~4047 powder blocked (Section~\ref{sec:res-metal}). The model's cost sets what can be simulated. The outlet section with 29{,}000 grains takes 26--30~min per revolution on one processor core, a full salt-filled auger with 1.1~million grains about 19~h, and a fine metal powder would need about 10$^9$ grains per auger, so those powders require coarse-graining \cite{Girnth2024Dimensionless}.

\ph{Inference results.} The emulator built from \ph{N} simulations reproduced held-out simulations within \ph{X\%}. Delivery depended most strongly on cohesion at low tilt and on friction at high tilt (Fig.~\ref{fig:dem}b), so the tilt sweep constrains both. For salt the posterior gave an effective cohesion energy density of \ph{X~kJ\,m$^{-3}$} and sliding friction of \ph{Y} (Fig.~\ref{fig:dem}c).

\subsection{Dosing response and powder properties}
\label{sec:res-props}

Even with literature values in place of measured properties, the fixed-setting records of the companion study \cite{Charles2026Open} ranked powders the way standard flow measures do (Fig.~\ref{fig:props}a). Across 12 powders, delivery at 45$^\circ$ fell as the angle of repose rose (Spearman $\rho = -0.84$, bootstrap 95\% interval $-0.98$ to $-0.50$; $p = 0.0006$, or 0.023 after a Bonferroni correction for the 42 pairs examined), and the same held at 22.5$^\circ$ ($\rho = -0.77$, $n = 11$). The five powders with a measured or manufacturer angle of repose ranked correctly on their own: those at 30--32$^\circ$ delivered 244--339~mg per revolution and those at 41--45$^\circ$ delivered 9--37~mg. The equilibrium moisture content tracked the error of 1~g doses under the hand-tuned controller ($\rho = +0.87$, $n = 9$; Fig.~\ref{fig:props}b): every powder dosed within 5~mg held less than 0.1~wt\% water, and every hygroscopic biopolymer missed by 33--292~mg. Literature median particle size did not predict delivery across materials ($\rho = +0.26$), although the two silicon grades of the companion study show that it decides flow within one material. The interruption fraction ranged from 0.13 for AlSi10Mg and 0.26 for salt to 0.81 for carboxymethyl cellulose and 0.96 for sodium alginate; it also followed the angle of repose ($\rho = +0.76$, $n = 10$) while only partly overlapping the volumetric flow index ($\rho = -0.66$ between the two), so the two descriptors are not redundant.

Against blade-spread single layers, \ph{the interruption fraction ranked the batches like layer coverage}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the optimizer contributed} On salt, one unattended session of 2.3~h found settings that were three times faster than hand tuning, with less than half its error, and that held up in eight repeats. The optimizer's 14 doses never beat the best single screening doses, so its contribution was not a better single dose. It was the recommendation: a model that averages over noisy doses picked settings that repeated, whereas the best observed dose, \texttt{corner-09}, was on the front largely by chance and was no faster than hand tuning when repeated. The 20 screening doses were well spent: they explained in physical terms why taps in the bulk stage speed doses up but cause overshoots, and they gave the model a balanced start. Two lessons follow for the next campaigns. First, time per dose has a long tail of slow doses (the centre point took 24--391~s), which a standardized GP on raw time handles poorly; a noise model that allows for occasional slow doses, or a log transform of time, should be compared in the retrospective benchmark. Second, the recommended settings sat at the edges of the search space, so the ranges were set too conservatively.

\paragraph{The powder is not a fixed target} Campaign~2 is the more important result for anyone who wants to run calibration unattended. Within a week, the same settings fed 7\% less in the bulk stage, the taps freed a tenth as much powder, and the recommended settings of campaign~1, validated eight times out of eight, ran out of tap cycles twice; two days later the feed fell fivefold within one session. A BO loop that treats every dose as a sample of a fixed function of the settings will attribute such drift to the settings. Three measures follow and are built into the plan for the multi-task campaigns: record the state of the powder with every dose (mass dispensed since the tube was filled, relative humidity, time since the last session) and give it to the model as a covariate; start every session with a short reference dose at known settings, and stop or re-validate when it drifts; and let the loop pause on a suspected empty or caked tube instead of scoring the dose as a failure. The firmware fault of campaign~2 is a smaller lesson of the same kind: an unattended campaign needs equipment errors to be detected and kept out of the model, as they were here.

\paragraph{Dosing records as a check of feedstock} For an alloy workflow, the Al~4047 doses are the clearest case of the doser measuring the powder. At settings that delivered 144~mg/s of AlSi10Mg, the laboratory's unsieved powder fed 11~mg/s or less, decayed within and between doses, and recovered once large particles were removed. Nothing beyond the dose record was needed to see this, and the same record flagged it on every batch. Sieving each atomized batch before use, as is now planned, should remove the cause; a fixed-setting reference dose of every new batch would then show whether it worked. The rankings of Fig.~\ref{fig:props} point the same way across materials: delivery ranked 12 powders like their angle of repose, and moisture content, not particle size, tracked dose error. These agree with studies of pharmaceutical screw feeders, in which bulk density and cohesion-related properties, rather than size, predicted feed rate \cite{Wang2017Predicting,Tahir2020Development,Bekaert2021Determination,Bostijn2019Twin}. The literature values are class estimates, however, not measurements of our lots, and a correlation of ranks across 12 very different materials shows only that the doser orders powders sensibly. Whether it can resolve the smaller differences between batches of one alloy (sieved and unsieved, dry and humid, virgin and reused), which matter most for feedstock quality control in AM, remains to be shown with measured properties of the same lots. The fixed-setting record also has two advantages over funnel tests: it still returns information on cohesive powders, recording a no-flow result rather than a failed test \cite{Spierings2016Powder,Marchetti2021Powder}, and it has several dimensions, so it can separate effects that a single flow time combines.

\paragraph{From dosing data to contact parameters} The DEM model reproduced salt delivery at one setting without calibration and followed its trend with tilt, which suggests that its geometry and contact laws capture the main mechanism. Its errors at other settings, and its strong sensitivity to the exit gap and the grain size, mean that inference must start from a measured exit geometry and a measured particle-size distribution; otherwise those uncertainties will be absorbed into the inferred cohesion and friction \cite{Coetzee2017Review,ElKassem2021Sensitivity}. The inferred parameters will be effective rather than intrinsic, comparable across powders only at a fixed numerical stiffness \cite{Thakur2014Micromechanical}. Static (triboelectric) charging, suspected in noisy readings of carboxymethyl cellulose and seen as salt sticking to the auger walls in a glove box, is not represented in the model \cite{Galindo2024Charging}. If these caveats are respected, the same contact parameters can drive DEM models of powder spreading, the standard tool for predicting layer quality in PBF-LB \cite{Haeri2017Optimisation,Nan2018Jamming,Meier2019Critical,Shaheen2021Influence}.

\paragraph{Limits of the present data} The optimization covers one powder at one target mass, with a hand-tuned baseline of four doses on the campaign day; transfer to AlSi10Mg rests on one dose. All doses were made in air, and the state of the powder in the tube was not controlled or recorded. The tap stage sets the cost of accuracy: at the recommended settings every validation dose landed inside the 3~mg band, but taps took most of each dose's time, and the mass a tap frees falls as the lip of the tube empties and, as campaign~2 showed, from one session to the next. Tighter tolerances or faster doses therefore need a smaller and more consistent final increment, for example a smaller outlet or a refill of the lip between taps, more than a different control law. Which printed tube produced each dataset was not recorded, and its exit geometry, which the DEM model shows to matter, was not measured. Finally, every powder was supplied commercially or made in the laboratory without a recorded lot analysis.

\paragraph{Implications for AM feedstock workflows} In an alloy-discovery workflow the doser is calibrated on every powder anyway, so the records described here come at little extra cost in powder or time. Stored with the dose records, the fixed-setting response, the validated settings and, in time, the inferred contact parameters of each lot form a feedstock record that can flag a batch that flows unlike its predecessors, as the unsieved Al~4047 batch was flagged before it reached an atomizer. \ph{Whether that record predicts spreading and packing in the powder bed will be tested on blade-spread layers (Section~\ref{sec:charac}).}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

The calibration of an open-source auger doser was treated as multi-objective Bayesian optimization, and its records were examined as a measurement of the powder. On salt, an unattended campaign of 42 doses found settings that dosed 0.5~g in a median of 78~s with a median error of 1.8~mg over eight repeats, about three times faster than hand-tuned settings, whereas the best single screening dose did not repeat. A second campaign showed that the powder's response drifts between and within sessions enough to defeat an optimizer that ignores it, so the state of the powder must be recorded and modelled. The salt settings dosed 8~g of AlSi10Mg within 0.1\% in one dose, while an unsieved laboratory-atomized Al~4047 fed an order of magnitude more slowly and stalled until oversized particles were removed, which the dose record alone revealed. Across 12 powders, delivery ranked like the angle of repose. A DEM model of the auger, with no parameters fitted to dosing data, reproduced the measured delivery of salt at one setting and its trend with tilt. \ph{Multi-task calibration across N powders reduced the doses needed for a new powder by X\%, and effective cohesion and friction inferred from dosing data tracked shear-cell and Hall-flow measurements.} Because every dosing workflow already calibrates its doser, the records of that calibration can double as a check of each feedstock at little extra cost.
