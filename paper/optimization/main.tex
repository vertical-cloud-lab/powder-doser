\begin{abstract}
The mass an auger delivers depends on how it is driven and on the powder, so every calibration of an auger doser is also a small measurement of how that powder flows.
We treat the calibration of an open-source, 3D-printed auger doser as multi-objective Bayesian optimization and ask what the calibration data reveal about the powder.
At fixed settings, 13 powders, from food-safe stand-ins to AlSi10Mg and silicon, delivered between 10 and 231~mg per auger revolution, and three delivered none; across 12 of them, delivery ranked like the angle of repose (Spearman $\rho = -0.84$).
On sodium chloride, an optimizer tuned eight controller settings in an unattended campaign of 42 doses. Its recommended settings dosed 0.5~g in a median 78~s with a median error of 1.8~mg over eight repeats, three times faster than hand-tuned settings, and dosed 8~g of AlSi10Mg to within 0.1\% without change.
We extend the formulation to many powders by treating each powder as a related task of a multi-task model, with added objectives for repeatability and accessible dose range.
A discrete element model of the auger with no fitted parameters reproduces the measured delivery of salt at the campaign's centre point (105 versus 105~$\pm$~11~mg per revolution); with measured particle-size distributions, it is used to infer an effective cohesion and friction for each powder from its dosing response.
\ph{Multi-task calibration cut the doses needed for a new powder by X\%, and the inferred parameters tracked shear-cell and Hall-flow measurements.}
Because calibration is a step every dosing workflow performs anyway, it can double as a characterization of each feedstock.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Alloy discovery by additive manufacturing (AM) starts with weighing powders. Each candidate composition is made by blending elemental or pre-alloyed powders, either in a bed for laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) or in the feeders of a directed energy deposition (DED) system \cite{Mosallanejad2021Situ,Moorehead2020Throughput,Li2018Combinatorial,Vecchio2021Throughput,Mooraj2024Additive,Nelaturu2024Multi}. In a self-driving laboratory (SDL), where a planning algorithm chooses each next composition and robots make and test it \cite{Abolhasani2023Rise,Tom2024Self,Seifrid2022Autonomous}, that weighing step must be automatic, accurate, and fast for every powder in the palette \cite{Szymanski2023Autonomous,Lunt2024Modular,Jiang2023Autonomous,Maffettone2023What}. The same powders must later spread into thin, uniform layers, and how well they do so depends on the same properties that make them easy or hard to dose: particle size and shape, cohesion, and friction \cite{Vock2019Powders,Meier2019Critical,Brika2020Influence,Haferkamp2021Influence}.

Measuring those properties is slow and often uninformative. Hall and Carney funnels report a single flow time and cannot test cohesive powders, which do not flow through them at all \cite{Spierings2016Powder,Krantz2009Characterization}. Shear cells and powder rheometers give richer data, but each test needs tens of millilitres of powder, a dedicated instrument, and a trained operator \cite{Schulze2021Powders,Freeman2007Measuring}. Rotating drums and spreadability tests come closer to the conditions in an AM machine \cite{Lumay2012Measuring,Yablokova2015Rheological,Snow2019Development,Cordova2020Measuring}, yet different flow tests often rank the same powders differently, and none of them predicts spreading reliably on its own \cite{Spierings2016Powder,Krantz2009Characterization,Snow2019Development}. For an SDL that cycles through dozens of feedstocks, including small batches of reactive or expensive powders, a separate characterization campaign for each powder is rarely affordable.

Powder feeders, by contrast, are characterized all the time, because each new powder must be calibrated before it can be dosed. In pharmaceutical manufacturing, the feed factor of a screw feeder, the mass it delivers per screw revolution, has been predicted from bulk density, compressibility, and shear-cell data \cite{Wang2017Predicting,Tahir2020Development,Bekaert2021Determination}, and loss-in-weight feeders have standard methods for measuring how their output varies \cite{Engisch2012Method,Engisch2015Feedrate,Hanson2018Control}. If material properties predict feeder behaviour, the inverse should also hold: a feeder that is driven through a designed set of conditions, and weighed, measures the powder. Small-scale dosers make this attractive because the measurement uses milligrams to grams of powder and happens anyway, every time a new powder is loaded \cite{Besenhard2015Accuracy,Besenhard2017Micro,Faulhammer2014Dose,Yang2007Metering,Alsenz2011Powderpicking}.

Calibration of this kind is an optimization problem with several competing goals. A dose should be accurate and repeatable, it should be fast, and the settings should work across the range of doses the workflow needs. Overshoot cannot be undone, because powder cannot be taken back out of the cup, so error in one direction costs more than in the other. Bayesian optimization (BO) suits such problems: it fits a probabilistic model to the doses made so far and chooses the next settings where the expected gain is largest \cite{Shahriari2016Taking,Liang2021Benchmarking,Baird2026Honegumi}. Multi-objective BO finds the trade-off, or Pareto front, between goals such as time and error \cite{Daulton2021Parallel}, and sparse priors make it practical with many settings and few experiments \cite{Eriksson2021High}. Each new powder, however, still starts from scratch. Multi-task BO treats related problems, here different powders on the same doser, as tasks of one model, so that data from earlier powders inform the first doses of a new one \cite{Bonilla2008Multi,Swersky2013Multi,Taylor2023Accelerated}. How strongly two powders are related is then a learned quantity, and it is natural to ask whether it reflects their physical properties.

Physics-based models can answer that question directly. The discrete element method (DEM) simulates every grain and its contacts \cite{Cundall1979Discrete,Zhu2008Discrete}, has been used to predict the output of screw conveyors \cite{Owen2009Prediction}, and represents cohesion and rolling friction with contact laws whose parameters are hard to measure \cite{Johnson1971Surface,Thakur2014Micromechanical,Ai2011Assessment}. These parameters are usually calibrated by matching simulations to bulk tests, which is itself an inverse problem \cite{Coetzee2017Review,Richter2020Development,Benvenuti2016Identification,Cheng2019Iterative}, best treated with the tools of Bayesian calibration of computer models \cite{Kennedy2001Bayesian,Higdon2008Computer}. DEM is also the standard tool for studying how powders spread into AM layers \cite{Haeri2017Optimisation,Nan2018Jamming,Meier2019Critical,Shaheen2021Influence}. An auger that is turned at controlled speeds and tilts is a well-defined shear and discharge test, so a DEM model of the auger could convert dosing data into contact parameters that a spreading model can then use.

In this work we build on an open-source, 3D-printed auger doser \cite{Charles2026Open} and make three contributions. First, we formulate per-powder calibration as multi-objective BO over the doser's controller settings and demonstrate it on sodium chloride, where an eight-dimensional search ran unattended (Section~\ref{sec:res-bo}). Second, we show that the doser's response at fixed settings separates 13 powders over a 24-fold range, and we extend calibration to many powders as a multi-task problem whose objectives also include repeatability and the range of doses a powder can reach (Sections~\ref{sec:res-powders} and~\ref{sec:res-mtbo}). Third, we build a DEM model of the auger, validate it against measured delivery without calibration, and use it to infer effective cohesion and friction from dosing data, which we compare with Hall-flow and shear-cell measurements (Sections~\ref{sec:res-dem} and~\ref{sec:res-props}). All hardware, firmware, optimization code, simulation inputs, and data are open \cite{PowderDoserRepo}.

\section{Materials and methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Doser and dose procedure}
\label{sec:doser}

The doser is a single channel of an open-source design described in detail elsewhere \cite{Charles2026Open,PowderDoserRepo}; the parts that matter here are summarized below. A one-piece auger, printed in PLA on a Bambu Lab H2D printer (0.2~mm layers), serves as both reservoir and screw: a tube of 21~mm bore and 250~mm length holds up to 58~mL of powder, and a single-start flight (0.5~mm thick, 10.4~mm pitch, 8 turns) runs over the last 84~mm before a 12~mm conical outlet with a 3~mm exit hole. A NEMA-11 stepper motor (11HS18-0674S, Pololu Tic T500 driver) turns the auger through a 20:44 gear pair, up to about 109~rpm. Two servos tilt the auger about a hinge near the outlet through a 2:1 gear, and a push--pull solenoid (JF-0530B, 60~ms pulses) taps the tube to free powder held at the lip. Powder falls into a cup on an analytical balance (A\&D HR-100A; 0.1~mg readability, 102~g capacity) that reports over RS-232. A Raspberry Pi Pico~W runs the dosing firmware (MicroPython), and a Raspberry Pi Zero~2~W next to the rig runs each dose, records it, and uploads it to a MongoDB database. All tilts below are angles of the auger axis above horizontal.

A dose has three stages (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}a). In the \emph{bulk} stage the auger turns continuously at a set tilt and speed, optionally while the solenoid taps at 2~Hz, until the mass still to go falls below a switch threshold. The firmware stops the auger early by the amount it expects to arrive after the stop, using a first-order afterflow model with time constant $\tau_\mathrm{af}$ applied to a Kalman-filtered estimate of the flow rate. In the \emph{trickle} stage the auger runs slowly (typically 2--15~rpm) under a proportional--integral controller on flow rate. In the \emph{tap} stage the auger stops, the tube moves to a third tilt, and single taps free a few milligrams at a time until the reading lies within a stopping band below the target. The dose is scored on a balance reading taken 2~s after the last action. Doses that exceed the target by more than 100~mg are aborted, and doses that use up their tap budget end as jams. The Kalman filter models the balance as a first-order lag; the controller used the datasheet value of 0.7~s, whereas drop tests on the rig gave 0.16~s, a mismatch that the optimized settings absorb but that should be removed before settings are compared across powders. Every dose writes one machine-readable record with the settings as executed, the time spent in each stage, every stop event, and the balance telemetry at 4~Hz.

\subsection{Gravimetric response at fixed settings}
\label{sec:protocols}

Each powder was first run through a fixed battery of test protocols, described fully with the hardware \cite{Charles2026Open}. The ones used here are protocol~C, six single auger revolutions at 30~rpm at each of three tilts (0, 22.5 and 45$^\circ$), each followed by a settled balance reading; protocol~D, three revolutions at 15, 45 and 90~rpm at 22.5$^\circ$; protocol~E, single taps after a re-feed revolution; and protocol~G, three closed-loop doses of 1~g with the hand-tuned controller. From these we extract a short \emph{dosing fingerprint} for each powder: the delivery per revolution at the three tilts, its scatter from revolution to revolution, the exponent $\alpha$ in $\dot m \propto N^{\alpha}$ for auger speed $N$, the mass freed by a single tap, and the afterflow time constant $\tau_\mathrm{af}$ fitted from the mass that arrives after each stop. A full battery uses 4--10~g of powder and takes 40--50~min. Two further descriptors were proposed for screening atomized batches \cite{PowderDoserRepo}: a gravity-assisted volumetric flow, the delivery per revolution at 45$^\circ$ divided by bulk density and normalized to a reference AlSi10Mg powder, and an interruption fraction, the share of balance increments at 15~rpm that show no flow, which is a doser analogue of the cohesive index of a rotating drum \cite{Brocksieper2026Drum,Lumay2012Measuring}.

\subsection{Calibration as multi-objective Bayesian optimization}
\label{sec:bo}

For one powder and one target mass, calibration searches eight controller settings (Table~\ref{tab:space}) to minimize two objectives: the total time of a dose, $t$, and its absolute error, $|e|$, both measured on the scored balance reading. Doses slower than 180~s or more than 20~mg off count as failures; these limits form the reference point for the hypervolume, the area of objective space that the best doses so far dominate. All other controller parameters, including the controller gains and the overshoot guard, were frozen at the values used in the hand-tuned controller.

The campaign ran in four phases. First, four doses at the hand-tuned settings gave a baseline. Second, a two-level fractional factorial screening design of resolution~IV ($2^{8-4}$, 16 corners) and four centre points measured main effects without confounding them with two-factor interactions. Third, the afterflow constant $\tau_\mathrm{af}$ was refitted from the 26 stop events recorded so far (a robust median-ratio estimate; $\tau_\mathrm{af} = 0.834$~s), and the centre point and hand-tuned settings were repeated with the new value. Fourth, 14 doses were chosen sequentially by Bayesian optimization in Ax~0.4.3 \cite{AxPlatform} on BoTorch \cite{Balandat2020Botorch}: a Gaussian-process model with a sparse axis-aligned subspace prior (SAASBO) \cite{Eriksson2021High}, suited to many settings and few observations, and the noisy expected hypervolume improvement acquisition function (qNEHVI) \cite{Daulton2021Parallel}, which accounts for the noise in each observed dose. Screening and centre-point doses were attached to the experiment as existing data so that the model used all 28 earlier doses. Each dose was run unattended over a secure shell connection to the rig, with the cup emptied at fixed intervals; a restart never repeated a dose, because each dose carries a unique identifier and its stored result is returned instead. The campaign and the validation blocks were launched and monitored by an AI coding agent (Claude Code, Anthropic) acting as the operator, which also wrote much of the firmware, campaign, analysis and simulation code under review by the authors; every prompt and response is public in the repository \cite{PowderDoserRepo}.

Because the observed front is built from single doses, a point can lie on it by chance. We therefore recommend settings from the model's Pareto set (posterior means) rather than from the observed front, and validate them with a block of eight repeated doses on a later day. The validation block also tested the screening corner that lay on the observed front (\texttt{corner-09}).

\subsection{Multi-task calibration across powders}
\label{sec:mtbo}

Each powder is a task, and all tasks share the eight controller settings and the dose record. A multi-task Gaussian process \cite{Bonilla2008Multi,Swersky2013Multi} models the objectives of task $k$ at settings $\mathbf{x}$ with a product kernel $K\big((\mathbf{x},k),(\mathbf{x}',k')\big) = B_{kk'}\,k_x(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')$, where the positive semi-definite matrix $B$ is learned from the data and its normalized entries are the correlations between powders. Two variants will be compared: an index kernel, which learns $B$ freely, and a descriptor kernel, in which $B$ is computed from each powder's dosing fingerprint (Section~\ref{sec:protocols}) and, where available, its measured properties, so that a new powder can borrow from similar ones before its first optimized dose \cite{Taylor2023Accelerated}. The objectives are extended from two to four, following the formulation developed for this controller \cite{PowderDoserRepo}: (i) accuracy, the median absolute error; (ii) repeatability, the probability that a dose lands within tolerance, $P(|e| \le \delta)$, estimated from replicate blocks, together with a constraint on the probability of overshoot, which cannot be undone; (iii) time per dose; and (iv) the accessible dose range, the smallest target that the settings deliver within tolerance with probability at least 0.95. \ph{Final formulation (constraint thresholds, replicate count per evaluation, and dose targets) to be fixed before the multi-task campaigns.}

The benchmark has two parts. Retrospectively, each completed single-task campaign is held out in turn, a surrogate fitted to its data stands in for the rig, and multi-task optimization on the held-out powder, informed by the other powders, is compared with single-task SAASBO, quasi-random (Sobol) search, and simply carrying over the best settings of the most similar powder. Prospectively, one new powder, a freshly atomized batch, is calibrated with the multi-task model. The figure of merit is the number of doses until the recommended settings reach 90\% of the best hypervolume known for that powder. \ph{Planned campaigns: AlSi10Mg, 316L stainless steel, sieved Al~4047 and at least one elemental alloy precursor, each with about 42 doses and an 8-dose validation block.} Reactive elemental precursors will be dosed in an argon glove box; where the elemental form is too hazardous to handle as a fine powder, a master alloy will be used instead.

\subsection{Discrete element model of the auger}
\label{sec:dem}

A discrete element model of the auger outlet was built in LIGGGHTS-PUBLIC~3.8 \cite{Kloss2012Models,Cundall1979Discrete}. Grains interact through a Hertz normal contact with history-dependent tangential friction and an elastic--plastic spring--dashpot rolling resistance model \cite{Ai2011Assessment}, and cohesion is added as a simplified Johnson--Kendall--Roberts (SJKR) term, a pull-off force proportional to the cohesion energy density and the contact area \cite{Johnson1971Surface}. For salt, the grains follow a three-size distribution around a median diameter of 0.425~mm (number fractions 0.3/0.4/0.3 at 0.85, 1 and 1.15 times the median) with density 2165~kg\,m$^{-3}$, a literature value for the material class. Young's modulus is reduced to 0.2~MPa to allow a time step of $2.1\times10^{-5}$~s, which keeps mean overlaps below 0.4\% of a diameter; Poisson's ratio is 0.3, the coefficient of restitution 0.5, sliding friction 0.5 between grains and 0.4 against the wall, and rolling friction 0.3. None of these parameters was fitted to the dosing data. The geometry is meshed from the CAD model of the printed auger: the conical outlet with its 3~mm exit, the solid core whose tip leaves a 1.07~mm annular gap at the exit, and the lowest 1.5 turns of the flight, filled with about 29{,}000 grains and topped up from a feed zone. Tilt is applied by rotating gravity, rotation by turning the auger mesh, and the delivered mass is counted as grains leave the exit.

To infer effective contact parameters for a powder, the model will be run over a designed set of cohesion energy densities, sliding and rolling friction coefficients, with the particle-size distribution fixed at the measured one, at the tilts and speeds of the dosing fingerprint. A Gaussian-process emulator of these runs then replaces the simulator in a Bayesian calibration \cite{Kennedy2001Bayesian,Higdon2008Computer}, which returns a posterior distribution over the contact parameters given the measured fingerprint and its scatter, with a discrepancy term for systematic model error. Because dosing data alone may not separate cohesion from friction \cite{Coetzee2017Review,Richter2020Development,ElKassem2021Sensitivity}, the identifiability of each parameter will be checked on synthetic data before the inference is run on measured data, and grain counts for the finer metal powders will be reduced by coarse-graining that preserves the ratio of cohesive to gravitational forces \cite{Girnth2024Dimensionless}. \ph{Emulator design (number of runs, parameter ranges) to be fixed after the friction and cohesion sweeps in the rig geometry.}

\subsection{Independent powder characterization}
\label{sec:charac}

To test whether the dosing-derived descriptors and inferred parameters carry physical meaning, the same lots will be characterized by standard methods \cite{ASTMF3049}: particle-size distribution by laser diffraction \cite{ASTMB822} or sieving, bulk and tapped density and the Hausner ratio \cite{ASTMB527}, Hall-flow time \cite{ASTMB213} or, for powders that do not pass the Hall funnel, Carney-flow time \cite{ASTMB964}, angle of repose, and the flow function coefficient $ff_c$ from a shear cell \cite{ASTMD7891,Schulze2021Powders}. Spreadability will be assessed by blade-spreading single layers of 50--100~\si{\micro\metre} and measuring areal mass and coverage \cite{Snow2019Development,Cordova2020Measuring}. \ph{Shear-cell measurements are expected to be outsourced; sieves have been ordered.}

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
\label{sec:stats}

Delivery per revolution is reported as the mean $\pm$ standard error of single revolutions, and a value counts as a measurement only when the mean exceeds twice its standard error. Associations between dosing descriptors and powder properties are reported as Spearman rank correlations, because $n \le 12$ and many property values are class estimates; $p$-values are not corrected for the 42 predictor--response pairs examined and are treated as leads. The optimization models are specified completely by the pinned software versions and the campaign configuration stored with each campaign, and all trials, including aborted and jammed doses, are kept in the analysis. \ph{For the multi-task and inference results, report baselines, held-out performance, residuals and hyperparameters as required by the journal's guidelines for machine-learning studies.}

\subsection{Dosing response separates powders}
\label{sec:res-powders}

At fixed settings the auger delivered very different amounts of different powders (Fig.~\ref{fig:powders}a, Table~\ref{tab:powders}). Ten of the 13 powders delivered between 9.6~mg (sodium alginate) and 231~mg (AlSi10Mg) per revolution at 22.5$^\circ$, a 24-fold range, and three delivered no measurable amount: fumed silica, brown rice flour, and silicon finer than 44~\si{\micro\metre}. The two silicon grades are the clearest case. They are the same material, yet the 75--150~\si{\micro\metre} grade delivered 211~mg per revolution and the finer grade delivered nothing in 17 of 18 revolutions, so particle size alone switched the flow on and off.

Across all powders and tilts, powder identity accounted for 70\% of the variance of the logarithm of the mass per revolution, tilt for 20\%, their interaction for 4\%, and repeat-to-repeat scatter for 6\%. Tilting the auger from 0 to 22.5$^\circ$ raised delivery by a median factor of 4.2, and tilting further to 45$^\circ$ by only 1.2 more; carboxymethyl cellulose was the only powder that delivered less at 45$^\circ$ than at 22.5$^\circ$. Delivery rose more slowly than auger speed for most powders (median exponent $\alpha = 0.78$), but faster than speed for the three slowest (white rice flour 1.36, carboxymethyl cellulose 1.13, sodium alginate 1.08). Put another way, free-flowing powders delivered less per revolution as the auger sped up (salt by 33--47\%, AlSi10Mg by 49\% over a sixfold range of speed), whereas cohesive ones delivered more (white rice flour 94\% more, carboxymethyl cellulose 23\% more), so the sign of the speed response alone separates the two groups. A single tap freed between 0.24~mg (sodium alginate) and 20~mg (calcium lactate), and the afterflow time constant ranged from 0.78 to 1.17~s across six powders. Each of these numbers comes from data that any calibration of the doser collects anyway.

The same powders also differ in how well a fixed controller doses them (Fig.~\ref{fig:powders}b). With one set of settings tuned by hand on salt, seven powders landed within $\pm$5\% of a 1~g target on every dose (24 of 24), and the three alloy-relevant ones (AlSi10Mg, coarse silicon and sodium sulfate) within 1\%. The two slowest-flowing powders ran out of time: white rice flour delivered 0.84--0.89~g and sodium alginate 0.69--0.73~g. Even among powders that met the limit, the median time for 1~g ranged from 65~s for AlSi10Mg to 11~min for carboxymethyl cellulose. A controller tuned for one powder is therefore accurate on similar powders but slow or short on different ones, which is the case for calibrating each powder.

\subsection{Bayesian calibration on salt}
\label{sec:res-bo}

The salt campaign ran 42 doses of 0.5~g unattended in less than 2.5~h (Fig.~\ref{fig:salt}). The four doses at the hand-tuned settings took 180--343~s (median 245~s) and ended 3.6--4.8~mg short. The screening design showed which settings mattered and why. Tapping during the bulk stage cut the average time of successful doses from 186 to 79~s, but raised the number of overshoots from two to five of eight corners, because taps free extra powder after the auger stops. Raising the tap-stage tilt from 0 to 15$^\circ$ cut the average time from 223 to 92~s. Switching to the trickle stage earlier, with 0.30~g rather than 0.05~g to go, cut the mean absolute error from 34 to 11~mg and the number of overshoots from five to two. In total, 8 of the 42 doses overshot, by 21--83~mg, six of them during screening, and three ended in a jam when the tap budget ran out.

The optimizer then chose 14 doses. The settings it recommended (\texttt{bo-005}, Table~\ref{tab:space}) took 100~s with a 2.4~mg error in the campaign, close to the model's posterior mean of 102~s and 0.9~mg. Repeated eight times a week later, they dosed 0.5~g in a median 78~s (60--114~s) with a median error of 1.8~mg (all 0.8--2.6~mg under the target) and no overshoot (Fig.~\ref{fig:salt}b). That is three times faster than the hand-tuned settings with less than half their error. The fastest single dose of the campaign, the screening corner \texttt{corner-09} at 52~s and 0.7~mg, did not repeat: eight more doses at its settings took 47--385~s (median 202~s), depending on how much powder happened to fall after the bulk stage stopped (22--69~mg). The observed Pareto front therefore overstated what that setting could do, and a recommendation based on the model's averaged prediction held up where the single best dose did not.

Most of the time saved came from the tap stage (Fig.~\ref{fig:salt}c). A hand-tuned dose spent most of its time tapping out the last tens of milligrams, freeing less than 1~mg per tap, whereas at the recommended settings each tap freed 1.5--3.6~mg, because the tube rose from a 10$^\circ$ trickle tilt to a 15$^\circ$ tap tilt instead of dropping to it. The optimizer changed seven of the eight settings, and all six numeric settings ended at an edge of their search range (Fig.~\ref{fig:salt}d). Some of these edges are hardware limits, such as the auger speed near its maximum, and others are choices, such as the 3~mg stopping band; the rest suggest that the next campaign should search a wider range.

The salt settings also transferred to a different powder. Applied unchanged to AlSi10Mg, with the afterflow constant fitted on salt, they delivered 7.993~g for an 8~g request ($-$0.09\%) in 315~s. An unsieved Al~4047 batch from our atomizer, in contrast, clogged the auger on coarse agglomerates. Free-flowing powders thus appear to share much of their calibration, while powders that flow differently need their own, which is what a multi-task model is designed to exploit.

\subsection{A discrete element twin of the auger}
\label{sec:res-dem}

Without any fitted parameter, the discrete element model reproduced the delivery of salt at the campaign's centre-point settings: 105~mg per revolution at 27.5$^\circ$ and 60~rpm averaged over the run (116~$\pm$~20~mg after the first quarter revolution), against 105~$\pm$~11~mg measured over eight doses (Fig.~\ref{fig:dem}a). It also reproduced the rise of delivery with tilt, but less closely at the other settings: 33 against about 28~mg per revolution at 0$^\circ$, 153 against about 195~mg at 45$^\circ$, and 71 against 106--110~mg at 22.5$^\circ$ and 90~rpm. The measured values at 0$^\circ$ and 45$^\circ$ were taken at 30~rpm and scaled to 60~rpm with the measured speed exponent.

The model is most sensitive to the geometry of the exit and to the grains. The tip of the auger core sits inside the exit and leaves a 1.07~mm annular gap, about 2.5 grain diameters, which throttles the flow: moving the tip back by 0.5, 1 and 2~mm raised delivery from 105 to 112, 224 and 427~mg per revolution. Reducing the median grain size from 0.425 to 0.35~mm raised it by 29\%. In a smaller 10~mm auger, adding cohesion (20~kJ\,m$^{-3}$) cut delivery from 46.8 to 0.2~mg per revolution, the same switch from flow to no flow seen between the two silicon grades. The model's cost sets what can be simulated: the outlet section with 29{,}000 grains takes 26--30~min per revolution on one processor core, a full salt-filled auger with 1.1~million grains about 19~h, and a fine metal powder would need about 10$^9$ grains per auger, so those powders require coarse-graining \cite{Girnth2024Dimensionless}.

\ph{Inference results.} The emulator built from \ph{N} simulations reproduced held-out simulations within \ph{X\%}. Delivery depended most strongly on cohesion at low tilt and on friction at high tilt (Fig.~\ref{fig:dem}b), so the tilt sweep of the dosing fingerprint constrains both. For salt the posterior gave an effective cohesion energy density of \ph{X~kJ\,m$^{-3}$} and sliding friction of \ph{Y} (Fig.~\ref{fig:dem}c); for \ph{the other powders} the values are listed in Table~\ph{S1}.

\subsection{Dosing response and powder properties}
\label{sec:res-props}

Even with literature values in place of measured properties, the dosing response ranked powders the way standard flow measures do (Fig.~\ref{fig:props}a). Across 12 powders, delivery at 45$^\circ$ fell as the angle of repose rose (Spearman $\rho = -0.84$, $p = 0.001$), and the same held at 22.5$^\circ$ ($\rho = -0.77$, $n = 11$). The five powders with a measured or manufacturer angle of repose ranked correctly on their own: those at 30--32$^\circ$ delivered 244--339~mg per revolution and those at 41--45$^\circ$ delivered 9--37~mg. The equilibrium moisture content predicted the error of 1~g doses under the hand-tuned controller ($\rho = +0.87$, $n = 9$): every powder dosed within 5~mg held less than 0.1~wt\% water, and every hygroscopic biopolymer missed by 33--292~mg. Literature median particle size did not predict delivery across materials ($\rho = +0.26$), although it decided flow within one material, as the silicon grades show. The shape of the tilt response also differed between powders (Fig.~\ref{fig:props}b), which is the information the discrete element model needs to separate cohesion from friction. The interruption fraction, computed from the same balance records, ranged from 0.13 for AlSi10Mg and 0.26 for salt to 0.81 for carboxymethyl cellulose and 0.96 for sodium alginate, and it also followed the angle of repose ($\rho = +0.76$, $n = 10$) while only partly overlapping the volumetric flow index ($\rho = -0.66$ between the two), so the two descriptors are not redundant.

\ph{Comparison with measured properties.} On the same lots, the effective cohesion inferred from dosing correlated with the shear-cell flow function coefficient ($\rho = $~\ph{X}, Fig.~\ref{fig:props}c) and with the Hall-flow time of the powders that passed the funnel (\ph{Y}); \ph{Z} powders did not pass the Hall funnel, but all of them produced a dosing fingerprint. Against blade-spread single layers, \ph{the interruption fraction ranked the batches like layer coverage}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Calibration data as a flow measurement} The doser measures a powder under conditions close to those of its use: a small sample, moving through a narrow gap under gravity, shear and vibration. Every quantity in the dosing fingerprint is collected during routine calibration, from 4--10~g of powder and in less than an hour, and the same measurement works inside a glove box, where Hall funnels and shear cells are awkward to use. The fingerprint has two further advantages over funnel tests. It does not fail on cohesive powders: a powder that will not pass a Hall funnel still produces a tilt response, a tap quantum and an afterflow constant, and complete absence of flow is itself a result \cite{Spierings2016Powder,Marchetti2021Powder}. And it has several dimensions, so it can separate effects that a single flow time combines. The correlation with the angle of repose and the failure of particle size to predict delivery across materials (Section~\ref{sec:res-props}) agree with studies of pharmaceutical screw feeders, in which bulk density and cohesion-related properties, rather than size, predicted feed rate \cite{Wang2017Predicting,Tahir2020Development,Bekaert2021Determination,Bostijn2019Twin}. The literature values used here are class estimates, however, not measurements of our lots, and a correlation of ranks across 12 very different materials shows only that the doser orders powders sensibly. Whether it can resolve the smaller differences between batches of one alloy, which matter most for feedstock quality control in AM, remains to be shown \ph{(Section~\ref{sec:res-props})}.

\paragraph{What Bayesian optimization contributes} On salt, the optimizer found settings three times faster than hand tuning with less error, in one unattended afternoon. Two lessons from the campaign shape the multi-powder design. First, repeatability has to be an objective, not an afterthought. The fastest dose on the observed front came from settings whose time varied eightfold between repeats, because the powder that falls after the auger stops varies from dose to dose. A noise-aware acquisition function \cite{Daulton2021Parallel}, a recommendation based on the model's averaged prediction, and a validation block together guarded against this, but an explicit repeatability objective, estimated from replicate doses, would let the optimizer trade it against speed directly. Second, the recommended settings sat at the edges of the search space, so the ranges were set too conservatively, which is easy to fix but should be expected for every new powder. The screening design was worth its 20 doses: it explained, in physical terms, why taps during the bulk stage speed doses up but cause overshoots, and it gave the model a balanced starting set. Because overshoot is irreversible, the current formulation's symmetric absolute error should become a one-sided penalty with a constraint on the probability of overshoot, as the formulation for the four-objective campaigns does (Section~\ref{sec:mtbo}).

\paragraph{Sharing calibration across powders} That the salt settings dosed AlSi10Mg to within 0.1\% suggests that powders which flow freely share most of their calibration, and that a multi-task model can start a new free-flowing powder close to its optimum. Cohesive and hygroscopic powders, which deliver an order of magnitude less per revolution and respond differently to tilt and speed, are less likely to benefit from the salt data, and a model that learns task correlations decides this from data rather than by assumption. Using the dosing fingerprint to compute the task correlations would also let the model place a new powder before its first optimized dose \cite{Taylor2023Accelerated}. \ph{Results of the multi-task benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:res-mtbo}) to be discussed here.}

\paragraph{From dosing data to contact parameters} The discrete element model reproduced salt delivery at one setting without calibration and followed its trend with tilt, which suggests that its geometry and contact laws capture the main mechanism. Its errors at other settings, and its strong sensitivity to the exit gap and the grain size, mean that inference must start from a measured exit geometry and a measured particle-size distribution, or those uncertainties will be absorbed into the inferred cohesion and friction \cite{Coetzee2017Review,ElKassem2021Sensitivity}. The inferred parameters will also be effective rather than intrinsic: with a softened Young's modulus, the cohesive force in the SJKR model depends on the contact area and hence on the chosen stiffness, so inferred cohesion energies can be compared across powders only at a fixed numerical stiffness \cite{Thakur2014Micromechanical}. Triboelectric charging, which visibly affected the fumed silica and carboxymethyl cellulose, is not represented in the model \cite{Galindo2024Charging}. If these caveats are respected, the same contact parameters can drive DEM models of powder spreading, which are the standard tool for predicting layer quality in powder bed fusion \cite{Haeri2017Optimisation,Nan2018Jamming,Meier2019Critical,Shaheen2021Influence}.

\paragraph{Limits of the present data} All doses so far were made in air, on one auger geometry, and the optimization covers one powder at one target mass. Barium chloride caked after humidity cycling, and the correlation of dose error with moisture content shows that ambient humidity matters for hygroscopic powders. The tap stage sets the cost of accuracy. All 16 validation doses landed inside the 3~mg band, but taps took most of each dose's time, and the mass a tap frees falls as the lip of the tube empties (on salt, from about 2.8~mg for the first tap after the auger moved to below 0.5~mg after 40 taps). Tighter tolerances or faster doses therefore need a smaller and more consistent final increment, for example a smaller outlet or a refill of the lip between taps, more than a different control law \cite{PowderDoserRepo}. Finally, every powder was supplied commercially or made in our laboratory without a recorded lot analysis, which is why measured properties of each lot are part of the work that remains.

\paragraph{Implications for additive manufacturing} In an alloy-discovery workflow the doser is calibrated on every powder anyway, so the measurements described here come at no extra cost in powder or time. Stored with the dose records, the fingerprint, the validated settings and the inferred contact parameters for each lot form a feedstock record that can flag a batch that flows unlike its predecessors, as the clogged Al~4047 batch would have been flagged before it reached a printer. \ph{Whether that record predicts spreading and packing in the powder bed will be tested on blade-spread layers (Section~\ref{sec:charac}).}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We treated the calibration of an open-source auger doser as multi-objective Bayesian optimization and asked what the calibration data say about the powder. On salt, an unattended campaign of 42 doses found settings that dosed 0.5~g in a median 78~s with a median error of 1.8~mg over eight repeats, three times faster than hand-tuned settings, and those settings dosed 8~g of AlSi10Mg to within 0.1\% without change. At fixed settings, 13 powders spanned a 24-fold range in delivery per revolution, three did not flow at all, and the delivery ranked powders like their angle of repose. A discrete element model of the auger, with no fitted parameters, reproduced the measured delivery of salt at one setting and its trend with tilt. \ph{Multi-task calibration across N powders reduced the doses needed for a new powder by X\%, and effective cohesion and friction inferred from dosing data tracked shear-cell and Hall-flow measurements.} Because calibration is a step every dosing workflow already performs, it can double as a characterization of each feedstock, at no extra cost in powder or time.
