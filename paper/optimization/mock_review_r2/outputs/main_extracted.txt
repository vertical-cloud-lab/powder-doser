Abstract

Alloy development for powder bed fusion and directed energy deposition
begins by weighing many feedstock powders, and an automated doser must
be recalibrated for each powder. This work treats calibration of an open-
source, 3D-printed auger doser as multi-objective Bayesian optimization of
eight controller settings, minimizing dose time and error, and asks what
the calibration records reveal about the powder. On sodium chloride, an
unattended campaign of 42 doses found settings that, repeated eight times a
week later, dosed 0.5 g in a median of 78 s (95% confidence interval 69–110 s)
with a median error of 1.8 mg, against 245 s and 4.6 mg for hand-tuned
settings (exact Mann–Whitney p = 0.004 for both). The best single screening
dose did not repeat. A second campaign, which also searched the controller’s
hand-over margin, found no effect of it and was halted when the salt feed
fell fivefold within one session. Unchanged, the salt settings dosed 8 g of gas-
atomized AlSi10Mg within 7.2 mg. At the same settings an unsieved Al 4047
powder from the laboratory’s atomizer fed 13 times more slowly and stalled
until oversized particles were removed, so the dose record flagged a feedstock
problem. Across 12 powders, delivery per auger revolution ranked like the
literature angle of repose (Spearman ρ = −0.84).  [Multi-task calibration
across metal powders, and contact parameters inferred with a discrete element

 1  1. Introduction

 2     Alloy development by additive manufacturing (AM) starts with weighing
 3  powders. Each candidate composition is made by blending elemental or
 4  pre-alloyed powders, either in the bed of a laser powder bed fusion (PBF-LB)
 5  machine or in the feeders of a directed energy deposition (DED) system
 6  [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6], or as a charge that is melted and atomized into a new powder.
 7  In a self-driving laboratory (SDL), where a planning algorithm chooses each
 8  next composition and robots make and test it [7, 8, 9], that weighing step
 9  must be automatic, accurate and fast for every powder the workflow uses
10  [10, 11, 12, 13]. The same powders must later spread into thin, uniform layers,
11  and how well they do so depends on the same properties that make them easy
12  or hard to dose: particle size and shape, cohesion and friction [14, 15, 16, 17].
13     Measuring those properties is slow and often uninformative. Hall and
14  Carney funnels report a single flow time and cannot test strongly cohesive
15  powders, which do not flow through them at all [18, 19]. Shear cells and
16  powder rheometers give richer data, but each test needs tens of millilitres of
17  powder, a dedicated instrument and a trained operator [20, 21]. Rotating
18  drums and spreadability tests come closer to the conditions inside an AM
19  machine [22, 23, 24, 25], yet different flow tests often rank the same powders
20  differently, and none of them predicts spreading reliably on its own [18, 19, 24].
21  For an SDL that cycles through dozens of feedstocks, including small batches
22  of reactive or expensive powders, a separate characterization campaign for
23  each powder is rarely affordable.
24    Powder feeders, by contrast, are characterized all the time, because each
25 new powder must be calibrated before it can be dosed. In pharmaceutical
26  manufacturing, the feed factor of a screw feeder, the mass it delivers per screw
27  revolution, has been predicted from bulk density, compressibility and shear-
28  cell data [26, 27, 28], and loss-in-weight feeders have standard methods for
29  measuring how their output varies [29, 30, 31]. If material properties predict

30  feeder behaviour, the reverse should also hold: a feeder that is driven through
31  a designed set of conditions, and weighed, measures the powder. Small-scale
32  dosers make this attractive because the measurement uses milligrams to
33  grams of powder and happens anyway, every time a new powder is loaded
34  [32, 33, 34, 35, 36].
35     Calibration of this kind is an optimization problem with several competing
36  goals. A dose should be accurate and repeatable, it should be fast, and the
37  settings should work across the range of doses the workflow needs. Overshoot
38  cannot be undone, because powder cannot be taken back out of the cup, so
39  an error in one direction costs more than in the other. Bayesian optimization
40 (BO) suits such problems. It fits a probabilistic model to the doses made so far
41  and chooses the next settings with an acquisition function, a rule that scores
42  candidate settings by their expected gain [37, 38, 39]; it has already tuned
43 AM processes autonomously [40].  Multi-objective BO finds the trade-off
44  between goals such as time and error, the Pareto front [41], and priors that
45  favour a few influential settings make it practical with many settings and
46  few experiments [42]. Each new powder, however, still starts from scratch.
47  Multi-task BO treats related problems, here different powders on the same
48  doser, as tasks of one model, so that data from earlier powders inform the
49   first doses of a new one [43, 44, 45]. How strongly two powders are related is
50  then a quantity the model learns, and it is natural to ask whether it reflects
51  their physical properties.
52     Physics-based models can answer that question directly. The discrete
53  element method (DEM) simulates every grain and every contact between
54  grains [46, 47].  It has been used to predict the output of screw conveyors
55  [48], and it represents cohesion and rolling friction with contact laws whose
56  parameters are hard to measure directly [49, 50, 51].  Those parameters
57  are usually calibrated by matching simulations to bulk tests, which is itself
58  an inverse problem [52, 53, 54, 55] and is best treated with the statistical
59  methods developed for calibrating computer models [56, 57].  Dosing of
60  cohesive powders by a screw feeder in a simplified geometry has been simulated
61  with DEM, with interparticle friction and cohesion calibrated against the
62  dosed mass [58], and the closest precedent calibrated DEM parameters of
63  pharmaceutical powders against several bulk responses extracted from auger
64  dosing experiments [59, 60], so inferring contact parameters from a doser is
65  not itself new. What is new here is a doser that optimizes its own settings
66  for each powder and whose records are then reused as a flow measurement
67  of AM feedstock. DEM is also the standard tool for studying how powders

 85     Sections 2.1 to 2.3 describe the doser, the powders and the dose records.
 86  Sections 2.4 to 2.6 describe the two calibration campaigns on salt and the
 87  doses of metal feedstock. Sections 2.7 to 2.11 describe the fixed-setting data

 91  2.1. Doser and dose procedure
 92    The doser is one channel of the open-source design in [64, 65]. A one-piece
 93  auger, printed in PLA on a Bambu Lab H2D printer with 0.2 mm layers,
 94  serves as both reservoir and screw.  Its tube, 21 mm in bore and 250 mm
 95  long, holds [58–70 mL; to be measured] of powder, and a single helical flight
 96  (0.5 mm thick, 10.4 mm pitch, 8 turns) runs over the last 84 mm before a
 97  12 mm conical outlet with a 3 mm exit hole. [The tube used for each dataset
 98   is not recorded; record it and measure its as-printed exit hole and core-tip gap.]
 99 A NEMA-11 stepper motor (11HS18-0674S, Pololu Tic T500 driver) turns
100  the auger through a 20:44 gear pair at up to 109 rpm. Two servos tilt the
101  auger about a hinge near the outlet through a 2:1 gear, and a push–pull
102  solenoid (JF-0530B, 60 ms pulses) taps the tube to free powder held at the


                                    4

## Page 7

103  lip of the outlet. Powder falls through a funnel into a cup on an analytical
104  balance (A&D HR-100A; 0.1 mg readability, 102 g capacity) that reports
105  each reading over a serial (RS-232) link. A Raspberry Pi Pico W runs the
106  dosing firmware (MicroPython; firmware versions are recorded with every
107  dose), and a Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W next to the rig runs each dose, records it
108  and uploads the record to a database. All tilts below are angles of the auger
109  axis above horizontal, and all speeds and revolutions are those of the auger.
110   A dose has three stages. In the bulk stage the auger turns continuously
111  at a set tilt and speed, optionally while the solenoid taps at 2 Hz, until the
112  mass still to go falls to a switch threshold plus a fixed 50 mg allowance for
113  powder that is still falling. In the trickle stage the auger runs slowly (typically
114  2–15 rpm) under a proportional–integral controller on the flow rate, which a
115 Kalman filter estimates from the noisy balance readings. The trickle stops
116 when the predicted final mass comes within a hand-over margin of the target
117  (35 mg unless stated); the prediction is the current mass estimate, plus the
118  afterflow still expected (the flow rate times an afterflow time constant τaf),
119  plus one standard deviation of the estimate. In the tap stage the auger stops,
120  the tube moves to a third tilt, and single taps free a few milligrams at a time
121  until the reading lies within a stopping band of ±δ around the target; when
122  taps stop freeing powder, the controller turns the auger by a small angle (a
123  5◦“nudge”) to re-feed the lip. The dose is scored on a balance reading taken
124  2 s after the last action.
125    Two rules end a dose early: a dose that exceeds the target by more than
126  100 mg is aborted, and a dose that uses up its allowance of tap cycles (120) or
127  nudges (20), or makes no progress for 8 s, ends as a jam. The Kalman filter
128  models the balance as a first-order lag, a reading that approaches the true
129  mass with a single time constant. The controller used the datasheet value
130  of 0.7 s for that constant, whereas drop tests on the rig gave 0.16 s. The
131  optimized settings absorb this mismatch, but it should be removed before
132  settings are compared across powders.

161  2.4. Calibration campaign 1: eight settings on salt
162     For one powder and one target mass (0.5 g), calibration searches eight
163  controller settings (Table 2) to minimize two objectives at once: the total time
164  of a dose, t, and its absolute error, |e|, both measured on the scored balance
165  reading. The campaign fixed no tolerance in advance: the stopping band δ is
166  itself one of the searched settings, and the error objective measures where the
167  dose actually ended. Doses slower than 180 s or more than 20 mg off count
168  as failures. These two limits form the reference point for the hypervolume,
169  the area of objective space between that reference point and the best doses
170  so far, which is the usual single-number summary of a Pareto front. All other
171  controller parameters, including the controller gains and the 100 mg abort
172  limit, were frozen at the values of the hand-tuned controller.
173    The campaign ran in three phases, with four doses at the hand-tuned
174  settings spread through it as a baseline.  In the first phase, a two-level


                                    7

## Page 10

175  fractional factorial screening design of resolution IV (28−4, 16 runs) estimated
176  the main effect of each setting without confusing it with any two-factor
177  interaction; each run sits at a corner of the search space. Four centre-point
178  doses, with every setting at the middle of its range, measured the dose-to-
179  dose scatter, and two doses ran at the hand-tuned settings. In the second
180  phase, the afterflow constant was refitted from the 26 stop events recorded so
181  far (τaf = 0.834 s, the median ratio of afterflow mass to flow rate; a linear
182  dependence on flow rate was tested and was not significant, t = 1.1), and
183  the centre point (four doses) and the hand-tuned settings (two doses) were
184  repeated with the new value. In the third phase, 14 doses were chosen one at
185  a time in Ax 0.4.3 [66] on BoTorch [67]: two quasi-random (Sobol) points,
186  then 12 chosen by the model. The hand-tuned baseline therefore consists of
187  four single doses, two at the initial τaf = 0.30 s and two at the fitted value.
188    The model was a fully Bayesian Gaussian process (GP) for each objective
189  with a sparse axis-aligned subspace prior (SAASBO) [42], which assumes that
190  only a few of the eight settings matter strongly and so suits a search with many
191  settings and few observations. The acquisition function was the logarithmic
192  form of the noisy expected hypervolume improvement (qLogNEHVI) [41, 68],
193  which allows for the noise in each observed dose. Inputs were scaled to the
194  unit cube and each objective was standardized. The screening and centre-
195  point doses were given to the model as existing data, so it used all 28 earlier
196  doses. Doses that jammed were reported to the model at the failure limits
197  (180 s, 20 mg) or at their measured values if worse, and overshoots at their
198  measured values. Each suggestion took 1–1.5 min to compute. A preset
199  deadline stopped the optimization phase after 14 doses, short of the 30–40
200  planned.
201    Each dose ran unattended over a remote connection to the rig. The cup was
202  not emptied during the campaign: it held 20.6 g at the end, against a 45 g limit
203  set for the session. A restart never repeated a dose, because each dose carries
204  a unique identifier and its stored result is returned instead. An AI coding
205  agent (Claude Code, Anthropic) launched and monitored the campaigns and
206  the validation blocks, and wrote much of the firmware, campaign, analysis and
207  simulation code, which the authors reviewed. The agent started the campaign
208  script, watched it and restarted it after interruptions, but the settings of every
209  dose came from the screening design or the optimizer, not from the agent.
210  Every prompt and response is public in the repository [65].
211     Because the observed Pareto front is built from single doses, a point can
212   lie on it by chance. The recommended settings were therefore taken from the

213  model’s own Pareto set, computed from its posterior-mean predictions rather

221  2.5. Calibration campaign 2: nine settings, including the hand-over margin
222    The tap stage took most of each dose’s time in campaign 1, and the trickle
223  handed over to the taps a median 54 mg short of the target. Campaign 2
224  therefore added the hand-over margin of the trickle (0–35 mg) as a ninth
225  setting and scored overshoots like jams, at the failure limits, because an
226  overshoot cannot be undone. It started from the 60 doses of campaign 1 as
227  existing data, at their 35 mg margin, and re-dosed the 16 screening corners at
228  a 0 mg margin on 13 October, so that each corner gives a paired comparison of
229  the two margins. Two check doses at the recommended settings of campaign 1
230  followed, and then the optimizer (SAASBO with qLogNEHVI, as above, with
231  one Sobol point). The campaign ran unattended within limits on the cup load
232  (40 g) and on the number of consecutive stalls or jams (four), and resumed
233  on 15 October. Three doses on 13 October and three on 15 October ended
234  with a firmware fault: the dose itself finished, but the microcontroller ran out
235  of contiguous memory while formatting the result. The fault was fixed during
236  the session of 15 October (firmware trickle_tap/2026-10-15) and did not
237  recur. Doses with an equipment error were not given to the model.

253  2.7. Fixed-setting records used for the property correlations
254    The companion study ran every powder through the same test protocols
255  with frozen settings [64]. Three of them are used here. Protocol C turned
256  the auger through six single revolutions at 30 rpm at each of three tilts (0,
257  22.5 and 45◦), with a settled balance reading after each revolution, and gives
258  the delivery per revolution. Protocol D turned it continuously at 15, 45 and
259  90 rpm at 22.5◦; only its 15 rpm record is used here, for the interruption
260  fraction below. Protocol G made three closed-loop doses of 1 g with an earlier
261  three-stage controller whose settings were tuned by hand on salt and frozen for
262  every powder, and gives a dose error per powder. Delivery at 45◦and the mean
263  1 g dose error were correlated with literature values of the angle of repose,
264  moisture content, particle size and Hausner ratio. Two further descriptors
265  were computed from the same records and are proposed for screening atomized
266  batches [65]: a gravity-assisted volumetric flow index (delivery per revolution
267  at 45◦divided by bulk density, normalized to a reference AlSi10Mg powder),
268  and an interruption fraction (the share of balance increments at 15 rpm that
269  show no flow), a doser analogue of the cohesive index measured in a rotating
270 drum [69, 22].

271  2.8. Multi-task calibration across powders (planned)
272    Each powder is a task, and all tasks share the eight controller settings
273  and the dose record. A multi-task GP [43, 44] models the objectives of task
                                                                             (︂                       )︂274  k at settings x with a product kernel K (x, k), (x′, k′) = Bkk′ kx(x, x′), in
275  which the positive semi-definite matrix B is learned from the data and its
276  normalized entries are the correlations between powders. Two variants will be
277  compared. An index kernel learns B freely from the dose data. A descriptor
278  kernel computes B from each powder’s fixed-setting fingerprint and, where
279  available, its measured properties, so that a new powder can borrow from
280  similar ones before its first optimized dose [45]. The model will also take
281  covariates of the powder’s state, at least the mass dispensed since the tube
282  was filled and the relative humidity, because campaign 2 showed that the
283  response drifts with them (Section 3.2).

292  single-task campaign is held out in turn, and a statistical model fitted to

300  a freshly atomized batch, is calibrated with the multi-task model. The figure

307  2.9. Discrete element model of the auger
308   A discrete element model of the auger outlet was built in LIGGGHTS-
309 PUBLIC 3.8 [70, 46]. Grains interact through a Hertz normal contact with
310  history-dependent tangential friction and an elastic–plastic spring–dashpot
311  rolling resistance model [51]. Cohesion is added as a simplified Johnson–
312  Kendall–Roberts (SJKR) term, a pull-off force proportional to the cohesion
313  energy density and the contact area [49]. For salt, the grains follow a three-
314  size distribution around a median diameter of 0.425 mm (number fractions
315  0.3/0.4/0.3 at 0.85, 1 and 1.15 times the median) with a density of 2165 kg m−3,
316  a literature value for the material class.  Young’s modulus is reduced to
317  0.2 MPa to allow a time step of 2.1 × 10−5 s, which keeps mean overlaps
318  below 0.4% of a diameter; Poisson’s ratio is 0.3, the coefficient of restitution
319  0.5, sliding friction 0.5 between grains and 0.4 against the wall, and rolling
320  friction 0.3. None of these values was fitted to the dosing data. The geometry


                                   11

## Page 14

321  is meshed from the CAD model of the printed auger: the conical outlet
322  with its 3 mm exit, the solid core whose tip leaves a 1.07 mm annular gap
323  at the exit, and the lowest 1.5 turns of the flight, filled with about 29,000
324  grains and topped up from a feed zone. Tilt is applied by rotating gravity,
325  rotation by turning the auger mesh, and the delivered mass is counted as
326  grains leave the exit. Each case was run for 0.3–3 revolutions, mostly from
327  a single random packing. Because the SJKR pull-off force scales with the
328  contact area, which depends on the softened stiffness, any cohesion energy
329  density inferred with this model is specific to the simulator and its numerical
330  stiffness, and comparable across powders only at that fixed stiffness. Adhesion
331  between particles and the printed PLA wall is a separate parameter that
332  dosing data alone may not resolve.
333    To infer effective contact parameters for a powder, the model will be
334  run over a designed set of cohesion energy densities and sliding and rolling
335  friction coefficients, with the particle-size distribution fixed at the measured
336  one, at the tilts and speeds of the fixed-setting protocol. A GP emulator, a
337  fast statistical stand-in fitted to these runs, then replaces the simulator in a
338  Bayesian calibration [56, 57]. The calibration returns a posterior distribution
339  over the contact parameters, the range of values consistent with the measured
340  response and its scatter, with a discrepancy term for systematic model error.
341  Dosing data alone may not separate cohesion from friction [52, 53, 60], so
342  whether each parameter can be recovered on its own will first be checked on
343  synthetic data. For the finer metal powders, the grain count will be reduced
344  by coarse-graining, which simulates fewer, larger grains while preserving the
345  ratio of cohesive to gravitational forces [71]. [Emulator design (number of runs,
346  parameter ranges) to be fixed after the friction and cohesion sweeps in the rig
347  geometry.]

361  2.11. Statistical analysis
362     Samples are small (4–16 doses per group) and dose times have a long right
363  tail, so comparisons use exact or resampling methods. Medians are reported
364  with percentile bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (20,000 resamples). Two
365  groups are compared with the exact two-sided Mann–Whitney test, with the
366  Hodges–Lehmann shift and its exact confidence interval, and paired corners
367  with the exact Wilcoxon signed-rank test and, for counts of overshoots, the
368  exact McNemar test. A success fraction is bounded with one-sided 95%
369  Clopper–Pearson limits. Associations between dosing descriptors and powder
370  properties are Spearman rank correlations, because n ≤12 and many property
371  values are class estimates; their p-values are not corrected for the 42 predictor–
372  response pairs examined and are treated as leads rather than findings. The
373  campaign’s GP model was checked by leave-one-out cross-validation against
374  three simpler models (Section 3.1; the full mapping to the journal’s checklist
375  for machine-learning studies is in Table S8). All records, including aborted
376  and jammed doses, are kept in the analysis; doses with an equipment error
377  are reported but excluded from comparisons. The scripts that compute every
378  number in this paper from the dose records are in the repository [65].

470  3.2. What limits unattended calibration: campaign 2
471    Campaign 2 ran 43 doses on 13 and 15 October (Fig. 3; Table S4). Eight

475    The hand-over margin made no detectable difference. At a 0 mg margin,

604  4. Discussion

605 What the optimizer contributed. On salt, one unattended session of 2.3 h found
606  settings that were three times faster than hand tuning, with less than half its
607  error, and that held up in eight repeats. The optimizer’s 14 doses never beat
608  the best single screening doses, so its contribution was not a better single dose.
609  It was the recommendation: a model that averages over noisy doses picked
610  settings that repeated, whereas the best observed dose, corner-09, was on


                                   19

## Page 22

611  the front largely by chance and was no faster than hand tuning when repeated.
612 The 20 screening doses were well spent: they explained in physical terms why
613  taps in the bulk stage speed doses up but cause overshoots, and they gave the
614  model a balanced start. Two lessons follow for the next campaigns. First, time
615  per dose has a long tail of slow doses (the centre point took 24–391 s), which
616  a standardized GP on raw time handles poorly; a noise model that allows for
617  occasional slow doses, or a log transform of time, should be compared in the
618  retrospective benchmark. Second, the recommended settings sat at the edges
619  of the search space, so the ranges were set too conservatively.

620 The powder is not a fixed target. Campaign 2 is the more important result
621  for anyone who wants to run calibration unattended. Two weeks later, the
622  same settings fed 7% less in the bulk stage, the taps freed about a fifth as
623 much powder, and the recommended settings of campaign 1, validated eight
624  times out of eight a week earlier, ran out of tap cycles twice; two days after
625  that, the feed fell fivefold within one session. The order of the settings in
626  the bulk stage, however, carried over between sessions almost unchanged
627  (ρ = 0.90), whereas the endgame did not. What a model can share between
628  sessions, and between powders, is therefore likely to be the bulk response,
629  while the tap stage depends on the state of the lip and has to be learned anew
630  or modelled with covariates. A BO loop that treats every dose as a sample of
631  a fixed function of the settings will attribute such drift to the settings. Three
632  measures follow and are built into the plan for the multi-task campaigns:
633  record the state of the powder with every dose (mass dispensed since the
634  tube was filled, relative humidity, time since the last session) and give it to
635  the model as a covariate; start every session with a short reference dose at
636  known settings, and stop or re-validate when it drifts; and let the loop pause
637  on a suspected empty or caked tube instead of scoring the dose as a failure.
638 The firmware fault of campaign 2 is a smaller lesson of the same kind: an
639  unattended campaign needs equipment errors to be detected and kept out of
640  the model, as they were here.

641  Dosing records as a check of feedstock. For an alloy workflow, the Al 4047
642  doses are the clearest case of the doser measuring the powder. At settings that
643  delivered 144 mg/s of AlSi10Mg, the laboratory’s unsieved powder fed 11 mg/s
644  or less, decayed within and between doses, and recovered once large particles
645  were removed. Nothing beyond the dose record was needed to see this, and
646  the same record flagged it on every batch. Sieving each atomized batch before


                                   20

## Page 23

647  use, as is now planned, should remove the cause; a fixed-setting reference
648  dose of every new batch would then show whether it worked. The rankings of
649  Fig. 7 point the same way across materials: delivery ranked 12 powders like
650  their angle of repose, and moisture content, not particle size, tracked dose
651  error. These agree with studies of pharmaceutical screw feeders, in which
652  bulk density and cohesion-related properties, rather than size, predicted feed
653  rate [26, 27, 28, 78]. The literature values are class estimates, however, not
654  measurements of our lots, and a correlation of ranks across 12 very different
655  materials shows only that the doser orders powders sensibly. Whether it
656  can resolve the smaller differences between batches of one alloy (sieved and
657  unsieved, dry and humid, virgin and reused), which matter most for feedstock
658  quality control in AM, remains to be shown with measured properties of the
659  same lots. The fixed-setting record also has two advantages over funnel tests:
660   it still returns information on cohesive powders, recording a no-flow result
661  rather than a failed test [18, 79], and it has several dimensions, so it can
662  separate effects that a single flow time combines.

663 From dosing data to contact parameters. The DEM model reproduced salt
664  delivery at one setting without calibration and followed its trend with tilt,
665  which suggests that its geometry and contact laws capture the main mechanis-
666  m. Its errors at other settings, and its strong sensitivity to the exit gap and
667  the grain size, mean that inference must start from a measured exit geometry
668  and a measured particle-size distribution; otherwise those uncertainties will
669  be absorbed into the inferred cohesion and friction [52, 60]. The inferred
670  parameters will be effective rather than intrinsic, comparable across powders
671  only at a fixed numerical stiffness [50]. Static (triboelectric) charging, sus-
672  pected in noisy readings of carboxymethyl cellulose and seen as salt sticking
673  to the auger walls in a glove box, is not represented in the model [80]. If these
674  caveats are respected, the same contact parameters can drive DEM models of
675  powder spreading, the standard tool for predicting layer quality in PBF-LB
676  [61, 62, 15, 63].

677  Limits of the present data. The optimization covers one powder at one target
678  mass, with a hand-tuned baseline of four doses on the campaign day; transfer
679  to AlSi10Mg rests on one dose. All doses were made in air, and the state of
680  the powder in the tube was not controlled or recorded. The tap stage sets the
681  cost of accuracy: at the recommended settings every validation dose landed
682  inside the 3 mg band, but taps took most of each dose’s time, and the mass a


                                   21

## Page 24

683  tap frees falls as the lip of the tube empties and, as campaign 2 showed, from
684  one session to the next. Tighter tolerances or faster doses therefore need a
685  smaller and more consistent final increment, for example a smaller outlet or a
686   refill of the lip between taps, more than a different control law. Which printed
687  tube produced each dataset was not recorded, and its exit geometry, which
688  the DEM model shows to matter, was not measured. Finally, every powder
689  was supplied commercially or made in the laboratory without a recorded lot
690  analysis.

691  Implications for AM feedstock workflows. In an alloy-discovery workflow the
692  doser is calibrated on every powder anyway, so the records described here
693  come at little extra cost in powder or time. Stored with the dose records,
694  the fixed-setting response, the validated settings and, in time, the inferred
695  contact parameters of each lot form a feedstock record that can flag a batch
696  that flows unlike its predecessors, as the unsieved Al 4047 batch was flagged
697  before it reached an atomizer. [Whether that record predicts spreading and
698  packing in the powder bed will be tested on blade-spread layers (Section 2.10).]

699  5. Conclusions

700    The calibration of an open-source auger doser was treated as multi-
701  objective Bayesian optimization, and its records were examined as a mea-
702  surement of the powder. On salt, an unattended campaign of 42 doses found
703  settings that dosed 0.5 g in a median of 78 s with a median error of 1.8 mg
704  over eight repeats, about three times faster than hand-tuned settings, whereas
705  the best single screening dose did not repeat. A second campaign showed that
706  the powder’s response drifts between and within sessions enough to defeat an
707  optimizer that ignores it, so the state of the powder must be recorded and
708  modelled. The salt settings dosed 8 g of AlSi10Mg within 0.1% in one dose,
709  while an unsieved laboratory-atomized Al 4047 fed an order of magnitude
710  more slowly and stalled until oversized particles were removed, which the dose
711  record alone revealed. Across 12 powders, delivery ranked like the angle of
712  repose. A DEM model of the auger, with no parameters fitted to dosing data,
713  reproduced the measured delivery of salt at one setting and its trend with
714   tilt. [Multi-task calibration across N powders reduced the doses needed for a
715  new powder by X%, and effective cohesion and friction inferred from dosing data
716  tracked shear-cell and Hall-flow measurements.] Because every dosing workflow
717  already calibrates its doser, the records of that calibration can double as a
718  check of each feedstock at little extra cost.
